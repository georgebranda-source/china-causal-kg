### Is, Without Doubt, the Most-Talked-of Feature of Fall Fashions—It Is the Ensemble Costume At Its Best.

Some of us, at least, have not forgotten the "whimsicalities" of clever "Mr. Dooley," and the gems of wisdom that were made to follow the introduction "I see by the papers." While it is quite an true with fashion news as it is, with world news that one may not believe all that she reads, it is equally true that the wise woman may squarrel the whent from the chaff, and profit greatly by the harvest that she glans. For the truly wise woman is not above reading advertisements and comparing them with the logical analyses of the mode that are authoritative "presentations of what fashion has to offer, guiding her course by the conclusions that she renehes. And when Paris dereen and sponsors, fashion critics prophesy and the best known shops present a mode, even the most sceptical can be reasonably sure that she will not go wrong in her choice if it is that much featured fashion. Such a fashion is the suit, and while there are sure to be many modifications of the mode, there is one that is rapidly assuming the proportions of a "vogue."

There have been many ramifications of the suit made in the past few years and the amusing fashions of one century have given place to fashions that will, no doubt, be just as amusing a century hence, although it is certainly true that there are not the radical changes in the silhouette, from season to season, that there used to be. The fulled skirt, quite wide at the bottom; the short coat—sometimes with sleeves of the leg-o-mutton type; and the normal waistline would result in nothing but ridicule, were they to make their appearance in a season when the long, tight sleeve, the long coat and the slender line silhouette were fashionable, but they are history, and a mode to be really acceptable, must have an ancestry of some sort, these days. For history teaches and history simplifies.

There has always been something fascinating about the matching of two articles of apparel or of an accessory to a suit of frock, but it is only very recently that clever minds have hit upon the idea of making this "note of identity"—whether it be fabric, crimming or colour—the basis of the entire costume. And never before has this costume or "ensemble" idea been the fundamental of the entire scheme of fashion as it seems to be to-day. And it is known, logically enough, as the ensemble costume. When an idea has smartness and charm, and at the same time is practical from the point of view of economy, it is assured of popularity, and the ensemble idea has all those attributes, and convenience, as well, for it means the correlation of one costume with another as well as the correlation of part.

While there will be sports suits and strictly tailored suits for those who wish them, the featured suit will be rather more of a dress affair, and deserve its appellation of costumes. It will consist of a one-piece dress with matching coat or a short-worn with a long, tunic blouse-and the same matching coat—the latter, of course, of the same material as the skirt. It is quite possible that the lining of the coat may be of the same fabric as the tunic. Suit materials will be, for the most part, of the satin-finished variety, and they will be supple, and so quite adaptable to the whimsualities of the mode. Stress should be laid on the use of faible and bengaline silks, for this new fashion will have its effect on the suit mode.

The Dress Or The Tunic And Skirt. Disregarding for the moment the cont part of the outfit, one is given her choice between the straightline dress and the tunic blouse worn over a separate skirt. The latter is the more versatile fashion since it permits a choice of blouses, thus achieving as many costumes as one possesses tunics. But whether dress, or blouse and skirt, be the choice, fundamentals remain the same, and one is assured of the straight simplicity of line and the same details of neck and hemline. From the accompanying photographs it will be seen that the skirt length is abbreviated—Paris says that skirts are growing shorter—necklines both high and low; and the beltless silhouette the favourite.
When the mode prescribes certain things, it is not an 'easy matter to achieve a breadth of detail that assures variety, but that is exactly what the designers of the season's fashions have done. Really elaborate fabrics are one of the reasons for the artistic effects obtained, and, trimmings are the most versatile that they have ever been. Every news dispatch brings word that some designer has "launched" a new trimming, or an unusual way of applying an old and familiar bit of decoration, and the result is a most gratifying width of choice. If it be true that the short skirt has become a little shorter, then it is equally true that it has become a little wider, though great width is not desirable.

## [10] The Eyes of the House.

The housewife who really studies decorative values in relation to her own furnishments has no inclination to discount the value of her window trentments, appreciating the power they have to brighten or darken; increase or decrease space; and to add or detract from the beauty and hominess of her rooms. Rooms have been made, and rooms have been spoiled, by curtains and overdrawals, and the wise woman studies her windows, viewing them as a knotty problem that must be solved correctly; and only at the cost of much thought.

Not necessarily as great financial cost, for it is the colour, the weight of the fabric and the type of the curtains themselves that are important. There are sun-fast fabrics that are comparatively inexpensive, if one collates them with silks, lace and tapestries. It is the effect that counts, though one may not, of course, reply on the economy of the too cheap fabric. One of the chief faults in draping and curtailing windows is the too heavy material, and many women have found it advisable to keep to what might be termed "sunmery ways" of dressing the "eyes" of her house.

Curtains are to be had all ready for hanging in sheer Marquisettes and hand drawn serines—the latter far more dainty and desirable, as a rule, than curtains that are elaborately trimmed—free voiles and dainty Grenadines; and for a certain type of window—like the nursery—the hobbinets. Or the curtains may be made at home from yardage goods, at an expense relatively small. Daintiness and freshness are most desirable and an instance of this is the kitchen and breakfast nook that is dressed in checked gingham—always dainty always fresh always inexpensive.

For overdrapes there are the eretonnes and chintzes--shaped and draped to fit the individual windows and if one must be economical, the very charming artificial silk fabrics in a wide range of colours and weaves. Shall the sun be allowed to shine in and brighten a dark corner or must it be subdued in its insistent glare? The exposure of the room—the type of furniture—the size of the room and colour harmonies—all these are part of the problem.
