## [1] “DISTRESSING.”

**"NEED FOR CAUTION."**

The need for caution in dealing with the South China problem is stressed in a "Times" leader of August 21 which terms as "only too distressing" Hongkong's particular difficulties and the "effrontery of the Red Government of Canton was hardly to be borne." For that very reason great circumspection is necessary, states the article, as the Bolsheviks in Canton are obviously anxious to provoke some action by Great Britain which would turn the uncertain situation throughout China more definitely in their favour.

The river—that dreary river, on which so much has been done with British aid to suppress piracy and to make profitable navigation secure for all the nations—flows out from Canton, the chief commercial centre of South China, to Kowloon and to the island that not long ago was the very prosperous British colony of Hongkong, states the "Times." Hongkong and Canton are mutually complementary. Each depends in a large degree upon the other for its prosperity and even for its existence. Canton commands the trade of the interior, Hongkong is the highly organized medium for transforming that trade into an international asset, increasingly valuable alike to Chinese and to foreigners. One of the very serious features of the present phase of the Chinese crisis is the practical severance of normal communication between Canton and Hongkong.

## [2] BOLSHEVISTS IN CANTON.

Through a movement engineered from Canton, the trade of the British island colony at the mouth of the river is paralysed, and the daily losses now amount to several hundred thousand pounds. This movement is associated with the general anti-British movement in China, of which the Whit-suntide incident in Shanghai was made the pretext. But in Canton the opportunity was seized to give it a specific direction. Canton happens to be the birthplace and the home of the Kuomintang Party, which, in the declining years of its late leader, Sun Yat-sen, established a very intimate connection with the Russian Soviet Government. The extremist wing of this party, after strange vicissitudes during the chaotic civil war of the last few years, succeeded, also about Whit-suntide, in recovering control in Canton with Bolshevist aid. A confessedly Red Government is now established in Canton. It is supported by Soviet Russians who act as instructors in the Wham-poa Cadet School and as advisers in administrative and revolutionary technique. Steamers from Vladivostok have passed Hongkong conveying to Canton Bolshevik agents and Bolshevik supplies. The Canton merchants probably object to the present régime and would like to side with the more moderate but now apparently helpless wing of the Kuomintang. But the merchants can do little now, less even than when, in the days of the civil war, the Volunteer Corps financed by them was thrust aside. The Red faction, reinforced by Russian revolutionary and military aid, is in complete control of Canton and the Kwangtung region. The activities of this Government are now violently directed against Great Britain, the immediate object being the British colony of Hongkong at the mouth of the river.

## [3] THAT DECREE.

The general strike among the large population of Chinese workers in Hongkong and the irregular boycott of British goods have already done great harm to the colony. The Canton revolutionaries have something to show for their efforts. Now they have been emboldened to take a new step. They have published decrees forbidding the approach of British and Japanese ships to Canton and permitting other foreign ships to come only if they have avoided calling at Hongkong on their way. The penalty for infraction of the decree seems to be some activity of labour pickets and the prevention of loading or unloading. The decree is an open challenge. It is tantamount to defiant repudiation of treaties that have governed for decades the relations between the European Powers and America on the one hand and Southern China on the other. What can the Powers do about it, for if Great Britain and Japan are defied directly, all other Powers are indirectly condemned by the prohibition of access to any foreign ships that may dare to call at British Hongkong on their way up the river? This typically Bolshovist challenge must be confined warily. The Admiral Commanding the

China Station has gone up the river to investigate, and in the British concession on the island of Shameen, facing the city of Canton, we have a very gallant Consul-General, Sir James Jamieson, who knows his Cantonese through and through and is facing the extraordinary situation with great courage and unruffled humour. It would be the simplest thing in the world to retort by blockading the mouth of the river, admitting those who may be our friends and refusing access to declared enemies. But it must be remembered that the real object of our enemies in issuing these decrees may be to provoke Great Britain into taking exactly some such step as might be construed as a war measure. The Nationalist extremists in China, the Bolsheviks, and their friends throughout the world are using all their resources of propaganda to demonstrate the ridiculous thesis that Great Britain has aggressive, military or imperialistic designs on China. Our Australian Correspondent reported that even a broadcasting centre in Queensland has been used for this purpose. The situation in Hongkong is only too distressing. The effrontery of the Red Government of Canton is hardly to be borne. For that very reason great circumspection is necessary. While the Admiral investigates and while Sir James Jamieson remonstrates with Dr. Wu, the Canton Foreign Commissioner, son of the well-known Dr. Wu Ting-fang, it will be well to let ships of all nations go up the river and test in practice the actual efficacy of this impudent decree.

## [4] PROVOKING.

There is the more need to proceed cautiously since the Bolsheviks in Canton are obviously anxious to provoke some action by Great Britain that would turn the uncertain situation throughout China more definitely in their favour. Canton is practically independent, and its military forces are pushing out into Yunnan and Fukien. But the Yangtsze Governors seem to be combining to maintain order and to check the dangerous and destructive anti-foreign agitation. In the north, so far as Chang Tso-lin, the Lord of Manchuria, is disposed to exert his influence, the situation is comparatively favourable for the moment. But near Peking, Feng Yu-hsiang, the general of Christian professions, Bolshevik associations, and violently anti-British sentiments, is steadily strengthening his position, securing control of Shensi and Kiangsu, pushing out towards the Musulman West, and all the time vigilantly maintaining his communications, not alone with Karakhan and Borodin in Peking, but along the roads for the supply of munitions of war that lead down from Soviet Siberia through Kalgan. General Feng is in close sympathy with the Government now in power in Canton. The success of a provocative manoeuvre there might give him the opportunity, for which he is perhaps waiting, definitely to seize power in Peking. He dallies yet. In the meantime the wavering Peking Government, after having subsidized the Shanghai strike and given direct and indirect encouragement to the anti-foreign agitation, has found it possible to yield for once to an impulse of common sense. It has decided to invite the Powers to the Tariff Conference provided for in the Washington Treaty of 1922. The probability therefore is that the Conference will actually take place in October, and the Powers may at last have a real opportunity of concerting their policies in regard to China. It must be admitted that Great Britain and Japan stand somewhat alone in the intensity of their concern in the present dangerous situation, and it is something for us in these difficult days to have an old friend at our side. It must, however, be repeated again and again that while little that is effective can be done without the co-operation of all the Powers, a wise and vigorous British initiative is necessary above all. It is good news that Sir Ronald Mackay, our Minister in Peking, is so far recovered from his illness that he will shortly be able to return to his post. Mr. Austen Chamberlain came up to town specially to deal with the Chinese problem. In such a crisis as this we cannot afford for a moment to be off our guard.

## [5] “THE SEA HAWK.”

**BABATINI'S MASTERPIECE**

### 답변

A fleet of five large vessels of sixteenth century design was built for First National's screen version of Rafael Sabatini's "The Sea Hawk," which comes to the World Theatre to-morrow. The picture, which has been given a great reception in America, was shown in the Albert Hall, London, on October 15. In other sea stories on the screen, schooners or steam vessels have been rented or purchased. But in "The Sea Hawk," the ships, which ranged from 90 to 192 feet in length, were specially constructed in the shipyards of San Pedro, California, under the supervision of Frederick Gabouri, an expert. Mr. Gabouri was assisted by more than 300 draftsmen, mechanics, riggers, carpenters, electricians, sailors, and labourers. With poop decks, grotesque figureheads, gaudily coloured sails, and old-fashioned cannon, the fleet affoated recalled the Pacific expeditions of Balbón, Megellan, and Cabrillo, three hundred years ago. Many of the scenes in "The Sea Hawk," as those who have read Sabatini's book will appreciate, had to be produced on the high seas. All the vessels were made seaworthy and equipped with motor power. One of the ships was a replica of an English frigate of 1500; a second, a Moorish galleas, and a third, a Moorish frigate. There was also a Spanish galleon and pirate craft. The Moorish frigate was propelled by over 200 men, four to six chained to each oar, and the other vessels had oarsmen in proportionate numbers. In order to accommodate the sailors, galley slaves, fighting men, principals, and technical workers required for the big battles at sea, Frank Lloyd producer-director, who, by the way, is a native of Glasgow, established a camp near an Algerian village on the west coast of Santa Catalina Island. Three naval speed boats and two large barges were used to carry supplies to the camp, which at times numbered as many as 750 men. Rafael Sabatini has been called the modern Dumas. His "Sea Hawk," as everyone interested in literature knows, has met with great success as a novel. Briefly, the narrative tells of a seventeenth century English baron, whose brother, seeking to divert suspicion for a murder, has him placed aboard a ship and carried to sea in the hope that he will be accused of "the crime." The British ship is captured by the crew of a Spanish vessel, and all on board become galley slaves. Later, the Spanish vessel is attacked and taken by the Moors, and among the latter, Milton Sills, leading man in a score of big productions, becomes a great chieftain and is known as "The Sea Hawk." This account falls a long way short of justice to the story, which embodies great sweeps of action, too full of detail for recital in limited space. Incident after incident of dramatic significance follow one on the other. The picture represents First National's most ambitious undertaking, which is a big statement to make on the heels of the wonderful reception accorded "Abraham Lincoln" by London and provincial newspapers.

## [9] FRANCE'S DEBTS.

*(Reuter's American Service.)*

The joint session of the Franco-American debt funding commissioners only lasted twenty minutes, the Americans indicating that they considered the French proposals inadequate and unsatisfactory.

The Americans handed over data, which the French spent the afternoon examining and preparing further proposal for presentation at next session.

Although the members of the American commission are not at all optimistic, it is understood that the majority feel that considerable progress has been made towards reconciliation of the divergent Franco-American views.

*Puris, Sept. 27.*

The "Journal" publishes a report from Washington that M. Caillaux has telegraphically informed M. Painleve on the state of the debt negotiations and the concessions he reckoned on making. He stated that the American counter proposals claimed maximum annuities of $120,000,000 compared with the 80,000,000 contemplated by M. Caillaux.

The “Le Matin” publishes a report for the same capital stating that the Americans presented a totally unexpected claim for the payment of interest on $245,000,-000 which the United States treasury advanced to the Bank of France which it demanded should not be regarded as a political loan but a commercial loan, similar to the Bank of England's credit to the Bank of France.

## [10] FRENCH AVIATION.

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*Paris, Sept. 26.*

The aviators Tarnscan and Favreau, who started at noon yesterday upon an attempt to break Droughin Landry's long distance record flight, crashed at Dreux to-day.

Both avivators were seriously injured, and their machine, which was specially built for their proposed flight from Paris to New York, was wrecked.

It is officially stated that Tarnscon and Favreau were only slightly injured.

The accident occurred at 3.30 in the morning, during a storm which necessitated a forced landing. Flames burst out as the machine grounded.

Watchers at Etampes saw the machine pass thirteen times, equivalent to 1950 kilometres, when it failed to reappear and search parties were sent out. Flight-Lieutenant Thored, piloting a seaplane weighing a ton, accomplished a flight with engine stopped lasting 213 minutes.
