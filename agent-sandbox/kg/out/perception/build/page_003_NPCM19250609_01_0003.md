Speaking of the forms of marriage and divorce practised in the country, she said that so far as Royalty and Priests were concerned, their marriage was "for eternity." A divorce could be obtained in a civil marriage, and in such an event everything was divided equally between man and woman, even to cutting in two a carpet. In a civil marriage a husband had to provide guarantors. If a husband beat his wife she was entitled to monetary compensation.

Mrs. Forbes produced garments in wonderfully worked silk, which had been presented to her by the Empress of Abyssinia in Addis Abeba, the capital. They comprised trousers of brocaded silk, over which are worn inner and outer robes of dazzling colours. These are worn by the ladies at the Court. The Empress is known as "The Queen of the Kings of Ethiopia," daughter of Menelik, conqueror, the Lion of Judah. She is a direct lineal descendant of Soiomon by the Queen of Sheba.

NATIVES AND THE CAMERA.
Her Majesty received Mrs. Forbes at seven o'clock in the morning and explained that as she was giving a feast the following day to 5,000 people she had to be in the kitchens to see that everything was all right. They eat their meat raw, the belief being that raw meat makes good warriors.

The natives thought the cinema to be a new form of religion. One day when a film was being taken of the harvesters they all dropped tools and bowed their heads to the ground. Others took the camera for a maxim gun and threw stones and spears at the party.

In the course of her journey, Mrs. Forbes travelled 1,100 miles by caravan. The party began the journey from the South of Abyssinia and were met on the Northern boundary by lorries sent by the Italian Government.

## [17] BUILT BY ANGELS

"A country of mountains of loose stones pinned together by thorns," was the impression she had formed of Abyssinia, said Mrs. Rosita Forbes, the traveller, on her return from that country to London. Traversing districts untrodden before by a white woman, she had been taken for a white angel, and one warrior had desired the same kind of soap, which should wash him white

Relating her experiences, Mrs. Forbes said that one of the main objects of the trip was to procure films of the troglodyte city of Lalibehla, where there are eleven wonderful churches underground. She visited each with the exception of that dedicated to the Virgin Mary. "Nothing female (she said) is allowed to enter that court. I wonder what would happen if a female sparrow built its nest there." There was a legend that this city with its bloodred sandstone courts was built by angels. In reality it was built by 600 slaves whom King Lalibehla brought into Abyssinia. They could not work fast enough for the tyrant king, and therefore had to carry on during the night. Hence it was said that the angels worked at night time and the slaves in the day time.

The Lord Chief Justice and Justices Sheurman and Salter, sitting last month as a King's Bench Divisional Court, discharged, without costs, the rule nisi granted on March 27 against the editor, publisher, and printer of the "People" for a writ of attachment for alleged contempt of Court in publishing articles in that newspaper relating to William Cooper Hobbs. Hobbs was sentenced at the Central Criminal Court to two years' hard labour for conspiring to defraud Sir Hari Singh—the "Mr. A" in the Robinson v. Midland Bank case—and for receiving certain cheques knowing them to have been obtained by unlawful means, and his appeal against conviction and sentence was stated to be pending at the time the rule was granted.

In the articles complained of, it was said, Hobbs was described as "The Wizard Crook of the Underworld," as being guilty of "Diabolical noguary," and as being "a master crook," and was referred to as having "deserved a sentence of 10 years' penal servitude." Complaint was made that the articles had caused Hobbs such angish that he felt that he could not carry out the intention he had of conducting his own appeal.
