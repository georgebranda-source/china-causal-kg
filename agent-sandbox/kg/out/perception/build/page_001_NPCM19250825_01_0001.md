## [8] BLOCKADE MACAO?

**CANTON THREATENS NAVAL ISOLATION.**

**ABOYREMB ASK FOR HELP.**

Reading very much like an inspired message is a report in a Canton vernacular paper, the "Kwok Wu Po," in which it is suggested that Macao be blockaded because "there are five steamers playing between that port and the Imperialistic Colony of Hongkong."
Briefly, the article says that several butches of strike pickets and delegates have returned to Canton after making investigations in Macao and adjacent Chinese territory. The Executive of the National strike is satisfied that nothing can be done to cut off steamship communication between Macao and Hongkong. But Macao is only acting as a clearing house for its hinterland. Accordingly the Executive must take steps to prevail on the Kuomintang Government, says the "Kwok Wu Po," to send Canton's warships to the waters outside Macao. Those armed vessels could then blockade Macao and cut it off completely, as regards river traffic, with China.
It is also suggested that the Canton Navy act in conjunction with the Strike Executive in maintaining the proposed blockade on Macao as the strikers are better able to carry out an embargo on passengers, foodstuffs, and any other merchandise.

## [9] Shekki Cut Off.

<sub>section under [8] BLOCKADE MACAO?</sub>

*(An Occasional Correspondent.)*

The tow-boat which runs to Macao daily from Shekki has been prevented from coming. Accordingly there are fewer passengers from that port who arrive here and re-embark for Hongkong. Most of these are real workers returning to their posts.

Recently the local commander at Shekki has gone out of his way to extend protection to people who want to sail from his port for Macao and to merchants importing cargo from Macao or exporting produce thereto. Unfortunately, he has been overcome by a batch of Whampoa Cadets sent there to obtain a footing for the first model of district government by Communism.

At first the commander forbade the Cadets to land in Shekki. Eventually he wavered and was soon overpowered. With the Cadets getting in, the local strike delegates—who had been in hiding from the former commander—came out into the open and held up the towboat. Previously the strike pickets had been kept away by threats of imprisonment for intimidation.

## [12] LOCAL SCHOOLS.

**ALL TO RE OPENED AS USUAL.**

**AWATING A LEAD.**

Enquiries made as to the position of local schools in relation to returning students show that without exception they will be re-opened as usual in September, although nothing is known as to the numbers

of those returning. The same applies to the University where, of course, there is a large number already in the Colony at the various Halls.

Some of the students who are due to take examinations, and whose parents are not willing for them to return, are making arrangements to take those examinations as external students.

Much surprise is expressed in local educational circles that the Government has given no lead in the matter of policy to be adopted toward students who are known to have been responsible for much of the trouble which resulted in the Easter term being prematurely ended.

there were 7,000 coal coolies in the Colony before the strike, there are now only a few hundred; but this is ridiculous," said our informant. He gave as the reason for misbelieving these figures facts regarding coal coolie boarding houses in the Colony.

It would appear that coal coolie labour, as being one in connection with which there is always in normal times a very strong demand, is very highly organised in Hongkong. Shipping firms have to deal through contractors who in their turn pass on orders for labour to the No. 1 coolies in charge of large numbers of coal coolie boarding houses scattered throughout the Colony. He had made enquiries at these houses and was satisfied that the percentage of coal coolies who had left the Colony was nothing like so great as had been represented said the "China Mail" informant. "It is nothing but a ring to send up charges beyond all reason" was his comment.

Asked if ordinary coolie labour was not now plentiful and whether arrangements could not be made direct for the supply of such labour, our informant said that the work called for great stamina and a certain amount of skill. Orders had to be performed quickly in a stated time and the contractors and "No. 1 coolies" certainly seemed to be able to get work accomplished in half the time that ordinary street coolies were able to. There was not the slightest reason why they should be making such exorbitant charges, however, as the decrease in the number of coal steamers to be attended to was in proportion to the decreased number of coolies. The only shortage there might be was purely artificial in that a coolie who, before to day's exorbitant charges earned $7 a week by working all the week, was content to work for two days only for the same money. The matter was one calling for intervention, was the opinion of the shipping representative in question, who thought very strongly on the matter.

## [15] RECOMMENDED BY REDS.

(From Our Own Correspondent.

*Canton, August 24.*

Recommended by M. Borodin, the Soviet emissary in Canton, Koo Ying-fan is mentioned as the most likely successor to Liu Chung-oi (the murdered Kuomin-tang Minister of Finance). Other names have been put forward for the post of chief of the labour unions.

Hu Han-min (Wu Hon-man) is stated as about to be despatched to Peking owing to the alleged failure of Sun Fo and Fu Ping-sheung to come to an understanding with Peking in connection with the Shakkee shooting incident.

Dr. C. C. Wu is rumoured as contemplating retirement, for the time being at least. The reason can only be guessed and the report has yet to be confirmed. Tuchun Wu, the chief of Police, is publicly stated to have aendered his resignation as the suspects arrested after the assassination of Liu Chung-oi are said to be connected with one of his departments.

Chan Chow-lam, the editor of the Hongkong "China News"—a vernacular paper which was closed by the Police—has died in the Kung Yee Hospital. After his release by the Hongkong authorities about two months ago, he came up to Canton when he was given an appointment. He was with Liu Chung-oi last Friday and mortally wounded.

## [21] COLONY'S LATEST.

**HOTEL METROPOLE NOW OPEN.**

A welcome addition to the hotels of the Colony is the Hotel Metro-pola, No. 22, Ice House Street, which has just been opened under capable and experienced management. The building itself, six storeys high, is of concrete. The rooms are all light and airy, the most of them having verandahs. The furnishings combine comfort with good taste to a happy degree. The floors are served by a lift of the latest automatic pattern, and each floor has a telephone. The sanitation it needs no saying is modern in every respect. Altogether the new hotel presents a very pleasing appearance, and there can be no doubt that it will soon become very popular.

## [22] BAMBOO BUNDLES.

**DISPUTE OVER CONSIGNMENT.**

A consignment of over 800000 bundles of bamboo, which, in spite of disturbed conditions, had been brought to Hongkong for sale, formed the subject of Summary Court proceedings this morning, plaintiff and defendant both claiming to be dealers in the trade.
It was the contention of the plaintiff that defendant had bought the consignment from him and not paid for it, whilst the defendant asserted that the plaintiff was his (the defendant's) agent and had been paid the money to go up country and get he bamboo.

After evidence had been heard Mr. Justice Wood found for the defendant with costs.

"His Honour the Chief Justice paid a tribute to the services rendered to the public by Mr. Focken on this occasion, and expressed his keen appreciation of his fine act."

Continuing, Mr. Wodehouse said that Mr. Focken's name has now been added to the long list of European civilians and service men who have from time to time given valuable assistance to the Police. "What we naturally expect of them," he said, "we always get."

The speaker expressed his pleasure at the part played by Mr. Li Chi-kong, especially as what he termed the Chinese national attitude—"each for himself, no b'long my pidgin"—was often encountered by the local Police Force, as every other Police Force in China, in the course of their duty. It was well known in the Colony that a crime of violence could be committed in a crowded thoroughfare without anyone lifting a finger, whereas in any other place in Europe or America a hue and cry would be raised. That being so, he considered that Mr. Li had set a good example to his fellow countrymen, which he (the speaker) hoped would be followed in future. (Applause).
The presentations were then made, Mr. Focken receiving a silver cigarette case and a letter of commendation, and Mr. Li Chikong a cheque and a letter of commendation.
