## [1] LEAGUE COUNCIL

**HOME GOVERNMENT AND DOMINIONS**

**CONSTANT CONSULTATION.**

*London, Feb. 17.*

A suggestion that the Dominions should be consulted before the British delegate on the League Council voted on the question of the new permanent members of the Council in addition to Germany under the several questions in the House of Commons.

Sir Austen Chamberlain, Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs, replying, said that the Government was already in communication with the Dominions. In regard to the question raised, He pointed out that a permanent seat on the Council was attributed to the British Empire, but the extent to which the occupant of the seat at a given moment could speak on behalf of the Dominions, and the circumstances of each particular case and the character of the communifications which had passed between the Imperial and the Dominions. Governments.

The Dominions, Sir Austen Chamberlain added, were separately represented on the Assembly. The Dominions there acted on the instructions of their own Governments, although frequent consultations were held between all the Empire delegates, whilst as regards the future the Imperial Government would pursue the same policy of consultation with the Dominions regarding other applications, for permanent membership as with any other international issue of importance.

## [2] Another Canard.

<sub>section under [1] LEAGUE COUNCIL</sub>

The report that Sir Eric Drummond (Secretary-General) of the League of Nations) and Dr. Stresemann (Germany Minister for Foreign Affairs) had agreed that Germany alone should be granted a permanent send on the Penguin Council in March is entirely without foundation.

The true question of the composition of the Council was discussed, and Dr. Stresemann pointed out that serious difficulties would arise if the Council were suddenly engaged by the inclusion of other members besides Germany; but he authoritatively emphasised that the foreign Government concerned must decide the future composition of the Council. Sir Eric Drummond confirmed himself to expressing his personal views, which are stated to be sympathetic toward the German attitude.

## [3] Mode of Procedure.

<sub>section under [1] LEAGUE COUNCIL</sub>

According to the arrangements made between Sir Eric Drummond and the Foreign Office, Germany's application for admission to the League will be referred to a Committee appointed by the Assembly at Geneva on March 8.

This Committee will elect a Sub-Committee at which the German delegate will probably give information.

The Assembly vote will not likely be taken before March 10.

As soon as admission to the League is agreed to, the Council will consider the question of giving Germany a permanent seat on the Council. If this is carried unanimously the Assembly will be asked to adopt it, for which a simple majority is sufficient.

## [10] MILL HOURS.

**LABOURITÉS ALLEGATION**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

London Feb. 17.

In the House of Commons, in reply to questions, Sir A. D. Steel-Maitland, Minister of Labour, said that statistics showed that the working hours in the cotton mills in Japan in 1924 averaged 9½ with two shifts daily.

Mr. T. Johnstone (Labourite) suggested that Japanese machinery was running 22 hours daily. He asked whether the Government refused to invite Japan to the International Conference dealing with the eight-hour convention and whether it proposed to boycott a country whose goods were produced in violation of the Washington convention.

Sir A. D. Steel-Maitland said that he was "unaware that the mills were working 22 hours, but if Mr. Johnstone had fresh information he would be glad to get it. As regards the Conference, the Minister pointed out that it was aimed preliminarily to get some agreement with some of the principal countries, after which it would be practicable to proceed with other countries.

## [16] AIR MASTERY

**FLYING IN FOG IS TOO RISKY.**

**GIANT ALL-METAL 'PLANE**

The Air Ministry, after a costly series of experiments extending over three years, is inclining to the belief that flying in fog will never be commercially practicable, owing to the pilots' human limitations.

Even the most daring stunters show marked disinclination to lose sight of the ground. Rather than trust to instruments of proved reliability, giving their height, and direction, and to gyroscopes, ensuring stability in a fog, they essay low flying, which is attended with appalling risks of collision with trees, chimneys and other objects.

So few pilots are able to overcome this natural psychological trait that the Air Ministry is likely to abandon its fog flying programme, apart from the installation of emergency apparatus at civilian aerodromes.

## [20] SHINGLED HAIR.

**OF IT**

**SOLD TO MAKE EYELASHES**

What becomes of all the hair women have shed during the past three years? Many left a pound or two of hair behind at the hair dresser's; others carefully carried away a neat parcel weighing a few ounces.

Those who cherished their hair are lucky, for nowadays there is a huge demand for long hair. This is made into switches and coiled round the head and fluffed into curls for evening wear.

The owners have had to spend a good deal on their revered tresses, however. About two guineas is charged for treating the hair directly it is cut off.

It has to be cleaned and prepared in a certain way or it becomes dull and lustreless. When the time comes to make it up for use, many guineas will not cover the cost.

Hence many of the new poor sell their own hair and buy a coil of someone else's—of inferior quality but at a lower rate.
New women, however, are poor enough or bold enough to collect the clippings that are shorn from their shingle every week. The hairdresser is careful that they should not.

For these clippings are valuable to him—they are sold to make into eyelashes.

At present, though eyebrows are "thin and pencilled", eyelashes are long and thick, and nothing makes such effective natural ones as human hair.

Girls who clip their shingles at home sometimes stuff a gift pillow with their hair, and at the moment many are saving their clippings with a view to making moustaches for amateur theatricals.

Some clever Frenchwomen have used the hair mixed with embroidered silks to form an original type of trimming for dresses and jumpers. A monogram of twisted hair on the blouse pocket is considered very chic in Paris.
Hair bracelets and hair rings after the Victorian fashion are being revived, but nowadays the hair belongs to the living, not to the dead.

## [21] GIRL ON FIRE.

**DEATH AFTER DASH INTO AUDIENCE.**

**IN JUVENILE TABLEAU**

Nearly 1,000 parents and children were alarmed at the Temperance Hall, Newport, by the cry of "Hire."

While one of 100 juvenile Rechabites performing in a tableau, “Butterfly Queen,” Lilian Roberts, aged 13, was in the dressing-room preparing for her last appearance on the stage as an Old English dancer, her dress of muslin become ignited when she was looking into a mirror over a large fire.

She rushed screaming towards the stage where 50 children were performing. A man tried to stop her from getting into the body of the hall, but with frantic screams and her dress in flames she broke away from him.

Others caught her and the fire was beaten out with coats. The girl was taken to hospital, where she died a few hours later. Mr. Henry Howell, one of the stewards, had his hands burned, but no one in the audience was injured.
