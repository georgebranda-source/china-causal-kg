"We adhere to our opinion that, if the strike can die a natural death, it had better be allowed to do so. If it can be broken by means of passive resistance, there will be the less heart-burning and bitterness afterwards. And if General Shing will act up to the threat of his own proclamations, put down the unions and suppress the intimidation, the workers would go back to their work with alacrity. Welcome news was received last night that he had caused six agitators to be arrested and there are other indications that his forbearance is reaching its limit. It needs only that he should shoot one intimidator, to make the rest understand that their game is up. But if he does not take action on resolute lines, then it will become necessary for the Settlement authorities to do so. Already it begins to be felt by persons in a position of responsibility that the strike and boycott can only be ended by a display of force. And if that line is reluctantly decided upon, there are plenty of ways in which it can be adopted. Why, for example, should the Settlement continue; by means of volunteer labour replacing the strikers, to supply water and light to Chapel, the headquarters of the strike? And why should the Chinese workmen who will not allow their fellows to work—every office probably knows whom it has to thank for this among its own former employees—be permitted to reside in the Settlement free to carry on their silent terrorization? As for those convicted in the Mixed Court, we cannot unfortunately use the "cat" but there are plenty of other more appropriate punishments for them, as Hongkong has shown.

The putting of agitators to "useful if unpleasant duties" in connection with the sanitation of the Colony has particularly met with approval as a fitting punishment, also the campaign conducted against intimidators and the banishment of idlers.

## [15] CHINESE PROCLAMATIONS.

Simultaneously Mr. Arthur Sowerby returns to the charge with his demand for a forward policy, asking if we are still in the same mind as when a week ago we deprecated the line he wished to take. And yet another correspondent details a number of measures which might be taken to break the boycott. The answer to Mr. Sowerby is that a week ago things may have been different from what they are to-day. In point of fact they were. At that time it appeared that the strike funds were running out and that General Shing Shi-lien's proclamations might be expected to have an effect on the intimidators. With regard to the latter we have been waiting
patiently. It is difficult to belliave that a man in General Shing's position can speak so plainly as he has done and not give effect to his words. Surely he must "lose face" by falling to do so to an extent far worse than any unpopularity he might earn with the riff-raff by acting sternly in defence of law and order. Nevertheless, the intimidation has grown daily worse, the strike funds have undoubtedly been replenished and the extreme party of the Chinese Chamber of Commerce is completely in the saddle.
