## [10] LEAGUE AND OPIUM.

The opium question once more having engaged the attention of a world conference and a measure of publicity having been given in the Home papers to the meeting held at the end of last month by the Advisory Committee on Opium of the League of Nations, it may not be unprofitable to recapitulate facts regarding measures taken to suppress the traffic in China and the response there has been to them. Since Britain by drastically reducing the export of opium from India to China gave proof of the sincerity of its desire to help China in the suppression of the consumption of the drug, opium growing in China has increased on an unprecedented scale and although there are in being restrictions on imports of Turkish Persian and other opiums, these in effect are nullified to a considerable extent by the operations of vast smuggling concerns centring chiefly around the Persian Gulf. Provided that China carried out the arrangements for proportionally reducing her production and consumption (measures which were foreshadowed with a great flourish of trumpets when the Republic was ushered in) the British Government were prepared to continue the rate of reduction with regard to Indian opium so that such export might in time entirely cease. To refer to more local conditions, it was this desire to reduce opium smoking and particularly the deleterious nature of its effects that led when undertakings were entered into by the several Powers to close all opium divans in concessions in China, to the establishing of a Government monopoly in Hongkong for the purpose of reducing the amount of opium consumed by weakening the drug's potency and making the price more exacting. China has failed to carry out her part of the undertaking with regard to production and consumption in China generally and her delegates to the League of Nations Opium Advisory Committee would have us believe that this is due not to lack of sincerity on her part but to factors beyond her control. Whether or not she has control over the provinces where the poppy is being cultivated so extensively to-day, it is a known fact that little has been done to limit production — in fact taxes on opium have come to be looked upon as a convenient means of raising revenue. The recent meeting held at Geneva was for the purpose of ratifying the Convention providing for the closest control over the manufacture of dangerous drugs. The American delegation would appear to be against ratification on the grounds that the Convention does not deal sufficiently stringently with opium smoking in the territories where it is still permitted, and they want international legislation to prohibit production of opium. The official British attitude is that the Protocol constitute a real advance in that respect, and that the immediate business is to pass the Convention in order that the machinery for effectively dealing with smuggling may be set up as soon as possible. Such a view does not overlook the fact that to all thinking Chinese not peculiarly interested in the trade or cultivation of opium-the use of the drug is looked upon as undesirable because of the drain of bullion from the country, the liability to famine through the cultivation of opium where cereals should be grown and the corruption of state officials but it must always be borne in mind that the demand for the drug in the first place came from China and so long as there are millions of people in Asia who demand opium, and until individual persons can be taught to see the folly of their vice there will always be other people to supply it—for a consideration.

When a London policeman puts up his hand to stop traffic, the traffic stops! But not in Paris? Ah, non.—M. Morain.

## [12] A YACHTSMAN'S PARADISE NORFOLK BROADS.

The Norfolk Brands remain, perhaps, the least appreciated of Britain's national playgrounds, writes Crawford Price in the "Christian Science Monitor." True, there is a loyal corrie of enthusiasts, whose ardour remains undamped through the worst of English summers, and who year by year respond to the call of the wide writers of East Anglia. But apart from an increasing flotilla of motor-boats—a questionable gain—the floating population of Broadland grows little with the passing of time.

In the case of a people so endured with the sailor instinct as the British, there can be no explanation of this save the love of the sea. Yachtsmen are only now learning not to despise the Broads, and to recognize that, if sailing on these waters is unaccompanied by the dangers and discomforts of life on the ocean wave, it nevertheless has its compensations and provides an even greater test of their mastery of sail and tiller than the more open navigation around the coast.
What are these Broads? Briefly put, they are tidal lakes whose genesis may be traced back to those ancient days when the sea claimed as its own much of what is now the County of Norfolk, when Yarmouth, of bloster, fome, was an island, and Norwich a flourishing port. As the sea receded, it left its rivers, while, in addition, the waters remained on many of the low-lying marshes, and thus be questioned a cluster of tidal lakes, thousands of acres in extent, of wondrous and kaleidoscopic charm, the home of myriad fish and water fowl, the haunt of artists and lovers of all that is most beautiful and restful in untouched nature.
The rivers and Broads, which comprise about 300 miles of navigable waterway, provide an inland sailing ground unqualified in all Europe. Three rivers, the Yare, the Bare and the Waveney, are having their outlet at Yarnouth, form the main channel, and they are connected to the Broads (of which

old country villages vic with ruined castles and abbeys and historic old churches to vary the monotony—if monotony it can be—of scudding along windswept reaches and leisurely cruising up winding streams fragrant with mint and meadowsweet.

The conditions of life on the Broads have been likened to those obtaining in the holiday camps beside the Adirondack lakes. To a certain extent that is true; but the yachtsman enjoys, as a rule, a greater measure of creature comfort. To be sure, a great many still prefer to rough it, although even the cabin cruisers, which constitute the most popular craft, are nowadays well designed and fitted. The tendency, however, is toward larger and more commodious yachts.

A first step in this direction was taken in the adaptation of the wherry for pleasure purposes. The Norfolk wherry is a distinctive type of craft, evolved during the centuries as the most suitable for these waters. It varies in length from fifty to sixty feet, is of broad beam and shallow draught, and as to rig carrier only one large mainsal.
Scores of these craft have been fitted up as yachts, and provide ample accommodation for eight to ten persons with two of a crew. But the wherry has two outstanding disadvantages for pleasure work. It is ungainly and lacking in deck space. Thus there has been brought into being the wherry-yacht, a vessel combining the comfort, convenience and remarkable sailing qualities of the one type with the graceful lines and greater deck spaces of the other.
Abord a wherry-yacht a family can pass an ideal holiday. Internally there are all the amenities of a small bungalow.
