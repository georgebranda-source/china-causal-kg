## [3] SOVIET'S COFFIN.

**MOSCOW AND FENG.**

The Marbin correspondent of the Shanghai Mercury writes on March 24:—

Marshall Wu. Fei-fu having declared in a communication that he he proposes convening a representative National Assembly for the purpose of bringing into existence a legal central government, and Marshal Chang Tso-lin having affirmed that he has no desire to take a prominent part in the formation of the new government except desiring to remove every vestige of Bolshvism from China, it is generally believed in Harbin that the field on the coffin of Soviet activities in the Chinese Republic will shortly be nailed and the coffin itself given an unostentatious burial.

There is considerable perturbation visible in Soviet circles in Barbin and all the so-called "commercial agents" of Moscow have been holding conferences day in and day out these past couple of weeks. In view of the fact, that the mills in Russia are not even able to fill the domestic demand for goods, it is not difficult to conclude that not all the "commercial agents" of the Soviet government are what they are represented to be.

That they should be, highly nerve-strung over the impending wholesale collapse of the Nationists is only to be expected for with a untitled China against them, the Soviet government and its agents out here will have to look for fresh laurels. Moreover, the seizure of the Volunteer Fleet steamer "Oleg" off Taikub by the Fengtian navy after the discovery was made that the vessel was loaded to the gunwales with arms and ammunition, will not help the Reds at all in their professions off friendship for the Chinese nation.

Moscow is said to have involved the iso-called Christian General and his suite to escape into Russia and it is thought likely that Feng Yu-hsiang will accept this invitation. According to a Mukden report, he has just announced that he would proceed to Europe, via Russia and that he has definitely decided to withdraw from politics. It is, generally believed here, however, that Moscow is about the furthest point the worthy Marshal would go and it is considered even likely that he would attempt, whilst there, to reorganise his movement against Chang Tso-lin and Wu Pei-fu.

There is a mournful tone apparent in the vituperations of the Soviet Press against the Imperialistic Powers and their recent ultimatum to Peking, but amongst the non-Reds jubilation reigns supreme over the prospect of the early passing of the Bolsheviks in China.

It is given out that a joint attack would be made in due course against Canton and it is considered certain that in the South also the deathknell of Bolshevism will be sounded; thereby depriving Comrade Borodin of his advisory post with the Cantonese "government."

## [5] BOXER

**A COLLEGE PLEA.**

From the Dean of the National Southeastern University College of Agriculture, Nanking, we have received a lengthy plea for a position of the British Boxer Indemnity Fund to be invested in the promotion by Chinese agriculture. We quote the following excerpts:

The China Indemnity Act, enacted by the Parliament of Great Britain on June 30, 1925, authorising the remission of the Boxer Indemnity. Is intended to promote mutual understanding and friendship between Great Britain and the Republic of China, and that such a measure is effective to end, is recognised by far-sighted people everywhere. To what extent the will of Parliament may be carried out, how far the mutual welfare of both countries are promoted, depend upon the disposition of the funds—a heavy responsibility which rests upon the judgment of your Committee. Happily the grave task has been entrusted to the hands of recognised leaders of intellectual thought in both countries, so that mature consideration of all phases of the intricate problem before the final judgment is passed is assured. Feeling that your high aims can be served best by the fullest expression of responsible opinion in this country. I beg to lay before you a proposal that will give the Friendly gesture of the British people quick and tangible foundation.

If your Committee is to initiative in China a far-reaching undertaking, which, after a period of not more than five years, can assure the greatest physical and spiritual benefit to the greatest number of the Chinese people, and at the same time afford the British people equal benefits, it is my earnest opinion that that undertaking is the promotion of modern agriculture in this eminently agricultural country. The foundation of China is agriculture. Fully 85 per cent of Chinese men and women are tillers of the soil. To spread modern agricultural knowledge, to institute modern agricultural practices, throughout this country, means the practical recreation of the lives of 300,000,000 people. When Chinese agriculture is effectively developed and efficiently maintained, Britain gains an important new market of raw products so vital to a highly specialised manufacturing country. In my opinion, the will of Parliament limiting the remitted portion of the Boxer Indemnity to such uses as shall benefit both countries can best be realised through the development of the agricultural possibilities of China, her greatest potential contribution to man

To justify this conviction some of the obvious benefits which both Britain and China may reasonably hope to derive from an improved agricultural condition in this country are enumerated in the document sent to us.

The Dean concludes: "To summarise the annual expenditures, the expense of training agricultural experts "amounts to $225,000, that for establishing and maintaining three agricultural colleges amounts to $1,800,000 and that for financing the farm loan banks' amounts to $1,000,000, bringing the sum to the grand total of $3,025,000 each year for the period of 15 years. As the result of the support of the Chinese Indemnity Fund during the 15-year period, there shall then be (1) well established and stable farm loan banks operating on a capital of $20,000,000 for the sole purpose of uplifting the economic status of the farmers; (2) three thoroughly equipped and highly efficient agricultural colleges, founded through indemnity funds and perpetuated by the earnings of the farm loan banks, devoting the whole energies to research, teaching and extension for the sole purpose of making scientific agriculture available to every farmer; and (3) three hundred highly specialised agricultural experts dispersed throughout the breadth and depth of China, who in conjunction with the farm loan banks and agricultural colleges will surely make modern agriculture a potent force for the good of mankind.

"The tremendous exertions" of the three million Chinese farmers should be turned to some better

account than the maintenance of a mere animal existence. Scientific agriculture alone is sufficient to turn the frugal industrious millions of Chinese farmers into happy human beings, happy not only in being able to breathe for the first time an atmosphere free from the fear of the inevitable famine—the sword of Damocles for ever hanging overhead—but especially happy in being able to share the fruits of their labours with those whose lines are cast in less pleasant places.

"I am satisfied that nothing less than a magnificent scheme would satisfy the will of a nation of Empire-builders. Deyote half the remitted portion of the Boxer Indemnity Fund to quicken the forty-centuries-old agriculture of China—and" Great Britain shall reap" where she has sown. Let the Chinese toiler toil not in vain—then shall every creature in every part of the globe augment his portion of happiness."

## [10] MISSION'S LOSS.

**ARRIVED IN 1868**

Mrs. Elizabeth Jane Judd, whose death is announced, was a member of the China Inland Mission for 58 years. Born 82 years ago, Mrs. Judd left England for China in 1867, immediately after her marriage to the Rev. Charles Henry Judd.

When they landed at Shanghai on March 3, 1868, after a three months' voyage in the ten-clipper "Taiping," the mission had, in the whole of China, only eight stations and 34 missionaries, including the new recruits. For 27 years she and her husband worked in Yangchow, Wuchang, Chefoo, Chinkiang, and Shanghai. The house which her husband built in Chefoo developed both into a sanatorium and later into a school for missionaries' children, of which one of her sons is now the principal.

The chief work of Mrs. Judd, however, was in Ninghai, 20 miles from Chefoo. She and her husband were twice driven out of the city. The first time the shock nearly cost Mrs. Judd her life, but they persevered and worked there till they left China. The district is a very poor one, in which, in addition to the ordinary work of evangelisation, they felt called upon to do a great deal to relieve the poverty of a population larger than the produce of the area would support.

Mrs. Judd worked indefatigably among the women; and was instrumental, in starting a lace industry to add to their earnings. After she and her husband returned to England in 1894, this work developed considerably, and she was actively engaged in England in finding markets for the lace produced.

Mr. Judd died in England in October. 1919. They had six sons, two of whom, Dr. Frederick Judd and Mr. Charles Judd, are on the staff of the China Inland Mission in the mission field.
