The fourth annual general meeting of the Hongkong Amusements, Ltd., is advertised to be held in the Company's office, China Buildings, on Saturday, May 16, at noon, to be followed by an extraordinary meeting at 1215 p.m.

The annual meeting of worshippers at the Peak Church will be held at St. John's Cathedral Hall, on Tuesday, May 12, at 5.15 p.m. The meeting is being called to pass accounts and elect officials.

The opium revenue of the Straits Settlements for the first quarter of this year was $2,804,822 as against $3,226,185 for the corresponding quarter of last year. Liquors and tobacco show slight increases.

With about 30,000,000 copies of the English Bible now being distributed each year, after 40 years the Bible still completely overtops all other "best sellers," according to a statement of the Federal Council of Churches in Washington, announcing the four hundredth anniversary of the first translation of the New Testament from Greek into English, to be observed of this year.

The cost of living in Paris has again risen and is more than five times that of the prewar figures, according to the statistics compiled by the Government service. These statistics apply to forty-five commodities. Taking the index number for July, 1914, as 100, the number for last December is 518, and for January of this year 525. The tendency is perpetually upward and the efforts of the Government to redeem its electoral promises in this respect seem doomed to failure.

Italy is to have a national encyclopedia which its organisers hope eventually will compare favourably with the Encyclopedia Britannica. A committee of the Italian Cultural Institute, which was formed through a gift of Giovanni Treccani, is now at work drafting the plans for the encyclopedia, which will offer to the world a statement of Italy's work in nearly every branch of knowledge. The first edition will be of 32 volumes in quarto of about 1,000 pages to each volume.

One case cerebro-spinal fever and one case of enteric, both Chinese, were reported on Friday.

"Lorna Doone" is now being shown at the Queen's Theatre to crowded houses and Cinema-goers have been impressed with the picture.

The “China Mail” acknowledges receipt of “The Romance of the Rockies: “Jasper National Park” a gorgeously illustrated booklet issued by the Canadian Railways.

Controversy over the British Wireless Bill continues. Radio societies and associations are all urging their members to write to their members of Parliament to point out how the bill, as it stands, would retard amateur research. Much apprehension is felt at what looks like the thin end of the wedge of government intrusion.

Twenty members of the Manila Chinese Y.M.C.A. have organized a glee club for the purpose of awakening an interest in vocal and instrumental music among its members. The club will hold weekly and monthly rehearsals in the main building on Calle Benavides. Each section will be in charge of a director. The club plans to give concerts and musicals later in the year.

"An adequate provision for secondary or some form of full-time postprimary education for all children up to the age of 16" is the object of a bill which the House of Commons will debate. It is introduced on behalf of the Labour Party, but it is expected that it will be rejected on the ground of financial stringency. Two members from the Government side have tabled an amendment, repeating on this subject Stanley Baldwin's much less definite election promises of which the chief was for the provision of efficient secondary schools with an adequate number of free places."

The part the drink trade played in the recent Ulster parliamentary elections is referred to by Viscountess Astor in a letter to the press in connection with the controversy she is engaged in with Sir Arthur Holbrook on the subject whether this trade can be disinterested. "Sir James Craig," she writes, "is no pussyfoot, or fanatic; or American, or woman, or anything else which Sir Arthur Holbrook seems to object to. "But because in Ulster's interests he has dared to pass a mild temperance act, "the trade' is against him. Sir James appealed for unity and support in the recent election. The trade answered by running independent candidates and in several constituencies beating Sir James's official representative. The trade showed in Ulster, as it shows everywhere, "that it cannot be disinterested."

Hongkong is not the only place to have enjoyed a lower temperature than usual in the month of April. The following is from a Calcutta paper of April 16:—"After temperatures ranging between 107 and 103 degrees Calcutta has experienced a sudden fall in temperature. This phenomenon has also been observed in other parts of Bengal, and Bihar has been revelling in exceptionally low temperatures. In Dinajpur, in north Bengal, the maximum day temperature on Sunday last was 79.3, or 15.3 normal; in Purnea, in Bihar, the maximum day temperature the same day was 86.7, or 10.3 below normal. In Assam, in Gauhati and Dhubri, day temperatures on Sunday last were 10.2 and 12.4 below normal and Shillong correspondents write of a sudden cold spell."

To the list of medical practitioners has been added the name of Dr. Dorothy Evaleen Galbraith, of the Nethersole Hospital, Member of the Royal College of Surgeons, England, and Licentiate of the Royal College of Physicians, London.

The monocle habit is growing among the suingled sex. The other night at a crowded London night-club no fewer than four ladies were thus adorned, each looking very uncomfortable and obviously afraid that the thing might fall out at any moment. The female eye would appear to be ill adapted to this form of vanity, to which even horn-rimmed spectacles are preferable.

A protest is being raised in the Paris Press against smoking by women in the streets. "After the theatre," expostulates one writer, "it has become an ordinary sight to see a woman produce her cigarette and puff away as she strolls homeward, just like the men. It is mostly foreign women, however, who set this far from graceful example, and we hope they will not find many followers among their French sisters."

The Prince of Wales did not suffer from lack of exercise during his voyage to South Africa. The squash racquet court with its glass roof which he had on the Renown had been moved to the Repulse, and put up at the side of the main deck. It is fitted with electric light, so the Prince can play after dark if his energy so move him. By the Prince's particular desire a billiard table had been installed on board. But the waves and the wind surely had a good deal to say as to its use.

In recent photographs of Princess Mary appearing at public functions, she is shown umbrellaless. Formerly the umbrella was a prominent feature in every photograph of the kind. It was a matter for much chaff amongst her brothers. The Prince of Wales once said, "When Mary is opening a bazaar or a hospital she clings to her umbrella like a drowning can to a straw. First you see the umbrella; then you see Mary." But now the umbrella seems to have disappeared. But why? Has Lord Lascelles said something?

That there is little new under the sun, even with regard to women's fashions, was pointed out by Ethert Brand, curator of the museum at Rotherham in the course of a recent lecture. Referring to an illustration of women dancers on the walls of a Spanish cave, he said these: palaeolithic belles were very near to the present day women with their high hats, chignous, tight waists and bell-shaped skirts. These cave pictures were probably the earliest fashion plates in the world, and were drawn about 20,000 years ago, declared Mr. Brand. Another illustration from Spain showed a costume very much akin to that of the modern flapper.

The greatest interest is taken in Dublin in the manner in which Joseph Devlin's victory in the elections has just been celebrated in Belfast. The proceedings took the form of a huge torchlight procession through west Belfast, the Nationalist stronghold. Electrically illuminated signs, arches and scrolls festooned the streets, great bonfires were lit at the street corners, the flames of which rose upward 20 feet in many cases. The flags, festoons and general decorations took the form of combined green and orange colours. If anyone before the war had said that such a thing would ever be possible, he would have been derided, for in those days the two colours were always a mark or symbol of rivalry and too often of violent hostility. The long procession having wound its way through the chief thoroughfares finished in Smithfield Square where a public meeting was held at which Mr. Devlin said he thanked the people from the depth of his heart for having placed him, not merely at the head of the poll, but so far ahead of the others. His grateful thanks were due, he said, to the many Protestant professional and commercial men who were among the 5,000 Protestant voters whose suffrage he had received. He desired to emphasize this indebtedness. All his energy and labour would be given to fostering good relations between the two communities and to helping toward an improvement in the condition of the workers.

The death is reported of William F. Massey Prime Minister of New Zealand. The Right Honourable William Feigusson Massey has been Prime Minister of New Zealand since 1912, and was the only War Premier within the Empire, who still held power. He was born at Limavady, Co. Derry in March 1856. He went to New Zealand in 1870 to join his parents who had emigrated with the Nonconformist settlers in 1862. There he grew upon the farm, at the same time taking marked interest in local government affairs, and at different times held office. In 1874 he entered the New Zealand parliament as member for Waitemata, and had been member for Franklin since 1896. He represented the Dominion on several occasions, among them the Peace Conference at Paris in 1919 and the Imperial Conference of 1921. He was a Grand Officer of the Legion of Honour and of the Order of the Crown of Belgium.
