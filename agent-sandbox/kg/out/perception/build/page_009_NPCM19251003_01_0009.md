## [8] CLUB NICKNAMES.

**HOME SOCCER TIT-BITS.**

With the start of a new football season, says a Home paper in mail week, club supporters have been turning out in their tens of thousands ready to cheer—and perchance to jeer—their favourite team. While an interesting story could be told of the origin, history, and name of famous clubs, an even more attractive narrative could be woven around "the manner" in which noted football combinations obtained their nicknames. For a club to receive a nickname which is joyously ejaculated by thousands of perfervid supporters is the hall mark of fame.

Many of the nicknames are, of course, obvious. For instance, the Heart of Midlothian are known all over the country as the Maroons, a name derived from the distinctive jerseys worn by the Tynecastle players. The same applies to Rangers, whose supporters yell at the pitch of their voices:—“Come on, the Light Blues,” a reference to the attractive hue of the Ibrox players' shirts.

There is also a smack of their origin in Third Lanark being dubbed the Warriors, as the club were originally known as the Third L.R.V., and even yet in some parts of Glasgow the Cathkin club are referred to as the Volunteers. Then Partick Thistle are known as the Jags, an obvious term emblematic of the prickly qualities of the Scottish thistle, while the Spiders, the name by which Queen's Park are known far and wide, is derived from the black and white striped jerseys worn by the amateurs of Hampden.

Aberdeen players respond vigorously to the encouraging shouts of their followers when they yell:—"Play up, the Dons," although why the Don should be preferred to the Dee is a mystery to many followers of the game in other parts of the country. But Aberdeen, at least up till recently, used to be greeted as the Wasps on account of the yellow and black striped shirts worn by the players of the Pittodrie club.

Two wild demonstrations, involving police and spectators, occurred at the beginning of the season in connection with Scottish League football games at Clydebank and Barrhead respectively.

## [13] COTTON TRADE'S LOSS.

The Chinese situation has cost the Lancashire cotton industry, of which China is the second chief market, the whole of next winter's China trade, and the greater part of next summer's trade. Its losses during the last four months are estimated to have been nearly £1,000,000 a month.

Order for the winter's trade, receivable during the spring and early summer, were completely lost, and most of the order for next summer's trade, which should have been fulfilled by the autumn, and early winter, have been lost also.

Chinese ports are loaded with cotton goods and merchants are unable to effect deliveries.

Private telegraphic advices received, however, show that, despite the embargo against British ships at Chinese ports, announced by the Canton authorities, many British ships with cargoes of cotton have got into Shanghai and are being unloaded.

## [14] WAR NOT WANTED.

We do not, of course, want to go to war with China, further states the "Daily Mail," or if we can possibly help it with any one of the domestic or alien tyrannies which usurp authority in that distracted country. With the toiling Chinese millions we have no quarrel, and we do not believe they have any hostile feeling towards ourselves; the majority are scarcely aware of our existence. Our difficulty is with the "students" and other Westernised agitators who are stirring up the mobs and rowdies of the cities at the instigation of the Russian Soviet. These persons care little for the sufferings they may bring upon Chinese peasants, by cutting off their chief source of foreign supplies, provided they can inflict loss upon Great Britain and add to the industrial depression in this country.

That they can do so, if they are allowed to maintain, the boycott they have declared, is obvious. China is one of the great markets for British manufactured commodities. For some of these products it is the greatest foreign market of all. It is Lancashire's largest customer for certain classes of cotton goods, and many mills will have to close down if the Far Eastern trade is arrested. Yorkshire will be no less badly hit. A most substantial portion of the worsteds, woollens, and dyed cloths manufactured in Bradford and the neighbourhood has been prepared for China. The woollens alone came to £8,387,000 in 1924, and the total export of these classes of goods for the year was little short of ten millions sterling.

The following article is an appreciation of the persistence of the American Museum of Natural History in sending these repeated expeditions to Mongolia, with such eventual rich reward.

The persistence of the American Museum of Natural History in sending repeated expeditions to Mongolia appears to have been richly rewarded. Two years ago Mr. Roy C. Andrews and his scientific colleagues found fossil dinosaurs with their eggs, but this summer they have also found many traces, though no remains as yet, of paluolithic man. The reason why Professor H. F. Osborn has for many years past advocated an exploration of the fossil deposits of Mongolia may be stated very simply. That vast country is a lofty and arid plateau, with no mountains and very few rivers. Consequently, there is a good chance of finding the fossilised remains of animals in a comparatively undisturbed condition. This prediction was confirmed two years ago, when Mr. Andrews, working at a place five hundred miles within the Gobi Desert, came upon the complete skeleton of a dinosaur with the nine eggs that she had just laid before a sandstorm came and buried her. This sand, falling gently, killed but did not crush, and the great reptile, with her eggs, remained slowly fossilising through perhaps ten million years.

In Professor Osborn's view, these Mongolian dinosaurs were ancestors of the still larger land reptiles found in Western America, such as the famous diplodocus that we may see at the Natural History Museum. Indeed, he inclines to regard Mongolian as "a centre of dispersal" of animal life—as the region, that is, where the main kinds or orders developed first, and whence tuoy spread to other parts of the earth. To establish such a during hypothesis we need, of course, infinitely more evidence than is yet available, but the American scientists who have repentedly braved the tigers of the Gobi Desert in the search for fossils have, at any rate, made the theory seem plausible. This summer Mr. Andrews has discovered six mammalian skills—and the remains of a giant rhinoceros, orbitally in strata above those containing the dinosaurs.

But special interest attaches to his finding of arrowheads, spear points, drills, and the like relics of man of the Old Stone Age, because very little is known of man's presence in Mongolia in the remote past, long before the Mongols lived there. Mr. Andrews's finds should throw welcome light on the migrations of those remote ancestors of ours in the Glacial Period, who apparently roamed over the whole earth, since their weapons and tools are much the same wherever they are found; whether in England or Egypt or Turkestan.
