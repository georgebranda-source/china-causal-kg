## [5] CHANGE OF FRONT.

**BETTER CANTON LANDING FACILITIES**

**MILITARY CONTROL**

The “China Mail” reported a few days ago that facilities rather than difficulties were being placed in the way of passengers wishing to land in Canton from the “Honam.” From later reliable reports it would appear that this change of front is due to the action of the military authorities who in this and other directions are evincing their determination to curb the activities of the strikers, as the exercise of power of the latter since the absence of certain troops has incurred disfavour and is looked upon with suspicion.

The supervision of the craft which are now actually placed at the disposal of passengers wishing to land is now in the hands of the military—Whampoa cadets—and although nothing definite can be ascertained as to what transpires when passengers from the "Honam" and "Fatshan" are once ashore it is certainly a fact that no actual charge for transportation is being made. Prior to the handling of disembarkation by the military, a visit to strike headquarters was necessary for the purpose of payment of money for the privilege of landing. It is thought that a small sum has now to be paid at military headquarters instead.

The military have also taken control of outward bound passengers from Canton and greater facilities are thus "available, although a charge in excess of that paid by incoming passengers is said to be levied on them. As the strikers' pickets and the military are still at loggerheads over the matter, most of the sampans bearing outward bound passengers go alongside at night, but the greater freedom which is being exercised in the matter of the issue of passes is evidenced by the number of passengers brought from Canton by the "Honam" yesterday afternoon—150, a record number since the run was re-established between the two ports after the outbreak of the strike.

## [9] $3,301 IN GIRDLE

**AMAH'S LIFE SAVINGS IN JEOPARDY.**

**AN EXPORTING CHARGE**

An elderly Chinese. amah was charged before Mr. S. B. B. McElderry at the Central-Magistracy this morning with attempting to take away from the Colony a sum of $3,301 in bank notes.

Defendant was arrested at the Wing Lok Street wharf yesterday prior to the sailing of the Macao boat when the money was found in a girdle round her waist by a female searcher who was acting under orders from Detective Sergeant McEwen. It was alleged that the defendant reluctantly yielded to the search, stating that the girdle contained only paper.

A Chinese woman who gave evidence was cross-examined by Mr. L. D. Turner, appearing for the defence. This witness stated that she intended to go to Macao and engaged the defendant to carry her luggage. Defendant was not going to Macao, and when she was arrested witness cancelled her trip as the defendant had got into trouble in trying to assist witness.

His Worship desired to have some reasonable explanation for the presence on the defendant's person of such a large sum of money, and she was put into the witness box. She said that the money constituted the savings of 27 years and she always carried it on her person. She was not going to Macao, and had not heard of the regulations regarding export of money.

Mr. Turner submitted that on the evidence it was clear the defendant had no intention of leaving the Colony, and in any case she would not be so rash as to risk losing the savings of 27 years by attempting to get away with such a large wad of notes which was sure to attract the notice of the searchers.

His Worship considered that the defendant had made an attempt to export the money and reserved his decision until to-morsow morning. Meantime an order was made for the defendant's release.

During the hearing of this case, a lukong seated on a side wing exceeded the bounds of Court behaviour by laughing and wriggling about in his seat. This was observed by His Worship, who promptly turned the man out and drew the attention of Inspector Lanigan to his improper conduct.

## [10] STRIKERS KILLED.

**TAIPING INCIDENT CLOSED?**

With an armed escort and supported by soldiers, new strike pickets have entered the port of Ta' Bing (near Bocca Tigris, at the entrance to the Pearl River delta) and assumed duty.

As previously reported, a squabble broke out over trade in saltfish and the local merchants are alleged to have killed four of the pickets and wounded thirty-seven in a pitched fight.

Since then the trouble is stated to have been patched up, hence the despatch of new pickets. Trade between Tai Ping and Canton has resumed.

The bodies of the four dead strikers were conveyed with ritual to Canton and the wounded were sent to the Canton Labourers' Hospital for treatment.

## [11] BITER BIT

*(From Our Own Correspondent.)*

How strikers' armed launches carrying pickets were defeated in a running duel with a Chinese passenger vessel is related by a passenger who admits that he hid in galley of the merchant steamer and lay flat while the shooting was going on.

Having left Macao for Kwong Hoi city, in the Sze Yap districts, the s.s. "Ng Tai Chow" (meaning "Five Great Continents") was challenged in Chinese waters by armed launches. Pickets from Canton had already forbidden the ship to run as it was alleged that passengers had travelled by roundabout routes to get to Macao.

As the “Ng Tai Chay” ignored the strikers she, so the passenger states, was fired-upon. But the strikers reckoned without the arsenal carried by the merchant-man. The fire was returned and the pickets retired, defeated. Subsequently the quarrel was patched up and the “Ng Tai Chow” returned to Macao.

## [15] SWATOW WAR.

**NO NEW DEVELOPMENT REPORTED:**

**ANTI-REDS SHORTEN LINE.**

Conditions at Swatow have, presumably, not changed, there being no direct report to the Colony of any development.

From a semi-official Chinese source it is gathered that the Reds have not made any material advance since the capture of Wai-chow but they may be making fresh dispositions to start another offensive. Although the anti-Red troops have suffered a reverse they are still an integrate fighting force and the initial defeat has been counter-balanced by their shortening the line and joining together.

“Heavy losses” are admitted by the “Canton Gazette” of Monday, in its description of the assault on Waichow, a part of which reads:—

"The defenders of the fortress, certain of ultimate victory, displayed considerable bravery. No sooner would the artillery fire cease than the defenders would creep out from their hiding places and keep up a deadly machine-gun and rifle fire against our attacking troops. Twice were the 21-foot walls being scaled by our Kuomintang troops and twice they were repulsed with heavy losses. Thus a commander of a Kuomintang regiment was killed, several battalion commanders were wounded or killed, a warning not to distinguish between officer and soldier in battle insofar as uniform is concerned. The means employed by the defenders of the fortress were those of the middle ages, mixed with modern weapons. Cotton soaked in inflammable liquids and thrown at the invader. Blankets soaked in the same liquid used to light up the approaches to the fortress at night." Stones, boiling tar, a sort of Greek fire, machine-guns and rifles, even artillery."

The farewell address to H.E. the Governor from the British and Foreign Community, which was extensively signed some months ago, when the departure of Sir Edward Stubbs was first officially announced, will be presented at a gathering to be held in the Theatre Royal at 4 p.m. on Thursday, October 29.

At the same time the address from the Chinese Community will be presented:

Sir Edward Stubbs will also be asked to accept, on behalf of Lady Stubbs, a handsome ring which is the gift of the ladies of the Colony.

The proceedings will be brief, and as this is the last public function in which H.E. the Governor will take part after the trying events of recent months, there will doubtless be a large attendance of the public.

## [18] MISPLACED COAL.

**ALLEGED THEFT AND ABETTING. TEN MEN CHARGED**

At the Kowloon Magistracy this morning, a Chinese junk master was charged with alleged theft of coal and nine others with aiding and abetting. Four of the latter were from the junk and the others from the steam launch “On Fat.”

Mr. J. M. d'Almada Remedios appeared for the five men from the junk and Mr. F. E. Nash defended the launch men.

Sub-Inspect or Andrew said that the men were arrested on Monday at Yaumati when the launch was alongside the junk at the time and the coal was found inside the junk.
The case was remanded till Friday, bail being allowed on $2,000 for the first defendant and $50 for the others.

## [19] NELSON'S FAME

**TRAFALGAR DAY CEREMONY:**

**WHEATH AT CENOTAPH.**

Trafalgar Day was observed locally by the laying of wreaths on the Cenotaph and Wanchai memorial, the arrangements for this observance being in the hands of the branch of the Navy League.

Those present at the ceremonies were: The Hon. Mr. P. H. Holyoak, Sir Henry Pollock, K.C., Mr. W. A. Dowley, Mr. B. Wylie, Lieut. Commander Ingham. Mr. E. F. Aucott, Mr. A. F. Exell and Mr. L. M. Whyte.

The conditions obtaining around the Wanchai memorial were again the subject of comment.

## [22] YOUTH'S OFFENCE.

**THROWING STONES AT A 'BUS.**

**PORTUGUESE BOY FINED.**

Edward. Xavier, 10, was charged at the Kowloon Magistracy this morning with throwing a stone at a motor bus near the junction of Nathan Road and Jordan Road at 9 p.m. on Monday.
Defendant denied the offence and claimed to have been surprised when he was caught while he was in the company of other boys.

A conductor stated in evidence that he saw the defendant throw the stone at the vehicle which struck a glass pane and broke it. He alighted... from the vehicle and caught the defendant, who was with a group of other boys. A conductor on another bus deposed to having seen a stone being thrown, but he did not notice by whom it was thrown.

Describing it as a youthful but dangerous offence, the Magistrate imposed a fine of $5 and payment of $1.50 as compensation for the damage done.
