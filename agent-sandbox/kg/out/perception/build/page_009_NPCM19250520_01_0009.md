## [1] CORRESPONDENCE.

### Garden Road Danger.

[To the Editor of the China Mail.]

Sir.—The Roads Department are to be congratulated on having put in hand much needed improvement in Garden Road. But are they going to stop at the end of the Detention Barracks wall? Surely the most dangerous part of the road is higher up, particularly opposite the Peak Tram station.

The danger of cars stopping at the Cathedral or to change gear lower down, thus putting drivers behind them in a quandary, is as nothing to that of cars stopping at the station where the road is so narrow that even if a motor bike is coming down the opposite way it becomes impossible for anyone following to pull out and pass. I know there is the wall of the Bank's mess premises to prevent expansion and I suppose it is too late now to talk of having those premises removed or taken further back. But what a pity the Tram station was ever allowed to develop that way instead of at the side where the exit is at present. People will have to come off the path that is going to be provided round to the entrance as it is at present and herein lies the greatest danger. Yours etc.

## [3] *(no headline: column 1)*

"Oppressed Chinese Seamen."

(To the Editor of the China Mail)

Sir.—In reference to your leaderette on the above subject, it may interest you to know that confirmation of the gist of the statements made in the circular referred to, could be easily obtained, together with definite facts, figures, from the musters, officers and engineers on board the coasting and river steamers sailing out of Hongkong.

The crew are engaged and paid by one of the numerous "owners" or one of his minions, and though they sign on the articles for the wages agreed upon, after the seamen's strike, between the Seamen's Union and the owners, it is an absolute certainty that not in more than half a dozen companies, at the most, do any of the crew get what they sign on for.

In the U.K., according to the Merchant Shipping Act, the crew are paid by the master, and woe betide any master, or owner, who might try to play tricks with the portage—or wage—bill.

It is not within, my knowledge that any local Act has ever been passed which negatives that clause in the Home Merchant Shipping Act, but, H.K. fashion, a custom has grown up, by which the master has nothing whatever to do with either paying or engaging the crew.

One reason is that the crew are paid monthly, though the articles are for six months. Every month, therefore, each member of the crew signs for his wages as an advance. He is actually paid anything from 10 to, say, 80 percent, of his wages, and signs a receipt in full. He dare not do otherwise. Should the shipping master ask any questions when the crew are signing off at the end of the six months, this receipt would be produced.

The only remedy at all possible, is to enforce the M. S. Act, and make the master responsible for the engaging and paying of the crew—if not the engagement, at least all payments. Incidentally it may, or may not, be news, that there are many Europeans paid on exactly the same system. They sign on for the full wages, and as the price of keeping their job, have to hand back an agreed amount to somebody at the office.
I am referring to Chinese owners, and even then, there are still a few who do not descend quite so low, but unfortunately not nearly enough, and a fast diminishing band.

## [7] CONSTABLE WOUNDED.

**HUNGHOM SENSATION:**

As the result of a desperate attack by two Chinese at Hunghom yesterday afternoon, Indian constable Abdul Khalil was seriously wounded and is now lying in the Government Civil Hospital.

One of the assailants drew a dagger, and the constable got into grips with him. The other man drew the constable's revolver from the holster and fired, wounding the policeman in two places. The latter struggled back to the station and made a report.

Police officers scoured the neighbourhood immediately after and succeeded in arresting a man with the constable's revolver, it is alleged, in his possession.

## [13] WHY SKY IS BLUE.

**LIGHT SCATTERED BY MOLECULES.**

Why the sky is blue was explained by Sir Ernest Rutherford, when, in the theatre of the Royal Institution, he delivered the second of his course of four lectures on "The Counting of Atoms." He explained that the incessant movement of minute particles was first observed by an Englishman named Brown in 1827 and was now called the Brownian movement. It is now known that this constant agitation is a necessary consequence of the invisible and irregular movements of the molecules of the surrounding fluid, to be expected on the kinetic theory of matter. Examples of this Brownian movement shown by minute colloidal particles viewed through a high-power microscope were illustrated in a striking way by a cinematographic film. The behaviour of a large number of equal spheres, obtained from an emulsion of gum arabic or mastic was carefully examined by Perrin. He showed that they behaved in exactly the same way as an atmosphere of molecules of very great size and weight, and that the concentration of particles with height fell off according to the same law as an isothermal atmosphere. Knowing the mass and radii of the drops, he was able to deduce from the distribution of particles the Avogadro constant for a gas, thus fixing the number of molecules in a known weight of matter. About the same number was also deduced from the actual movement of individual drops, and from the rate of rotation of a large selected drop showing that the equipartition of energy held for large particles as well as for a gas. Finally, the Brownian movement of a small oil drop in air has been accurately measured by Fletcher, and has given a very concordant value for the Avogadro constant. These experiments had, in addition to serving as a method of counting the molecules, given a complete verification of the kinetic theory of gases, so the speed and number of collisions a molecule makes per second can be estimated with certainty.

An account was then given of the late Lord Rayleigh's theory of the blue of the sky, which was ascribed to the scattering of the light of the sun by the molecules of air in its path. This scattering is analogous to the scattering of light by fine particles, shown long ago by Tyndall at the Royal Institution. Experiments were shown to illustrate the polarisation of light by liquids and gases containing fine particles in suspension, and the cause of the red sunset. The present Lord Rayleigh, and afterwards Cabannes, showed experimentally that this scattering of light by gases could be measured in the laboratory. The variation of the intensity of the sun's light at different zenith distances gives an actual measure of this scattered light, and from this the number of molecules in a cubic content of air can be directly deduced. This value is in good accord with other data.

## [18] SEWER MYSTERY.

**WOMAN'S TERRIBLE ORDEAL.**

The gossips and police of Brie Comte Robert are now exercising their ingenuity and imagination on a mystery which seems at present unlikely ever to be solved. Deroubaix, a gardener, was returning to his house one morning, when he heard a woman's voice calling for help. There was no person in sight, and the voice seemed to come from the ground. At length Deroubaix found that it came from a sewer grating to which a woman was clinging with torn and bleeding hands. The grating formed the top of a shaft leading down into the sewers, and though she was terribly injured, the woman had climbed up this shaft as her only chance of escape from the dark labyrinth underground. Blood was clotted on her head, and her head and face bore wounds apparently inflicted with a heavy blunt instrument. Her shoes were worn through at the toes and her knees and feet were bleeding from the wounds sustained by the woman as she crawled along the drains. But she would give not a word of explanation as to how she came into such a plight. She is a widow named Pughe, aged 45, who lived alone in a villa some distance from where she was found. When pressed to tell her story, she would only reply: "It is a secret which I shall keep until my death." Witnesses have told that recently they heard revolver shots near the widow's house, and it has proved that the woman herself could not have moved the heavy gratings which are the only means of access. The police are asking whether the story that Pugh refuses to tell and which may yet end in her death, for she is very seriously hurt, is not one of jealousy and vengeance.
