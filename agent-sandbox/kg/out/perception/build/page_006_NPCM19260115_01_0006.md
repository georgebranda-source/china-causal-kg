## [12] “THE OVERSEAS.”

Patriotism is the last refuge of a scoundrel, thundered Dr. Johnson. The Overseas League was not then in existence. Had it been so, the portly doctor would doubtless have had something to say concerning it. The League is about the sanest thing we know in the region of patriotism. It is a non-party society of British subjects residing in all parts of the world. Its underlying motive is to promote the unity of British subjects. Its four chief objects are:—

1. To draw together in the bond of comradeship. British people the world over; 2. To render individual service to our Empire; 3. To maintain the power of the Empire and to hold to its best traditions; 4. To help one another.

## [13] The creed of its members is:

<sub>section under [12] “THE OVERSEAS.”</sub>

Believing the British Empire to stand for justice, freedom, order and good government, we pledge ourselves, as citizens of the British Commonwealth of nations, to maintain that heritage handed down to us by our fathers.

There is nothing blatant about these objects, and the creed is simple and direct. It is the sanity which characterises the working of this great League which appeals to us. If great crimes have been done in the name of freedom and progress, they have been done no less in the name of patriotism which can become the last resort of the unthinking. The thinking part of the League is probably expressed in its excellent monthly journal—a journal which is unique, so perfectly is it edited, compiled and written. Mr. Eric Rice's visit at this particular moment is most opportune. We may not think it necessary to be told of the heritage and traditions of the Empire, but a reminder of them now and then should not come amiss. The British people here have not yet reached the ideal of comradeship which the League desires. Only when a crisis occurs do we realise our kinship and our need each of the other. The traditions of the British Empire are wrapped up in

justice, freedom, order and good government. Hongkong itself is an example of this. The reiteration of the fact can becomedtious. Facts speak for themselves, although they are not always allowed to be heard. The thought that each overseas Briton is a potential ambassador for his country, should be a chastening one. It very rarely is. Perhaps it is not looked at in that light. Nor in the light of the fact that in what we may say and do is the Empire and its members judged. The evil that men do lives after them. The courtesies and righteousness of every day life are forgotten whilst the mistakes of speech and action are so readily remembered. A great heritage should produce great minds with an ever-widening outlook, sympathetic in these present days to those who, in their own way, are striving to see the light and to build firm and secure. The journal of the League reminds us that—

We sailed wherever ship could sail.

We founded many a mighty state.

The lines suggest a sort of grandiose boasting on meritorious achievements. It is the function of the Overseas League to bring us to hard facts and to make us realise the importance of the Empire. It is easy therefore to recommend membership of the League to those who regard the British Empire in the light of the wording of the already quoted Creed.

## [15] Master and Man.

It was only to be expected that the proceedings at the Coal Commission would be fruitful to some extent of recriminations by masters and men. Mr. Evan Williams, President of the Mining Association of Great Britain, for instance, complained of the high percentage of absenteeism among the miners—particularly in England and Wales, whilst he strenuously denied that the miners were receiving miserable wages. Mr. Evan Williams is apparently no disciple of Lord Birkenhead, who appealed at a public dinner last month for the "New Spirit in Industry," stating that to his mind nothing was more touching or more delightful than when one found reminders that even in the days of industrial claims there was maintained an element of the old-fashioned conception of the relations between masters and men, that the sufferings of the one were regarded as the concern of the other. His own conception of an ideal business organisation was that it should be an organisation of all concerned—those who provided the capital, those who produced, and those who bought and sold—for their good and also for the good of the community. He believed that the average employer—or, indeed, nine employers out of ten, and nine employees out of ten — were just "ordinary average human Christians." He believed that "if we could only find some means of applying that average ordinary human Christian spirit to our industrial life there would be an end of all this 'war talk" as between masters and men. Evidently, the type of man to convert to what he described as a 'quixotic' effort to introduce the Locarno spirit in industry are the Evan Williams's of the employing world, who can see only the more in the men's eye and not the beam in the masters. The extremists are not all on the side of the wage earners. For the impression of a

"natural vendetta" between employer and employee, which Lord Birkenhead deplored, and for the deviation from the paths of rectitude in the industrial world, the men are not alone accountable.

## [19] FIRE FIGHTING.

**INTERESTING LOCAL TESTS**

**CONVINCING DEMONSTRATION.**

A novel demonstration of fire extinguishing took place on the Hongkong Cricket Club ground yesterday evening for the purpose of proving the efficacy of extinguishers.

The first performance was that of Jardine's "Fire Suds" extinguisher. A pan was filled with packing shavings and firewood, and saturated with petrol. It was then set alight and two men played the fire suds on it. The blaze was extinguished in a minute and a half.
Mustard and Co.'s extinguisher was Phomene, "Foam Type" capacity 2 gallons. The pan in which the packing shavings, wood, and petrol were put, was double the size of the other. The blaze was extinguished in 1min.85secs. A large number of spectators watched the de-onstrations.

## [22] TEXTILE INDUSTRY AMONG THE ANCIENTS.

At the dawn of history, wool, flax, cotton, and silk were being woven in the East with the greatest skill, and which was the first material used in weaving is not known. It is probable, however, that the possession of flocks and herds led to the spinning and weaving of wool before either cotton, flax or silk was so used; and the fact that here and there ancient records speak of fabrics of cotton and silk as if they were rare luxuries would indicate that linen and woollen fabrics were too common to receive much attention, and that those of the other materials were relatively novel.

The earliest ancient history describes Eastern nations as having already attained a high degree of skill, not only in the spinning and weaving of fabrics, but in their dyeing and ornamentation. On the walls of Nineveh, Babylon, Thebes, and the ancient cities of Peru and Mexico, throughout most of the ruins of Assyria, Persia, Egypt, and among similar ruins of both North and South America, is depicted the whole process of the textile industry, from the raising of the sheep or growing of the flax to the spinning of the yarn and weaving of the fabrics.

The Bible in Genesis and Exodus refers to the art, Homer, Herodotus, Confucius, and Pliny, all relate traditions of how and when it originated. It is a fact established by thousands of hieroglyphics and confirmed by the oldest of Eastern historians that the Chinese, Hindus, Assyrians, Babylonians, Persians, Egyptians, and Hebrews practised spinning and weaving with great skill at a very remote period.
One Chinese tradition is that silk weaving was practised in Kiang Nan, China, in 2640 B.C. Another is that cotton originated in India, and that shawls and carpets were first woven in Parala. Fabrics of wonderful excellence were wrought by the Egyptians certainly twenty-five hundred years before Christ, and probably three thousand. At this early period the dwellers along the Nile wove linen cloth of a fineness that is still unequalled.....

It is said that the Egyptians put a shuttle in the hands of their goddess Isis to signify she was the inventress of weaving. Joseph in Genesis about 1600 B.C. records that Pharaoh "arrayed him in vestures of fine linen". Another early reference to weaving is in the Bible (Leviticus xiii 47-59), which speaks of the warp and woof of woollen and linen garments.....and shows that in 1500 B.C. the Israelites knew the art.....Fabrics of many textures and degrees of fineness were commonly used by the Egyptians for clothing, draperies, banners, and for many ceremonial uses. Wool, flax, and cotton were all known and used by the Egyptians.....and the process of dyeling was well established.

According to Pliny, the Assyrians believed Queen Semiramis invented weaving, although he gives the honour of the invention to the Egyptians. Among the Greeks Minerva is shown with a distaff, and is recognised as having first taught man the art. Again, the Mohammedans say that the art originated with a son of Japhet; while the Peruvians point to Mama Ocllo, wife of Manco Capac, their first sovereign, as the originator. The absence of any authentic written records means unmistakably that it was prehistoric among different peoples about the same time.
