ASSETS TO THE COLONY.

The Hongkong Government's grants to unemployed officers of the British Mercantile Marine are touched upon in the latest issue of Shipping and Engineering (Shanghai). Locally, of course, it is known that the grants offered were the outcome of a Board of

investigation into cases of hardship occasioned through the strike and the boycott. Amongst those most concerned there has been no great rush to participate in the grant. Many have been idle for months; their little capital has been well eaten into—in some cases of married officers doubtless melted away by now—but they shrink from what some are inclined to look upon as a dole; that hateful word begotten of the unemployed problem at Home. It has never been intended that the grants here should smack either of doles or charity; and it is a great pity that such an unfortunate misconception ever arose. As our Shanghai contemporary rightly puts it:

It should not be forgotten that these officers are actually an asset to the Colony (we are referring entirely to those who have been thrown out of employment through the boycott and whose services, if the boycott were fully lifted, would be in immediate demand for the benefit of the Colony's trade), and that the Hongkong Government, in deciding to make them grants from public funds, is doing nothing more than is just. These men are respectable members of an honourable profession, many of them having families to support, and it was an obvious necessity, if their services were to be kept available in the event of a full resumption of shipping services, that the Government should take steps to keep them "going."

The grants range from two dollars a day in the case of single men to a maximum of five dollars in the case of married men, due regard being paid to the circumstances of each particular case. Were these grants—not too generous by any means—to be taken advantage of by all (and not merely a few) whom they are designed to aid in the Colony's own interests and also paid promptly, the community would feel that the Government was doing something of which to be justly proud. But—and why there should always be a but we know not—our Shanghai contemporary refers to a local report of delays in paying out grants already passed by the Board of Investigation. It continues:—
From private information which we have received from Hongkong the regrettable and unnecessary delay in making payment may be placed wholly at the doors of the Hongkong Treasury. Department. Whilst it has come generally to be admitted that only in the most exceptional circumstances will any Department of any Government do anything quickly and in a business-like manner, it is submitted that the present circumstances are sufficiently exceptional, and the need of monetary relief by those affect

ed sufficiently acute, to warrant even a Treasury Department departing from precedent and practice and producing without delay money the payment of which has been approved by the competent authorities, and the need of which is so urgent to the grantees. Surely the Treasury Department of the Hong Kong Government is not so overburdened with labour that it takes weeks to issue money for grants to some of the Colony's best non-official public servants. If it is, then some other method should be devised, and quickly, whereby the payments approved by the Grants Committee will reach those for whom they are intended in a reasonable time.

Our northern contemporary has been quoted so fully not with any desire to "fling mud" at the Hongkong Government, but to show the great amount of interest taken in the subject of these grants outside this Colony. When a journal of the standing of Shipping and Engineering gives prominence to an alleged grievance of this nature—even though it rests on the usually flimsy base of private information—it is time for something to be done. Either the allegation of undue delays in paying out these grants is beside the mark (in which event they can be officially denied) or there is some foundation for it (in which event the local authorities should be trusted to remedy the defect immediately). The good name of the Colony is at stake. And the community requires official enlightenment of the true position of affairs!

The initial difficulty in following Chinese militarism is the number of leaders who figure in the strife they create and recreate. A more important one is the difficulty foreigners find in seeing reason in all that is done in the cause of China's reunification. Reunification, it might be said what sins are committed in thy name! Marshal Feng Yu-hsiang will go down in Chinese history as "the Christian General". His "braves" have been known to sing hymns — they have probably entered into "battle" with the tune of "Onward Christian Soldiers" ringing in their ears—while a Methodist clergyman is said to have been engaged to minister to the spiritual wants of the General's troops. Feng's publicity department is beyond criticism. Like consistent, well-arranged advertisements, he sticks in the memory. His exploits—Christian and otherwise—are always likely to receive first consideration. His latest intention is to pawn—by pawn we mean put in safe keeping—his Marshal's baton, and lie him, not to a nunnery, as the poet would have it, but to a Moscow factory, there to study politico-economic conditions. This must be an ideal factory for workmen—unless workmen would rather work than study. And Feng is going to use the result of his study on his return to his native land. The change, he says, "does not disturb me." All this savours of a nice little holiday. How the knowledge acquired in Russia of economic conditions there, is going to benefit conditions in China, is not quite apparent. Some of China's troubles—economic and otherwise—are of her own seeking. And these troubles include militarists—Christian or otherwise. Perhaps the good marshal seeks to become China's Mussolini. We must hope for the best.

## [20] THE FREEDOM OF THE PRESS.

It has seemed good to the Foreign Office to collect and lay before us a mass of information concerning the freedom of the Press in other lands. Whether we are intended to "read and envy and pine and die," or to give thanks that we are not as others are, is not clear. There is much variety in the restrictions placed upon writers, from the extremity of despotism in Russia, where "each manuscript, before being set up in type, must be passed by" the State Publishing Department, to the untrammelled Liberty of Abyssinia, where "there is no legislation specially affecting the Press." In China it is forbidden to publish not only misrepresentations of the Government, but "any attack upon the private character of an individual which tends to injure his reputation." Whether the attack is justified or not seems to be of no importance in China. In Hungary, it's a newspaper, publishes "other openly or furtively untrue facts or true facts in a distorted form about anybody, the editor has to publish in the next number such rectification" as the offended party chooses to supply, provided that it does not run to greater length than the attack. Whether the offended one is the judge of the truth and dis

tortion of the facts does not appear. In Greece there are particular regulations about editors which we are bound to call unkind, (observes a London daily paper). An editor must be able to prove that he "has been taught at least ancient Greek by some teacher," he must have some unmortgaged landed property, he must not have been condemned even once "for any crime though the law will wink at three convictions" for an offence connected with the Press." But the most unkindest cut of all are the specifications that your editor must be, first, physically capable of his duties, and, secondly, responsible for his duties. The most uncomfortable amendment to the law of libel is suggested by the Netherlands. There the embarrassed publicist can libel not only the living, but the dead. Let him write of the deceased anything which if it were written of the living "would be regarded as defamation or libel," and fine and imprisonment are the penalty. This must gravely restrict the publication of the diaries and memoirs highly spliced with the scandal of yesterday, such as was delight in. "De mortuis ill niss bonum" is an excellent principle, but it seems stern to enforce it by law.
