## [9] COMMERCE AND FINANCE.

**RESEARCH RESULTS**

The Belfast linen trade is experiencing severe depression, but the results achieved by the Linen Research Association are regarded as so hopeful that it has been decided to prolong the society's five years' probationary period for another five years. It has been recommended by the Committee of Inspection that an upkeep of £15,000 per year will henceforth be necessary; and the Scientific and Industrial Research Department will make a grant till 1929 of £21,000, the Northern Ireland Government will donate £10,000, leaving £35,000 to be subscribed by the Irish, Scotch, and English linen manufacturing concerns forming the Association.

A plentiful and cheap supply of raw material is essential to every industry, and in its development of the "J.WS," pedigree flux seed, which promises a 200 per cent greater and better spinning fibre product, the Linen Research Association has probably fulfilled its greatest achievement. From a single pickle of this seed, evolved by minute selection and breeding, Dr. J. Vargas Eyre, Director of Research at the Linen Institute, has been able to report a bulk now available of 20 tons. Continental linen activity now tends to make hopes of future foreign flax supplies both costly and precarious. Simultaneously the acreage under flax in Northern Ireland has dwindled during the last 30 or 40 years from 200,000 acres to 40,000. Happily the work carried on at the Research Institute, near Belfast, gives every hope that flax growing will in future be more remunerative to the home farmer, and this is imperative if "cultivation is to be maintained at an adequate level.

## [11] THE RUBBER POSITION.

**SOME UNCOMFORTABLE**

It may confidently be expected that, for the quarter beginning on May 1, the amount of rubber exportable from the East Indies under the Stevenson Scheme will be raised to 65 per cent of the standard output, the increase of 10 per cent being the maximum that can be made in any quarter. Meanwhile the rubber situation has one or two uncomfortable aspects. Stocks in London have declined continuously, for some months, and it is doubtful whether even now the price of rubber, which has certainly increased steadily during the current quarter, adequately reflects the strong statistical position of the commodity: this can perhaps be attributed in part to the hand-to-mouth buying policy adopted by American consumers. It is certain, however, that consumption has increased and is increasing, and there is now a strong demand for "spot" and "near" rubber. In some quarters there are fears that a shortage will develop within a few months, but the restriction scheme is not so rigid as to preclude adjustment in face of such a contingency. If fears of a scarcity become acute, prices will rise to such an extent that it will be profitable for producers to pay the extra tax levied on exports above the prevailing quota, though necessarily it would take some time for these increased supplies to be forthcoming. With the price of rubber at its present level, however, and with the prospects of a good demand, it would appear that the time is propitious for considering the abandonment of the scheme, now that it has served its purpose. Probably the existence of the scheme has induced a cautious attitude in the minds of investors, so that the shares of rubber plantations have not benefited as much as might have been expected from the state of the commodity market. It appears that the restriction of exports has increased not only the overhead expenses of the plantations but also the working costs per pound, and, in consequence, the financial condition of most of the companies has not improved very greatly. This perhaps may account for the disappointing condition of the market for rubber shares at the present time.

## [12] INSURANCE NOTES.

**LEG L AND GENERAL**

Although the past year was an uneventful one for the Legal and General Assurance Society, yet it marked a real and solid step in the progress of the affairs of this very efficiently controlled office. In the Life department the number of policies issued during the twelve months was 5,308, compared with 5,141 in the previous period, and the gross sums assured were thus brought up from £2,135,868 to £3,506,717, and new premiums from £157,177 to £201,362. Deferred annuities were granted for £35,137 per annum. In contrast with £30,085 per annum for 1923. After deducting the amount of business placed with re-insurance offices, the total sum assured amounted to £3,087,317, compared with £2,941,868, while new premiums totalled £189,863, compared with £150,056. Although the total of net assurances is not a record, it compares very favourably with that of 1919, which was the boom year, while as regards the gross figures, they constituted a record, with the exception of 1909.

## [17] P. I. LUMBER.

**QUALITY SAID TO BE OBJECTIONABLE,**

The lumber industry in the Philippine Islands is threatened by numerous complaints received by local dealors from buyers of Philippine wood in foreign countries. For the last few months, the quality of Philippine lumber had been considerably reduced by the destructive insects which attack the wood prepared for shipment abroad.

The precarious state of Philippine wood may be attributed to the lack of sufficient inspection, it is said. Because of the absence of enough personnel, the forest bureau cannot assign inspectors to examine every piece of lumber shipped to other countries. In many instances, there are lumber shipments which were never inspected at all, it was declared.

The situation explains the decline in the popularity of Philippine wood in some countries. Australian lumber, manufacturers recently returned a consignment of lumber to Manila because the quality is so poor that it cannot be sold in the market.

Director Fischer of the bureau of forestry declared that unless the government set aside an increase in the appropriation for the services of more lumber inspectors little or nothing would be accomplished to improve the lumber production.

The Philippine lumber prices had been considerably high in the last few years. The revenue entering the government from the sale of wood could be doubled if the yield is only improved. It seems, however, said one dealer, that there will be a slump in the lumber market if the yield should remain unimproved.

Another important factor which must be considered, it is pointed out, is the safeguarding of the prestige the Philippine wood has in most foreign markets. In China, Japan, and in the United States, the reputation of Philippine wood is high.
