## [1] NAVAL YARD FETE.

**SATURDAY'S ENJOYABLE OUTING.**

**POMPEY V. FLYMOUTH**

### 윤리적경영

A large assembly of the members, their wives, children and friends, of the Naval Yard Recreation Club, gathered together at Stonecutters on Saturday afternoon to celebrate their annual sports.

The programme of events had been nicely arranged, and the Gala spirit was very much in evidence. 'The "Tamur's" Bungalow was kindly loaned, and this handy little shack, with attachments, served as a Ten House, Bar and ladies' dressing room. By kind permission of the Captain and olliers of H.M.S. "Hermes," the ship's splendid band was in attendance, and discoursed some lively selections at intervals. Much amusement was caused by the eccentricities of Messrs. Jones, Grant, Blight, Spanton, Griffiths, Sutter, and "Splinter" Phillips, who were all dolled up in dazzling original costumes. Amateur photographers had plenty of opportunity. Although it was a very hot afternoon, considerable interest was evoked in the various handicaps put up by Mr. A. Smith (serving in chief) and his colleagues.

## [5] KNIVES, DRUGS, ETC.

Mr. Pecy Hutchison's new production: "The Man from Hong Kong," announced for July 81 at the Queen's Theatre, London and postponed until August. Bank Holiday owing to last-minute alternations, has had a very mixed reception. The Bank Holiday crowd enjoyed it, but were not struck, while the critics, one and all, have little good to say about it. One describes "The Man from Hong Kong" as containing material which might have thrilled readers of serials or cinema-goers, but "as a play it only succeeded in making the restive audience laugh in the wrong places." Another says: "The play is quite well built, but the dialogue is banal in the extreme. Not for many months have such mirth provoking sentimentalities been spoken on the London stage." The writing of the play has been done with a special delight in pathos, and on this point one of the critics observes: "There are a number of lines which could not be accepted without a snigger. They might have gone down in old-fashioned Lyceum drama, but not to-day." All of this will, of course, account for the fact that Sir Alfred Butt expressed the opinion that the play should not be produced.

"The Times" gives the following account of the first night's production: — Bramley Graydon's house-boat certainly is exceedingly Chinese, with its decorated ceiling, its twinkling lamps, its slinking servant, and its profusion of cushions. Vivien might have guessed what she was in for as soon as she saw the divan and heard that Bramley was not taking her to London to dine with her mother. But, of course, she did not know what we did of Bramley's dark past. She hadn't an idea that he was a half-Chinese, or that his name was Ti Tong, or that he was a trafficker in cocaine. She had been thousands of miles away on that evening years ago when we saw him murder an Englishman and learned much of his licentious ways, and rejoiced to see such a bulldog as Lieutenant Tony Travers, R.N., vow to be avenged upon him. So she sat down to Chinese dinner and did not become seriously alarmed for her safety until Bramley began to eat with chopsticks.

When she did try to escape, the courteous Bramley promptly revealed the villanious nature of Li Tong. She returned to her mother's lungalow? Never in this world. There, behind the divan, was an cupcions chest. Into it she should go, and that very night be borne away to Timelouse. You trouble, or are supposed to tremble, for her fate, knowing that Tony has smoked one of Li Tong's drugged cigarettes, and is gloriously unlikely to arrive in time. However, after the usual Oriental passion and Occidental protests, she wisely faints and Tony bursts in full of heroic guile. Even then it might have gone ill with them if the Thames Conservancy had not been in reserve.

Knives, drugs, lamps and pistols; locked doors, panic-stricken innocence, and ruscally fellows behind screens and curtains—here they all are, with a couple of naval officers thrown in. It ought to be thrillingly romantic. In fact, it is at times a rather embarrassing joke. It seems heartless to laugh when Tony's voice is shaking with emotion or the final touch is being given to Vivien's terror by the appearance of chopsticks, but laughter is the best way out. Miss Mary Merrill makes the most of Vivien's innocent misfortunes, and Mr Percy Hutchison, as the naval officer, is prodigiously marine. It is not possible, however, with the best melodramatic will in the world, to take their troubles seriously, with the result that at the full of the curtain the house as audibly, but good-humouredly, divided in its opinion.

## [8] CHINISSE AGAINST BOYCOTT.

*(Neuter's Service.)*

*London, Sept. 6.*

It is understood that Sir Ronald Macleay is likely to depart from London to Peking shortly in order to resume his duties in connection with the conference on the Chinese customs, at which he will be supported by fiscal experts, one of whom, the "Daily Telegraph's" diplomatic correspondent says, is of considerable professional eminence.

Sir John Jordan, in a letter to the “Times,” welcomes Mr. Kellogg’s declaration of policy as regards China, but asks why the conference of the Powers on October 26 meets at Peking. He says the conference has a unique opportunity of appealing to all sections of the Chinese nation and therefore to achieve any prospect of success it must meet at Shanghai or some other neutral centre, where the provinces may take an active and untrammelled part in the negotiations.

The “Times,” in an editorial, says practical sympathy with the Chinese, collaboration with friends of law and order and adaptability to the changing conditions will give the keynote to British policy in China. Progress along the road to reform will be checked not by British reluctance but by Bolshevik intrigues and Chinese chaos. The fact that American policy accords with the British is shown by Mr. Kellogg's speech.

Special Committee.

Peking, September 5. — The Cabinet decided to issue a mandate this evening appointing twelve members as a special committee connected with the Tariff Conference.

"British Are a Just People." Peking, September 5.—Tsai Ting-kan, in a letter to the "Peking Leader," urges the dropping of the anti-British agitation and says, inter alia: "I have always maintained that the British are a just people. The joint resolution of the British Chamber of Commerce and the China Branch of the British Association of Shanghai is a splendid gesture, proving that the British will meet us more than half way."

Mr. Tsai Ting-kan quotes Mr. Chamberlain's reply to Mr. Chu's note concerning the judicial enquiry. He says that with the joint resolution of the British at Shanghai and Mr. Chamberlain's replies, "We are justified in changing to a new front. With the coming of the conference before us, we should meet with a smile and a warm handshake."

## [9] REGULAR RUNS.

**STEAMBOAT COMPANY'S DEIERMINATION.**

**"HONAM" AND CANTON**

The s.s. "Honam," which returned yesterday from Canton, was again unable to discharge its cargo there on account of inability to obtain lighters.

Passengers who went up from Hongkong were able to land, but few desirous of returning to Hongkong were able to elude the strike pickets and sampun owners at Canton have evidently been threatened with severe measures if they convey anyone to the ship. Thirty-five Chinese managed to give the pickets the slip and board the ship.

The Steambont Company have announced their determination to maintain a regular service between Hongkong and Canton.

Another sign of the gradual return to normal times is the announcement that the sale of cheap rice to relieve the poor will be discontinued by the Kwong Wah Hospital, Yaumati's leading Chinese institution of philanthropy.

According to the vernsacular papers, strict orders have been issued to police officers in regard to possession of toy platols. It is understood that the sale, manufacture or keeping will be subject to regulation.

## [11] MOROCCO FIGHTING.

*Paris, Sept. 5*

A message from Fez says that French and Spanish squadrons and air forces have been bombarding Alhuemas for the past three days; but there is nothing to indicate a landing has been effected. There has been no action on the French Northern front indicating a beginning of the Franco-Spanish offensive yet.
Madrid, Sept. 6.

A Communiqué from Morocco states that fighting is general all day long on the western front in the Tetuan sector and is especially fierce in Benihosmar Massif. The enemy pressed their attacks with the greatest determination. Troops destined for our main operations are concentrated along the front.

*Paris, Sept. 6.*

Despatches: from Fez tell of enemy activity increasing on both fronts. The Garrison at Ainmaluf has been relieved. An American air, squadron co-operated in the effective bombardment of Sheshuan.

## [12] AIRSHIP DISASTER.

*(Reuter's American Service.)*

*Washington, Sept. 5*

Mr. Wilbar declares that it is purely a budget question whether another dirigible will be built to replace the “Shenandoah.” The disaster would not deter the Navy Department pursuing its lighter than air programme.

Swampscott, Sept. 6.
President Coolidge has expressed the assumption that the Navy Department would want to replace the "Shenandoah" by another dirigible usable for military purposes. He was of the opinion that the loss of an airship did not differ from the loss of a warship, which invariably resulted in plans for replacement.

Caldwell, Ohio, Sept. 5.
The barograph and records of the “Shenandoah,” which the Navy Department officials expected would aid them in determining the cause of the disaster, were found here in the possession of souvenir hunters.

## [13] AVIATION OUTBURST

*(Reuter's American Service.)*

Colonel Williams Mitchell, formerly assistant Chief of the Air Services, has issued a statement "after mature deliberation," charging the War and Navy Departments with "incompetency, and criminal negligence, amounting almost to treasonable administration of national defence." He says that all aviation policies are directed by non-flying officers, knowing nothing about flying and that the "lives of airmen are being used merely as pawns in their hands," the airmen themselves being bluffed so that they dare not tell the truth." Mr. Mitchell deplores the fact that weather bulletins are not available to airmen, although issued "for the benefit of onions and cabbages."
