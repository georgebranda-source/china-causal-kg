## [11] COMMERCE AND FINANCE

**CUNARD LINE.**

**AMERICAN EXPRESS SERVICE.**

The experiment of the Cunard Line of so arranging their timetable that the incoming mail-ships can call at Plymouth, Cherbourg, and Southampton all in the one day certainly seems a success as far as it has gone, judging from the first few sallings only. But this promise only holds good in the winter, when ships travelling by the short route can fit in the call's conveniently and still land their passengers in London or Paris at a reasonable hour. When they have to go farther south on account of icebergs, however, it will be found that this does not apply.

Unfortunately, it is just this summer season that the first call in England is wanted as many people complain that the Cherbourg call coming first persuades many American visitors to see France before they come on to England, with the result that they have done all their shopping in Paris and spent very much less in this country than would otherwise be the case.

In the meantime we have got n very considerably accelerated mail service between London and New York, and that is something to be grateful for. Before the war the Germans learnt that the extra half-knot could be just as troublesome as it could be advantageous.

## [13] FREIGHT FALL

The general level of British shipping freights fell in March to the lowest point touched since August, 1923, according to the Chamber of Shipping index number published by "The Statist." The March index, at 26.08, is 935 per cent lower than for February, and 1886 per cent lower than for March, 1924. The time charter rate, in which a fall of 4.37 per cent was recorded in February, fell another 1.56 per cent in March. Freights are now virtually at the pre-war level, as if the average for 1913 is taken at 100, the average for March, 1925, is 105.5. Virtually all routes contributed to the March decline, the heaviest falls occurring in the Red Sea, Arabia, and India group, Java to United Kingdom-Continent, Australia to United Kingdom, Argentine, Uruguay, &c. to United Kingdom-Continent, and United States to United Kingdom groups.

## [14] IDLE SHIPPING.

Quarterly returns of the amount of shipping laid up Idle at the principal ports of Great Britain and Ireland on April 1, compiled by the Chamber of Shipping, show that as compared with January 1, last, there was a decrease of 95,190 tons net, or 19.5 percent; and compared with a year ago a decrease of 17,303 tons, or 42 per cent Theports at which there have been the largest decreases as compared with the beginning of the year are: Bristol, with a decline of 12,000 tons; Cardiff, 13,000 tons; Liverpool, 46,200 tons; London Docks, 36,800 tons; and Glasgow, 14,500 tons. On the other hand, increases are recorded at Dartmouth, 4,000 tons; Hull, 6,000 tons; London River, 7,000 tons; Manchester, 6,000 tons; Newcastle, 13,000 tons; and Southampton, 8,750 tons. The total for English ports has decreased by 63,800 tons net; that for Scottish ports by 25,600 tons; and that for Irish ports by 5,800 tons net.

## [16] COTTON.

American cotton "prices have been unsettled, but influences of weakening character have predominated at the close of the week and values have lost more ground. The report of Messrs J. F. Hutton and Co., Ltd.'s weekly statistics, shewing that the visible supply of American has decreased by 222,000 bales on the week to 2,969,000 bales against a reduction of only 82,000 bales to 1,882,000 bales last year attracted bullish support. Trade demand has also been more active. At Liverpool spot sales have increased substantially owing to a revival of Continental buying. Speculative selling has, however, proved stronger than these factors owing to generally favourable crop news. It is believed, indeed, that a fairly extensive short interest has been created. If this is the case it may prove to be a strong influence in a reversal of sentiment should either the crop outlook or trading demand. shew any unexpected development, as the present situation has already been discounted by a fall of 1½d per lb. Egyptian prices have been firmer. In the Manchester market there has been a fair volume of enquiry of a very varied nature, but the easier tendency of cotton values has effectively checked enterprise on the part of most buyers. A moderate turnover, composed almost wholly of small lines, has been arranged, but the larger lots which have been under negotiation are apparently held up pending some further indication as to the course of values. In American yarns there has been an improved demand for fine and also coarse couts; prices are irregular and ¼d to ¼d per lb. lower. For Egyptians there has been a fairly good enquiry but transactions have been disappointing and prices are 1d per lb. down. India has bought some good lots of drooties, also shirtings, light whites and miscellaneous styles, and enquiry for the finer makes has been more in evidence. For China a few shirtings and dyed goods have been sold. A moderate business has been arranged for the Near East and Africa. The Home trade, Persia and South America have also bought sundry styles.

## [18] SMYRNA CLAIMS

**INSURANCE COMPANY WINS.**

London, May 2.—The test case on which depends enormous claims against various insurance companies, brought by the American Tobacco Company against the Guardian Assurance Company, was decided in the Court of Appeal yesterday against the American Tobacco Company. The company sued to recover £168,000, the result of loss sustained in the destruction of Smyrna on September 19, 1922, when the Turks evicted the Greeks.

The insurers refused payment on the ground that the losses incurred were war risks, and not ordinary fire risks. The total Smyrna losses were estimated at £20,000,000, of which a considerable percentage was covered by fire risks, which are now held to be void. The present decision confirms the decisions already rendered by the French and Italian courts.

## [30] LIVING COSTS.

**SYMPTOMS OF INFLATION**

The cost-of-living figure, which indicates the purchasing power of our money, is shown to have fallen appreciably during the month of March. On February 25, it was 97 per cent. above pre-war; on April 1 it was 75 per cent., says the Daily Chronicle.

Though seasonal cheapening of eggs and milk counts for something in this, we are probably justified in assigning a definite influence to the raising of the Bank Rate. The justification of that criticised step was that, apart from any question of the American exchange, commodity prices, were slowly but steadily rising in this country, i.e., the purchasing power of money was falling.

"In other words, there were the initial symptoms of inflation. The raising of the Bunk Rate was calculated to correct them, and appears to have done so. But the process needs careful watching, lest it go too far. With trade as it is, we are in no position to embark on an active deflationary policy."
