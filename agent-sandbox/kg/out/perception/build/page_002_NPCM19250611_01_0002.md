## [11] COMMERCE AND FINANCE.

**CANADIAN TRADE**

### —

**CONSIDERABLE GAIN SHOWN.**

Ottawa, April 28.—Business in Canada is holding its own very well, proof of this being seen in the latest statistical material.

Bank clearings for last week were $67,000,000 higher than for the corresponding month last year, an increase of nearly 28 per cent. The increases were quite general, for 27 out of 29 cities reported them.

It is especially noteworthy that that every city in the east reporting had an increase, those in the case of Toronto and Montreal being $20,000,000 higher.

The March bulletin of the Labour Department shows the percentage of employment to have been approximately the same as during the corresponding month last year, or, if anything, slightly better.

In the manufacturing industries there has been pronounced recovery during the last month, their staffs having been increased by nearly 11,000 workers.

The largest gains were in iron and steel, notably in vehicle and rolling mills, and in lumber, clay, glass, and stone, pulp and paper and rubber works. The improvement reported a year ago was much less extensive, while on April 1, 1923, there were large reductions in personnel.

Pig Iron production during March was 69,932 long tons, or more than the combined output during the first two months of the year. The foregoing information dispels some of the gloom that some are all too prone to depict.

The International Paper Company has awarded contracts for the extensions to its plant at Three Rivers, which will bring its total capacity up to 700 tons of newsprint a day. Eight machines will be operated when the new plant is complete. When in full operation it will produce enough newsprint every minute to cover more than two acres of ground or enough every day to make nearly 4,000,000 24-page newspaper, of standard size: Wayagamack News, Ltd, has made an issue of $1,500,000 6½ per cent bonds, a portion of the proceeds to be used in the building of its 200-ton newsprint mill.

The erratic action of the grain exchanges continues to be a source of anxiety to legitimate dealers and farmers. The latter especially are complaining, for while the existing price is good, they think that it is really much below the figure warranted by the world's crop situation. Seeding operations in the west are going forward rapidly, and, indeed, in some parts the wheat is appearing above the ground.

A number of Canadian Government bond issues are being placed in New York, the most important of these, during the last week, having been that of the Province of Ontario, which sold $20,000,000 of 37½ per cent one and two-year Treasury bonds to a United States syndicate, the price being 98.65. The proceeds are for maturing issues. The sales of Canadian bonds to the United States so far this year run close to $100,000,000.

Rumours that a merger of Canadian biscuit-making firms is in sight have not been confirmed by actual developments, but the re-organisation of the oldest of these corporations, the Christie-Brown Company of Toronto-is announced with considerable infusion of American capital, which is being accompanied by the presence of three American directors on the board. An offering of $1,000,000 of preferred stock has been taken by a Canadian American syndicate.
Canadian financial opinion is much interested in the approach of sterling to par. Its strength has also been reflected in the movement of the Canadian dollar to par in New York during the last week. It is expected that the United Kingdom will soon return to the gold standard, which means that Canada will do likewise, though such action on Canada's part will not be hurried.

## [15] LABOUR BANKS.

**NEW ERA BETWEEN CAPITAL AND LABOUR.**

San Francisco, California, April 22.—The development of labour banks, conservatively operated for productive purposes only, and the increasing participation of wage earners in investments, are seen by Don D. Lescohier, associate professor of economics, University of Wisconsin, as the salients of a new alignment between capital and labour.

Speaking before the Commonwealth Club of California, Dr. Lescobier pictured the labour union as decadent, and the advent of the open shop or so-called American plan, as "indicating labour's changing attitude on collective bargaining.

Founding of the Mount Vernon Bank in Washington, D.C., in 1920, by the International Association of Machinists, started a new movement in the history of American labour, the speaker said. The Railroad Brotherhood's chain of branch banks, keyed to the main bank in Cleveland, capitalized at $28,000,000, is a subsequent development, one of many that has thrown a large part of $7,000,000,000 of wage earners' savings into labour a new modus of action. Dr. Lescohier continued:

This may sound very theoretical but it is quite the reverse, forecasting a happier relationship between employer and employee. Among patrons of the new Federation Bank in New York on the first day were 200 manufacturers, and merchants, persons who had never held a union card. They were there because a new bank had been opened at a convenient location. And these banks differ from capitalist banks only in that they are co-operatively owned by labour unions, that they will make loans to business men only for productive enterprises which give employment to manual labour, limit dividends and distribute all earnings in excess of 10 per cent. to their depositors.

## [16] MONEY BELONGS TO

**STOCKHOLDERS.**

A new theory is evolving. About thirteen-fourteenths of the bank funds of the United States, used by the banks to make loans, belong to the depositors rather than to the stockholders. The labour banks maintain that this fact entitles the depositors to a part of the profits. Not charity but justice underlies the dividend to depositors. The depositor's dividend also has proved to be good business. It has reduced depositors' interest costs on borrowed deposits and made it worth their while to trade with the labour bank.

I believe American labour is crossing the threshold of a new epoch. Wage earners, depending in the past on earnings only are now being encouraged to draw an income from invested capital. The railroad engineers have established five investment banks which is a step toward the participation of labour in industrial management and control.

No economic development in the world to-day is so full of promise as the entry of organized labour into the mobilized saving power of workmen, employed through safe and intelligent managed investment, will lift the whole relationship between capital and labour to a higher plane. It will be a bridge to better understanding on both sides. It will teach labour the problems and risks of capital; and it will teach capital more common sense in its dealings with labour.

**THE POSITION IN BRITAIN.**

The Anglo-Standard Oil entente, is being carried a stage further by the purchase of control of the British Mexican Petroleum Company by the new Standard Oil subsidiary — Pan-American Eastern Petroleum Company. The British Mexican is the fuel oil bunkering company formed in 1919 by Lord Iaverforth and other shipping interests in conjunction with Mr. Doheny, and it now controls the Agwl Petroleum Corporation, as well as a petrol distributing concern, the Redline Motor Spirit Company.

The Standard Oil of Indiana thus obtains a foothold in the British petrol market and fuel oil bunkering facilities at all the principal British ports. It is expected that it will also take over Lord Inverforth's interest in the Lago Corporation, which is now the second largest shipper of oil from Venezuela.

It should be appreciated that the Anglo-American Oil Company will have no connection with the British companies to be controlled by Standard Oil of Indiana. The Anglo-American is a subsidiary of the Standard Oil Company of New Jersey, and Standard Oil companies have a habit of working independently in the same markets.

## [20] PHILIPPINE HATS

**INDUSTRY PICKING UP.**

### —

That the Philippine hat industry is gaining a firm foothold in the foreign trade of the Islands, is seen from recent statistical report made public by the customs authorities.
For the first time in the history of the hat industry in the Islands burl hat is now exported to foreign countries. During last month 2,700 burl hats valued at P678 were exported. Straw hats manufactured in Bulacan also showed a considerable increase Statistics show that during April this year, straw hat shipments to foreign markets amounted to P866 as compared with P170 valued for April, 1924. Hats made of bamboo also made an advance, having netted a balance of P77 in favour of this year.
