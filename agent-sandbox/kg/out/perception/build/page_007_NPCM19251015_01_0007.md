## [12] "VIA SIBERIA"

One of the signs of the return to normal after the war was early considered to be the "Via Siberia" mode of travelling to the East, or the direction to this effect written on correspondence Only this week is it given as a piece of interesting news that a mail from Home arrived in a record transit of twenty days. Information of this sort was fairly common before the war, and the Via Siberia route was becoming a fairly popular one with those who either were "no sailors bold," or with whom time was of some consequence. As a mail route, the Russia way has yet to be equalled—unless it will be equalled and surpassed, as seems probable by air mail routes—for it has meant the early confirmation of cables, contracts, and orders, and has made home seem just round the corner. It is only recently that the line has again been open for traffic and there is on record the interesting experiences of those who have essayed the journey since the Soviet became responsible for the control of the line. These experiences were interesting to say the least, but not encouraging to those who contemplated a journey across Europe. The news now cabled out will effectually dampen any andour that would be

travelers had in this connection. A judge, the son of a judge, feeling the restraint of Soviet hospitality, freely acknowledged, feels called upon to recount his and his fellow-passengers' experiences during a journey home. Three times held up by the overheating of an axle, may not be a matter worth complaining about; but a considerable delay due to the work of bandits, is a reasonable cause of complaint, more so when the work of these outlaws results in the loss of lives of railway workers, and is likely to do so in the case of passengers belonging to different countries. The incidents mentioned by Mr. W. W. Grantham, K.C., point to serious facts which cannot be blinked. Russia's internal affairs are by no means settled or secure. Counter-revolutionaries appear to be carrying on a sort of guerilla warfare, and this suggests that "Red" activities now so well known under the word Bolshevism, are not generally appreciated. Nor are they, we imagine, likely to be appreciated. Thus we may still expect the removal of bolts and pins from railway-lines, more or less as a matter of course for some little time to come. The statement that the railway track and rolling stock are both in a "shocking state," suggest a laxity of control due either to indifference, or to what is probably more correct, to the fact that the Government has it hands too full as a result of its interference in things which it can be argued do not concern it in the least. The local Police Department should have just reason to be proud of their efforts when the statistics for the year come to be published as the measures taken since the commencement of the strike four months ago have resuited in a remarkable falling off in the number of serious crimes which in normal times fill the daily register. This happy result has been achieved by hard work on the part of the officials of this department and to some extent through other causes. These latter include the vlaublé assistance of special constables, the quietening effect on evildoers by the presence of the mobilised Volunteers, and the stoppage of the Canton traffic, which enabled the authorities to exercise more efficient control on Chinese passengers by ships. Added to these is the good which resulted in eliminating the lawless element by periodical raids on suspected houses and dwellings in general.

## [15] CHARMED 1492.

**A LUCKY SWEEP TICKET NUMBER.**

**MOVIE AND FORTUNE.**

Has any particular number special significance in lotteries, cash sweeps, raffles, draws, etc. purely matters of luck in which each and every number has the same chance? Some people maintain that No. 738 has a better chance of winning than say, 777. Again, the first and last number in a sweep are always thought to "not have the slightest chance of coming out." Those who went down to last Saturdays' races will be able to bear testimony that ticket No. 1 won a big prize!

At any rate a story is going around town that ticket No. 1492 in next year's Derby sweep of a local club—in which the total is likely to be very big—is going to be lucky. Like the cash sweeps of most clubs, each ticket costs $10. But ticket No. 1492 is now assessed at a value of $50. Superslittions? If you like. But there is also a coincidence. Very recently the Queen's Theatre showed a picture "Conductor 1492" featuring a well-known comedian. For some reason or other, somebody who snow the picture is alleged to have taken a special fancy to the number, hence the rise in value of the ticket. Just as there are faddists who say that a number divisible by seven or three, or the total of the digits of which is divisible by n, will win a piece of green cheese from the moon, so there will be others who find a message from the Goddess of Change in a movie title.

## [16] CLASSICAL RECITAL.

**LAST NIGHT'S GREAT SUCCESS.**

**(10)·YEAR OLD VIOLIN.**

Sheer delight was the result of the concert given last night by Messrs. Leon Jongen and J. A. Filewski. The programme opened with the exacting Kreuzer South of Beethoven, for violin and piano. The title has a Totstovan flavour; the composition is one of the great masterprices in music. Amongst the whirl and excitement of the bold and vigorous opening Prestö, the bymin-like second subject stands out. Nothing is lost of the tenderness of the Andante in the brilliant variations which follow it, whilst the turnicelle-like Finale has a wonderful appeal. Both artists played as to the manner born, and gave a silver rendering. Mendelssohn's Concerto in E minor is beautiful—the Andante and allegro movements particularly. Mr. Bilewski's technique, lightness of touch, and great interpreliative powers were shown in his rendering of this thing of sweetness. This was further shown in a cycle of four pieces including Cooperin's Chanson Louis XIII and Pavurne—to the immense liking of the audience—a Brilms Vulse and Temps brilliant Capricios. Mr. L. Jongen is not a whit behind his collegial and demonstrated the possession of great gifts in his work at the piano. Mr. Bilewski's violin (by Guernerius) is exactly 200 years old. Mr. Jongen had the satisfaction of playing on a tuneful Collard and Collard supplied by Messrs. Anderson. The audience was huge in numbers and keenly appreciative.

## [17] SHREDS AND PATCHES.

How political enemies become very human in their everyday relations is illustrated by Mr. Will Thorne, the Labour M.P., in his autobiography "My Life's Battles," which Messrs. Newnes publish at 3s. 6d.
Mr. Thorne says that he met Lord Birkenhead in a London tube station when he (Mr. Thorne) was about to set out on his wartime visit to Russia.
“Hello, Thorne,” said Lord Birkenhead (then Sir F. E. Smith). “I’ve been reading about your going to Russia as a delegate. Do you know it is very cold out there?”

Mr. Thorne replied that he only knew of what he had read of Russia.

"Oh!" came the reply. "You had better take this coat with you."

Despite the Labour member's protests, Lord Birkenhead insisted on lending his magnificent coat there and then.

Lady Astor regarded Mr. Thorne with special favour because he gave his corner seat in the house of Commons to her.

“Just after this incident,” he relates, “she presented me with two gold-cased pencils and a beautiful diamond and pearl tie-pin.

"When I was ill in Aberdeen she sent me roses from her own garden. During my wife's illness she also sent her fruit and flowers, as well as to my son when he was in hospital in Falmouth."
Mr. Thorne was later a guest at one of Lady Astor's political dinners, but he would only go on condition that he could wear his everyday clothes.

The last page of the book contains a pathetic reference to the author's late wife.

“During the 30 years of our married life,” he writes, “I had many tips and downs, all of which she shared with me. She was a thrifty and economical Chancellor of the Household Exchequer.”

"Her foresight was revealed to me in a way I never realised when, after her death, I found that she had left me £450, which she had saved, and of which I knew nothing."
