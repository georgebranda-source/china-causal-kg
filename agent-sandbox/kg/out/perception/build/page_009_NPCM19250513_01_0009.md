## [1] CORRESPONDENCE.

### Hongkong Education.

(To the Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—This “Certificated Teachers” controversy seems to get more futile every day. May I attempt to bring it back to sanity with a few points with which I think everyone will agree.

1. If a man who was not oriously unfit through his own fault started giving advice as to how to keep one's health, he would probably be discredited. So too if a man says, "I don't hold with this 'ere education,' but what I does say is...," I think we should take his opinions with considerable reserve.

2. It is dangerous to generalise. I remember suffering at the hands of a wrangler who could not explain conic sections, but that should not make me condemn wranglers as teachers. So too I know of the headmaster of a Board School who has written one of the most illuminating books on elementary literary criticism ever published, and who has obviously all the attributes of the perfect teacher. The only hope is to plump for the system that gives the most reasonable chance of success, taking things by and large.

3. I think that most local educational authorities in England would jump at the young enthusiastic Oxford honours man if they could get him at the price. It looks as though Hongkong were offering a fair wage if we can get such; or perhaps the said hypothetical enthusiast is attracted by the glamour of the East and the chance of contact with an unknown civilization—if so, good luck to him: he will certainly be more likely to teach the Chinese something than the man who "is not here for his health."

4. After all the teaching of Chinese, with their amazing educational traditions to which we have no sort of parallel in Europe, is not the same thing as drumming the three Rs into the rising generation in England. Other things being equal, I think the only hope of achieving anything out here is to have men who have "read.....not to remember.....not to criticise.....but to weigh and consider" (I quote Bacon from memory, probably inaccurately); men whose first and constant thought will be "What, if anything, have we to teach them? Is it worth while?". How far can the best in Chinese culture be combined with the best in European? Am I to aim at turning out typists or engineers or good citizens?".....and so forth and so on. I am far from saying that such a state of mind is incompatible with the holding of a certificate, but I certainly think that a long probation in educational technique in England might quite possibly be an actual handicap. No one gets into a rut sooner than a teacher if he does not keep a constant watch on himself. (No one in this controversy seems to have quoted our University, which offers the most illuminating comments possible on the points now at issue).

(To the Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—With reference to the reported trial of summer time or the daylight saving scheme by still another local firm and the arguments for and against it as given to your representative by other business houses, I think those were most sound which pointed out the great inconvenience of any alteration which was not mutually agreed to by other ports in this zone both from the point of view of actual shipping and keeping in touch with each other's market. Again, the Bank, as another pointed out, would be a big stumbling block unless the new hours were adopted owing to certain work being impossible to proceed with until the exchange lists are in.

There is another way of looking at it, too. "Who" seriously thinks that the taipan will adhere strictly to the new hours? He works late at night often enough or his own sweet will and is it to be expected that he will be up again at some unearthly hour in the morning?—and if the taipan is not to time in the morning well—there is still a little truth in the adag when the cat's away the mice'll play." No, this early in the morning stunt may be alright for the A.P.C. line of business which can be closed down at 4:30 promptly but it certainly wouldn't be for me or any employee of a firm which has anything to do with Chinese brokers. It would take an earthquake or a stick of dynamite to move the Chinese brokers engaged in the piece goods trade, for instance, and in my case getting earlier to the office would be of no avail whatever.

To the Editor of the China Mail).

## [12] ARUS EVIL

**A GENEVA DEBATE.**

*LONDON, May 12.*

The Romanian, Polish and Persian delegates in particular displayed much anxiety during the important discussion of the general committee of the arms conference with regard to the obligation of giving publicity concerning supplies of arms.

They contended they should be exempted from affording information as long as "neighbouring country, meaning Russia," was not party to the proposed convention.
Mr. Burton declared the United States in all circumstances would give the fullest publicity to arms shipments. The United States Government and people would be very disappointed if the convention did not sufficiently provide for publicity.

The technical committee of the arms conference has adopted the British proposal to exclude warships and aeroplanes from the category of objects of which the export is not allowed. The decision must be ratified by the general committee. The legal committee pronounced in favour of the prohibition of the export of arms etc., to private individuals, but favoured its being allowed case of governments under certain conditions.

## [13] HOW AMERICA STANDS.

Official circles believe that the action of the American delegation at the Geneva arms conference in supporting British motion to exclude warship and aircraft from the operation of the proposed arms treaty, represents a desire to facilitate the conference reaching an agreement rather than any particular attitude of the Washington government on the question immediately involved. It is understood that the delegation did not discuss the status of aeroplanes and warships prior to their departure for Geneva.

## [16] THAT PEACE PACT.

**"PROFOUNDLY PACIFIC."**

*(Reutor's Service.)*

*PARIS, May 12.*

The French viewpoint regarding Germany's guarantee pact proposals is embodied in a draft Note to Germany already handed to the British and Belgian Governments.

## [19] EARLIER CABLES!

While the bare announcement that the State Department is informally negotiating with France regarding the French debt is the only official information available, it is generally understood that increased pressure for the French settlement of the obligation was recently exerted in several informal ways. It appears that the French Ministers who recently conferred with the Under Secretary of the Treasury, Mr. Winston Churchill, on his visit to France, were clearly informed by the American government that it is desired that the French funding commission should be sent to Washington at the earliest moment in order to discuss the debt settlement with an American committee headed by Mr. Mellon at Washington. The Government, anyhow, does not favour the idea that the French debt to America should depend on the sums France receives from Germany.
