## [12] HOLLAND'S OUTLOOK

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*The Hague, Sept. 15.*

In her speech from the throne at the opening of the new session of the States General, the Queen declared that in many respects the country had reason to be grateful as the position of agriculture and horticulture was generally satisfactory, though the future outlook was not so bright.
Commerce and industry were still beset with difficulties largely owing to economic conditions outside Holland. The general situation was somewhat improved though there was still ground for anxiety.

The danger disturbing the State's financial resources was past. Thanks to the radical measures adopted the financial situation continued to develop favourably and a reduction of burdens might shortly be considered, but the most sober administration of the State's finances was still imperative.

Holland's relations with foreign Powers were most cordial and efforts would be immediately made to gradually reduce Holland's military forces compatibly with the international situation. Measures were being considered for the establishment of a Department of National Defence to which the interests of the army and navy would be entrusted so far as it did not concern the Colonies Department.
As regards the colonies the influx of capital was essential to meet this growing needs of the people, but care must be taken that the interests of the native populations were not adversely affected.

## [13] HISTORIC JERBA

The discovery of a submerged city in the hoard channel separating Jerbin from the miniland of Africa is likely to make the majority of readers inquire—where is Jerbin? It is certainly one of the least known of the islands of the Mediterranean, and very few Englishmen have ever set foot upon it, writes Major Gordon Home in the "Daily Telegraph."

I was there for some time when I was endeavouring to reach Tripoli by means of coasting vessels and Arab feluncens, and although I reached the low sandy island without great difficulty, to leave it was by no means easy unless one chartered a native sailing boat and ran all sorts of chances of being becalmed off a most inhospitable shore or storm-bound in some equally deserted spot.

On the shores of Jerbin near the place where the city has been found are the remains of a Roman port known as Meninx. No excavation has yet been carried out, but finely-scriptured Roman capitals and here and there bases of columns are to be seen placed casually among the handful of "low houses forming the modern village of El Kantam. This name is the Arabic for "The Bridge." for it was here that the Roman Cuusway, of which some remains exist to-day, crossed the shallow strait. Only in the centre of the channel is there any depth of water, for on the three occasions when I have sailed to or from the island I noticed that the boat was often churning up the sand with its keel, and the bottom is visible almost everywhere.

It is therefore clear that a very slight subsidence of the coast would place the lower sandy shore below the level of the sea. That this sinking of the coast has affected the shores of Africa between Cape Bon (north of Tunis) and the Gulf of Gubes, in which Jerba is situated, is fairly plain from the fact that a portion of ancient Carthage is now submerged. And such an alteration in levels is not in any way surprising when one remembers the seismic energy which has throughout the whole historic period caused frequent disturbance in this central portion of the Mediterranean. As recently as 1891 a submarine volcano produced an island (known as Graham's Island) between Sicily and the African coast. Previously there had been a depth of 100 fathoms at the spot, and as the peak rose 200ft. above the sea a cone of volcanic debris some 800ft. in height had been produced. The action of the sea soon reduced the island to a rest, and even that has disappeared, but that such violent disturbances occur is ample explanation for the slight subsidence of the adjoining coast.

The question of the age of this submerged city is one which a close examination of the spot may settle. That it is of great antiquity is probable, for a number of reasons. Jerba has preserved its present name certainly since the time of Aurelius Victor, who wrote about A.D. 370, and it may have been ancient then. Very few names, particularly of islands, changed during the Gracco-Roman period. Then there is the name of Meninx borne by the Roman town known to have existed at this spot, traces of which, as just mentioned, are still to be seen on the island. It is a name suggesting great possibilities of exceptional antiquity, and does not appear to be Greek or Latin in form. It may be Phoenician, but there is some possibility of its being of Minoan origin, and if so the sea may give up long-buried secrets of a colony of this very early civilization. The fact that a vessel stated to be of Grecian origin has been found lying across one of the streets of the city suggests that the earth movement which caused the submergence occurred before that galley with its cargo of vases was sunk. Jerba has always been associated with the land of the Lotos-Eaters, and readers of Homer will recall the lines of the Odyssey.

Nine days by cruel storms I thence was borne

Athwart the fishy deep, but on the tenth

Reach'd the Isotrophagi, a race sustain'd

On sweetest fruit alone.
It is quite possible that the wearied mariners were given dates or figs. Certainly the island today is in remarkable contrast to the arid mainland, for it is covered with dats of palms, olives, and other fruit-bearing trees, and if it were equally well-cultivated by its inhabitants in 800 B.C. it must have been a green and pleasant shore that the smallers of the Odyssey would have been loath to leave.
Pottery of a very ancient type is still produced in Jerus. I have watched the walking of vescalis so closely resembling Roman amphores that the feeling of having travelled back in time some 2,000 years becomes almost overwhelming. This pottery is carried in fluocess glory the North African coal, where it is greatly in demand.

## [20] CANNIRALS SCARED.

**EFFECT OF BRIGHT LIGHT A CIG**

**EXPLORE R'S BLOODLISS VITOTUY:**

Capt. G. F. Wilkins, leader of the British Museum expedition, which has been two years in Australia, has returned to London. He told a "Daily Mail" reporter how he was able to gain the confidence of cannibal natives in the North-Eastern Territory who had always been hostile to white men.

Captain Wilkins stated that when a party of natives attacked his camp one night he went out carrying a bright light, and the attackers, afraid to throw their spears, disappeared into the bush.

His own native helpers were afraid to go to sleep, and they spent the night telling him of their exploits. In the morning they realised that they had confessed to a great number of crimes, and they no doubt considered that the only way to secure his silence was to tell him other and more dreadful things and take him completely into their confidence.

From these people, he said, he learned a great deal of native lore and customs and this enabled him later to approach the even more aggressive natives of Groote Hyland, who had never before allowed any white man to visit their camp.

Thousands of specimens collected by the expedition will eventually be shown in the British Museum.

## [21] LEGACY OF ERRORS.

**MISPRINTS THAT PERSIST IN SHAKESPEARE.**

For some years past it has been increasingly recognised in England that anyone who sets out to edit or annotate Elizabethan or Jacobean plays without an adequate knowledge of the handwritings of the period is not likely to do much to improve his author's text. Dramatic texts, including Shakespeare's, despite all the work which has been done on them during the last two centuries, still stand in need of further corrections, because while in other forms of literature many, perhaps most, authors corrected their proofs, most dramatists did not. The main reason for this was, doubtless, that as a rule they had no pecuniary interest in printed editions of their plays, all the rights (as far as the extent evidence shows) having passed to the companies of actors for which the plays were written. Proof-reading in those days, moreover, seems to have involved frequent attendance at the printing house; and for an actor-dramatist, with performances in the afternoon and rehearsals (presumably) in the morning, this would have been especially inconvenient. The blame cast on the printers for the misprints which resulted from their being left to do the best they could is thus largely unjust; but the misprints were made, and undoubtedly many of them still disfigure current texts, some as recognised difficulties and others as poor readings which editors are too conservative to alter.

If more of the corruptions which still linger in dramatic texts are to be eliminated, emendation must be inspired and controlled by a knowledge (1) of the different kinds of misprints which have already been corrected, (2) of the various ways in which letters were formed, more especially by the dramatist himself, but also by other authors of the period, (3) of the various ways in which words could be spelt.
Dr. Keilner, who has issued a work entitled "Restoring Shakespeare" prefaces it by saying that "from 1914 to this day" he has been "busy to the exclusion of all other work, applying the key of the elizabetthan handwriting to the unexplained passages in Shakespeare's works"; and he ventures a modest hope that as a result of his labours he has "arrived at a number of satisfactory readings."
