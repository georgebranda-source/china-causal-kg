## [13] COMMERCE AND FINANCE.

**GRAVE TRADE OUTLOOK.**

He had always been an optimist, said Sir Robert Horne, K.C., M.P., speaking at Reading, but to-day it seemed to him that we had arrived at a period when it would be folly if we did not face the facts, however unpleasant they might be. Looking at some of the staple industries of the country, he could find no glimmer of light.

Over the whole field of the great coal industry, he continued, there was no factor which gave reason to hope that there was going to be any immediate revival of prosperity. In the steel trade, the situation was becoming worse instead of better. In regard to shipbuilding, everyone knew of the great orders which had been going to shipyards in Holland and Germany instead of to British shipyards. A friend of his, who had just returned from Holland, told him of one particular yard in which ten ships were being built, nine of them for British owners. (Shame.)
"Only last week," said Sir Robert, "my services were sought by two very large and important shipbuilders in this country to try to obtain some arrangement by which there would be brought to their idle yards a great contract which is being given out at the present time for eight ships to carry refrigerated meat from South America. The disparity between their tender and that of the foreign tender was in the region of £20,000 to £30,000 per ship, and their tender was based upon figures which involved absolutely no profit to the British shipbuilder. It was put in at actual cost. Ulimately they were prepared to build these ships at a loss, and made an offer to that effect, but it was still very many thousands of pounds above the offer of the Continental shipbuilder."

They had got, concluded Sir Robert Horne, to bring the facts of the situation home to the minds of the people in order that they might get such co-operation in the work of the country as had never been seen in the past. This was no time for people to be going about urging combinations of workmen to bring the work of the country to a standstill. That could only end for everybody in disaster. The only hope for the country and the people was that they should work together in amity and goodwill.

## [14] FOOCHOW SHIPPING

**THE TEA EXPORT TRADE.**

Ocean-going steamers call at Foochow principally in connection with the tea export trade. They stop generally at the Pagoda Anchorage, the channel further up being too shallow, although dredging operations in this section of the Min River are now in progress. According to the Foochow Customs, Report for 1923, 1,526 ocean-going steamers entered and cleared the port in addition to 76 sailing vessels. The total tonnage of the year aggregated 1,483,442 tons, of which 564,178 tons were contributed by Chinese, 381,236 tons by Japanese and 369,968 tons by British shipping. Foochow is also an important centre for coastal shipping. There are four companies keeping regular sailing schedules between Foochow and Shanghai and other northern port. Several foreign firms are also engaged in coastal shipping, their sailings being confined to ports near Foochow including Chuan-chow, Hinghwa, Wenchow, and certain ports in Formosa, Junk traffic in the Min River has been badly interfered with lately by bandit activities, The frequent commandeering of boats and pressing of men into military service by the local military leaders have terrified the boat men and oftentimes caused complete suspension of junk traffic on the river. Fukien officials are now fully alive to the necessity of affording due protection to the boatmen in order to keep the traffic on the river in normal condition. A shipping bureau has been organised at Foochow to give guarantees to the junks against commandeering. The Bureau collects a toll from all the passing junks, which are divided into five classes according to their size and the value of the cargo they carry. In addition to this levy, each junk is required to register itself with the shipping bureau by paying a registration fee of $5. When a junk has paid these levies, the officials will guarantee its immunity from commandeering. A British merchant recently established a transportation company in Foochow. The fleet of junks owned by the company are engaged principally in carrying cargo from the hinterland to the coast either for others or on the company's own account. ## [15] £10,000,000 LOAN PLAN.

**WHAT EAST AFRICA NEEDS FOR TRANSPORT.**

### السلام عليهم!

A £10,000,000 Government guaranteed loan for the development of transport in East Africa is the outstanding recommendation of the East Africa Commission; whose report has been issued.

The Commission, consisting of three members of Parliament, Mr. W. Ormsby-Gore, M.P. (Conservative), chairman; Mr. P. C. Linfield (Liberal); and Major A. G. Church (Labour), spent three months in the various territories last year. They consider that the further economic development of both native and non-native production is dependent on the early provision of increased transport facilities, and, in particular, on new railway construction.

They, therefore, propose a Bill; authorising the issue of a loan for the amount stated.

The demand made on the British taxpayer would, it is argued, be more than counter-balanced by indirect benefits of increased trade and production within the Empire. Approximately half the capital sum would be spent. If Great Britain on rails, bridging material, rolling stock, etc.

For Kenya, the Commission recommends the establishment of a Trust Board, in which all native lands should be vested. Mr. Linfield, in a supplementary memorandum, proposes a series of Trust Boards.

Stress is laid upon the duty of the Government to develop agricultural efficiency in all native areas.

Mr. Linfield's memorandum contains but several interesting points. One is his recommendation that the tax collected in each native area, instead of going into the central treasury, should be expended in part on direct services in the area

More far-reaching is his suggestion that an Imperial Development Board should be set up, to which a fixed sum would be paid annually from the Imperial Exchequer. He mentions £3,000,000 a year for a period of ten or fifteen years. By the end of that time the Board would have a steady revenue from interest and repayments, and eventually it would become independent of any further Exchequer grants.

## [16] BRIFISH CAPITAL IN CANADA.

**RAILWAY CHIEF'S ADVICE.**

Mr. E. W. Beatty, President of the Canadian Pacific Railway Company, addressing the Quebec division of the Canadian Manufacturers' Association, emphasised the importance of encouraging the investment of British capital in Canada. The British investor, he said, both on patriotic and national grounds, would, under proper conditions, prefer this country to any other portion of the world, but Canadians should not create the impression that this was not a country in which honest investments were given proper protection. "Whatever we may do to encourage the introduction of capital, we should see that it is entitled to such a measure of just protection as will place the burden of failure, if failure ensues, on its own administration, and not upon the country's fiscal political policies." Mr. Beatty's reference follows the publication of British Press comment on the Canadian railway situation.

## [17] LEVER'S

News of Lord Leverhulme's death caused a slight decline in the shares of Lever Brothers, not because of any apprehension over the future of the business, but because it was feared that the deceased peer's shares might come on the market. This is not to happen.

Lord Leverhulme, a few years ago, made the necessary arrangements for the future conduct of "Lever's," and the constitution of this huge business is unlikely, for the present, to be affected by his death.

Just 30 years ago Lever Brothers was turned into a public company with a capital of £1,500,000. So rapidly has the business grown that to-day the issued capital totals £56,627,546. Last year's profits amounted to £5,476,000. The shares largely held by the public are the 7 and 8 per cent. Preference issues.

## [19] EASTERN TELEGRAPH CO

**MR. J. C. DENISON-PENDER TO DE VICE-CHAIRMAN.**

An interesting appointment which has just been made by the E stern Telegraph Co., is reported by the “China Express and Telegraph” Mr. J. C. Denison-Pender, in addition to being joint manager of the Eastern Telegraph Co., has become its Vice-Chairman as well as Vice-Chairman of the Eastern Extension Australasia and China Telegraph Co., of which he is already a director.

The son of the present chairman of the company and grandson of Sir John Pender, one of the pioneers of the cable industry, to whose firm belief in the future of the submarine telegraph, this country owes its pre-eminence in the underseas communications of the globe, Mr. J. C. Denison-Pender may be said to have been born for the exalted position he now holds. He first saw the light in 1882, and is the third generation of his family to be virtually at the head of the Great Eastern group of cable companies. Educated at Eton, it is to his credit that he is gifted with practical knowledge, beginning from the lowest rung, which few men enjoying such early advantages can boast.

He joined the Eastern Co. as a young man, and went through the telegraphists' hard school in the ordinary way. Then he roughed it at sea for three years on cable ships, taking part in extensive cable-laying operations, and finishing the training for the position he now holds by serving in all the big departments of the Eastern Co.

Although an intensely busy man, he yet found time for relaxation in politics. From 1915 to 1918 he represented Newmarket Division in the House of Commons, and also sent for one of the Wandsworth Divisions from 1918 till the Labour Party came into possession in 1922. He also represented St. Pancras, on the London County Council for nearly ten years.

## [29] COPRA DEMAND.

**REACTION IN MARKET NOTED AS SUPPLY DIMINISHES.**

A reaction in the wholesale copra market in Manila as reflected by the renewed demand in the United States for coconut oil, coupled with the approaching rainy season when copra drying by sun will be a slow and tedious process, is expected to come at any time, according to the markets section of the bureau of commerce and industry.

Copra and coconut oil, both in domestic and foreign trading, have continued weak and featureless, little attention being given by local producers and dealers.

Now that the demand is growing again, values of copra and coconut oil will naturally increase, it is explained. Reports from New York stated that American soap men have just entered the market for coconut oil, despite the campaign to credit coconut products in the western states. This change in the oil market, it was observed, has had favourable reflection in the local coconut oil and copra trading.
Co-operating for the development of the coconut industry in the islands, W. L. Brooke, chemist of the bureau of science, has recently determined in a study made at Nagcarlan, Laguna, the weights of the component parts of the coconut. It was found that about 37.54 per cent., by the weight of the coconut is represented by the husk, 25.45 per cent., by the meat (parcel), 17.69 per cent., by shell, 14.08 per cent., by water, 3.61 per cent., by parings, and 1.82 per cent., by steami g loss.
