## [13] HongKong, Saturday, March 20, 1926.

**A SATURDAY CAUSERIE.**

Tourists may come and tourists may go, but they are "ever welcome if only for the grossly material" consideration that they have money to spend. 'Tis money makes the mare to go—and if it cannot or will not go in one direction the choice of routes is various. The great liners "Resolute" and "Laconia" have each brought large numbers of passengers, who have had opportunities not only of spending freely but of inspecting the natural beauties of Hongkong and its "environs" (as the old-fashioned guide books were wont to phrase it). Unfortunately for the fullest possible enjoyment of all that is pleasing to the aesthetic eye, the weather was lamentably wet, detracting alike from the pleasures of sight-seeing and shopping. The Legislative Council has had another meeting, but its proceedings were colourless. Two Bills were introduced providing for the issue of Hongkong Treasury bills in London and in the Colony to facilitate borrowing as occasion demands. The third reading was passed of the Bill restoring to enemy aliens certain privileges that automatically lapsed at the outbreak of the war. A little more vim than usual was imparted to the meeting of the Finance Committee by reason of an animated discussion on the feasibility or otherwise of re-opening the statistics office of the Imports and Exports Department. The question has been referred to the General Chamber of Commerce and the Chinese Chamber of Commerce thus further deferring a final decision by the Government. Three Company meetings have been held, of which one—the Star Ferry Co., Ltd.—was purely formal for the purpose of altering the Articles of Association. Satisfactory results were disclosed by the Hon. ## [15] THRIFT:

One thing at least should strike the reader who takes the trouble to read the accounts of school prize distributions. In the midst of the list of things attempted and done, there is never a word about the question of thrift. It may not be done now—but years ago a branch of the Penny Savings Bank was a feature at a number of elementary schools in England. Each Monday, schoolchildren brought their pennies to school and the amounts were recorded in a diminutive pass book. Thus, very early in life, the young idea was taught the value of thrift, and the fact that if the pennies were saved, the pounds would take care of themselves. Perhaps we ask too much that the Hongkong dominie should act as bank clerk in addition to his or her educational duties proper. But if education is meant to fit a child for life, the subject of thrift is, of important subjects, the most important. In a place where generally speaking, money is readily earned, it is also readily spent. The child mind does not grasp economic facts readily. He knows the value of money from the spending point of view, and is content to let it go at that. Canon Peter Green thinks there is more morality in £10 in the savings bank than in the Ten Commandments. "The Ten Commandments told you what you ought to do; £10 in the savings bank made you want to do it." The Canon thinks there is no work more patriotic to day than the inculcation of thrift. The epigram is so good that it would be a pity to analyse it too scrupulously; whilst the inculcation statement is so true that it is needless to insist upon it.

## [16] $50,000 CASE

**WING ON PROSECUTION CONTINUES.**

After a delay through counsel being engaged at the Supreme Court, the case was continued before Mr. R. E. Lindsell (senior magistrate) yesterday afternoon of alleged conspiracy by two Chinese to obtain $50,000 from Messrs. Wing. On's blanking department.

Features of the case so far has been the raising of bail after a third defendant had jumped bail," and the spirited passages between the lawyers engaged.

Mr. F. C. Jenkin is prosecuting: Mr. Leo d'Almada (who instructed Mr. Elsley Zeililyn) acted in the absence of counsel (who has gone to Shanghai) and Mr. G. K. Hall Brutton represented the other defendant.

Evidence was given yesterday (before Mr. Hall Brutton continued his cross-examination) by an interpreter at the Supreme Court, and also a member of the registry staff; and by the sub-accountant of the Industrial and Commercial Bank, Hongkong.

The case will be continued on Tuesday.

## [20] MAKING MERRY

### 一

About five hundred dancers turned up last night at the City Hall for the fancy dress masked curvilval ball and masquerade which was declared to be a complete success for the promoters.

The different schemes of decoration, with soft-shaded electric light, streamers, bunting, flags, &c. lent an appropriate touch of colour which was noticeable from the entrance to the dance floor, supper room and other places.

Two dance bands carried through a non-stop programine of forty-two dances and extras from shortly after nine o'clock till a late hour.

As little interest had been taken in the popular ballot for selection of dances, the following (as returned by voting papers) were given:

Fox-trots.—“Ten for Two” (7); “Show me the Way to go Home” (9); “I want to be Happy” (12); “Katherine” (14); “Rose Marie” (17); “Don't Bring Lulu” (20); “My Swiss” (28); “Tittina” (25); “Oh How I love my Darling” (28); “Yenning” (30); “Susie” (32); “Ukulele Lady” (35).

"Wallz"—"Moonlight Roses" (6); "All Alone" (11); "What'll I do" (16); "June" (31); "Dreamy Melody" (36); "Kiss in the Dark" (41).

Some of the tourist ship "Laconia's" passengers were present and thoroughly enjoyed themselves.

The promoters laid stress on the advantage of masking. With their indentities concealed, dancers entered into the spirit of the carnival with gaiety. It was an occasion for people to feel at liberty, dispensing with the formalities usually associated with big dances arranged in the Colony.

There were fancy dresses of all descriptions, some of "a really ambitious and unique make-up. As an initial venture of its kind, the carnival must be considered a successful forerunner of others to come.
