Commencing in what has been described "a small way," the strike begun by numbers of school-boys has developed to such an extent as to leave no doubt where it will finally end. The issues are clear so far as the Government and peaceful inhabitants of this Colony are concerned. The strike is none of their seeking. They have no questions of any sort to answer. There is nothing of which they need feel ashamed and nothing that can be laid to their charge. People boasting an ancient civilisation; an old-world courtesy, and, hitherto, a modicum of common sense, have, at the instigation we must assume, of irresponsible factions, left their work without warning and without notice. Of most of them it is charitable to think "that they know not what they do." Of the result of their action they are soon likely to know. The weapons of unreason and hate are double-edged weapons. Time will demonstrate this fact. Those who live by these things will surely suffer from them. In the meantime those who are temporarily inconvenienced or made to suffer must bear a brave front. Bearing in mind that the quarrel now forced upon the Colony is not of its seeking, that, metaphorically its hands are clean; that Chinese above all have had treatment accorded to them of the kindliest, the best, and in accordance with the highest traits of justice and freedom, the attitude of all must be one of distinct firmness. There must be a tightening of the belt or decision and determination. A lead has

been given by the authorities and by those bodies reflecting and representing bodies of residents. Panic must be eliminated from all minds. Actions must be free from the halevolene which it is natural to feel at the unwarranted conduct of masses of people who like "silly sheep" have wandered from the guiding hand that has protected them; given them freedom of action and thought forbidden them in Canton. It will be in a spirit of quiet determination that the lesson will be brought home to those who have bowed down to the command of unseason and the fear that plays such a part in the life of the many whose ignorance is played upon so successfully. The Government with commendable promptness has initiated methods of combating the spread of this unnecessary evil. There is established a censorship of telegrams and letters; and means whereby the commodities necessary to life may be requisitioned; also the arrest of those who willfully obstruct public officers in the execution of their duty. These precautions will initial confidence and give that assurance—not merely to foreigners but to those Chinese who are peaceful and law-abiding which should conduce to confidence. In the knowledge of the dastardly thing which has been thrust upon the Colony, the "China Mail" urges the use of those British traits which are this, have demonstrated the righteousness of justice. It is time for the tightening of the belt of decision and determination.

## [12] The Cenotaph.

The Bishop of Manchester has declared himself in favour of memorials which are memorials only and not put to any "useful" use. In this way the mind centralises on the main fact of the memorial—the reason for it. It is this which makes Hongkong's Cenotaph—or should do so—the realistic thing it is—a perpetual reminder of a cause and notable self-sacrifice. Two things have disturbed the minds of those who continually see in this piece of unaffected inspiring piece of masonry, something which is uplifting in its beauty and dignity. The first has been the misuse which has been made of it and its surrounding green by unthinking coolies, who have come to gaze on the empty tomb and to wonder at its significance and then, in the cool of the evening to use the steps as a meeting—sitting and sleeping place. There can be no objection to the former; there can and has been to the latter.

Prolixity.

Lord Oxford is responsible for the reference to an American orator that “he was like a train of fifteen cars and only one passenger.” And England’s orator of good English comments—“That is prolixity.” The quotation comes very apt after a glance at the current issue of that sometimes exciting journal “The Hongkong Government Gazette.” In it is to be found a series of regulations under the caption “Prison Rules.” They are indeed “a train of fifteen cars and only one passenger.” No fewer than three hundred and thirty-five clauses make up these “Prison Rules” to say nothing of menus of prison fare. Tribute is not paid to the author of this literary masterpiece. Jaded readers of fiction will undoubtedly find something of interest in these pages; and if only on that account; and on that account only a perusal is highly recommended. Clause 177 instructs that “.....the hair of the prisoner shall not be cut closer than may be necessary for the purpose of health and cleanliness.” The scintillating purity and clearness of this instruction like the peace of God, passes all understanding. The Chief Warder has twenty-four clauses devoted to him and of these 22 begin. “The” Chief Warder shall.” His job is not exactly “a bobby’s one.” He is not to absent himself from his duties without the permission of the Superintendent and—and this seems terrible—he “shall endeavour to exercise a sound moral influence over both the officers and prisoners placed under his supervision. He shall restrain by his authority every tendency to oppression or undue harshness on the part of the prison officers, and likewise every tendency to levity, rudeness and insubordination on the part of prisoners, and shall aim to raise the minds of the prison officers to a sense of their responsibility.” If Chief Warders die young we now know the reason.....But space is limited for a further analysis of this momentous piece of official prolixity. If similar productions govern the working of other Government Departments we can well understand why there is a slowness in performing things.

## [19] FAITH IN FUTURE OF CHINA.

Dr. Jacob Gould Schurman, Ambassador designate to Germany, a caller at the White House, expressed the utmost confidence in the future of China, where he has represented the United States as Minister. The Chinese will establish a stable government, but they must do it in their own way and in their own time; he asserted, adding:—
“The Chinese people do not do things with the speed of the Western world, but they recognize the importance of an effective central government, and the pre-

sent Chief Executive of the provisional government has arranged for what Americans would call a national convention, in which he hopes to bring about some form of permanent constitutional government in China. With this end in view a preliminary conference of the provinces has already been held.

“I want you to bear in mind that the Chinese people are confronted with difficulties which we and the people of European countries do not have in the formation of a new government similar to our republican form of government,” Dr. Schurman said. “The country is divided into provinces in which military governors exercise control over their respective provinces. The chief difficulty is to get them to recognize the national government. Each one would have to give up his army. If force were used, one might destroy all the others, but others would rise in their places. They can be bought, but they do not stay bought.”

“The country seems to be in about the same condition it was four years ago in this respect. If one rival is put down, another springs up in his place. The only way a stable government can be formed is through the agreement of these military governors.”
Profound faith in the Chinese people was expressed by Dr. Schurman, who, while in China travelled into every part of the country. The difficulties in the founding of a democratic form of national government there were enhanced by the fact that their imperialistic government had been the oldest in the world. It had suited them during about 5,000 years, and up to this time they have only had 12 years in which to bring themselves to a republican form of government.

“They are such a wonderful people that I have perfect confidence they will get on the basis of a republican form of government in time,” he continued. “We must have faith in the Chinese people.” Their history justifies this and our present experience with them justifies it, so that we must have patience with them, as they do not do things in a hurry like Americans or Europeans.

“We must also let them do it in their own way. We cannot tell them how to do it. Their new Government, when formed, will be adapted to their conditions, and it will be their own Government, set up in their own way. It would be the height of folly for foreign governments to intervene for the purpose of giving China a stable government. That country would resent it. National consciousness and racial consciousness is developed there, so that such intervention would unify the Chinese people against the other governments. Nobody can do it for them, but they must do it for themselves. We must give them their own time, and continue to exercise confidence in them.”

The present policy of America toward China should continue," Dr. Schurman added. "This policy is one of sympathy, goodwill, and helpfulness, whenever China wants it. China regards America as its best friend, he declared. This is because we do not want special privileges and we have respect for her ideals."
Communism cannot gain a foot-hold in China, according to Dr. Schurman's view. Locally the Chinese people are the most democratic in the world, most of them owning some property. This with the fact that there is no "proletariat" or large bodies of workingmen congregated in large cities, would operate against the extension of the Soviet into China, he declared.
