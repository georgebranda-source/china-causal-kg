## [16] WELSH NOTES.

**MORE OUTSPOKEN CRITICISM.**

Outspoken criticism of “slavish adoration of degrees, the enthronement of financial success as the ultimate object of life, the sports mania which took 54 train loads from the land of religious revivals to see 22 men kick a piece of leather about and the growing professionalism in our religion” has drawn on D Martin Lloyd Jones the wrath of some who have dubbed him a “second Caradoc Evans” but a closer examination of the text of the address in the latest Welsh papers shows that Dr. Jones has been wronged by the extracts which have been published away from their proper context.

Dr. Jones's criticism was a feature of the Union of Welsh Societies Conference, a full report of which has reached me. Dr. Jones was at some pains to explain that what he said about modern Wales could be said with equal truth about England and other countries but what was evil and regrettable in the case of other nations was to him a tragedy when applied to Wales, with a tradition second to none other. A critic who addressed his criticisms to a foreign nation and not to his own countrymen might deservedly call upon himself the assignation of a traitor but his treachery was only equalled by the treachery of those who, while realising that all was not well with Wales, persisted in saying in public that all was well.

The standpoint of Dr. Jones was that of a lover of his country to whom any evidence of deterioration in the object of his love was intolerable and there is nothing in the text of his address to which exception could be taken by any other than the narrow minded who refuse to contemplate the possibility of there ever being in the principality anything calling for a measure of reform.

Striking corroboration of the point made by Dr. Jones as to there being at the present time a kind of ebb in the tide of intellectual activity in Wales is to hand in a statement appearing in a Welsh weekly paper which has had to give up its poetry column owing to the poor and tawdry character of the work submitted for publication. Commenting on the fact, the "Welsh Outlook" states that it is discorparting after all the ferment of years to find so little good brew and hardly any of exceptional quality. In support of its contention that the Welsh fiction of the last few years has almost without exception been lengths below the standard of Daniel Owen, this paper comments as follows:—We still remember the thrill which the Rev. Dewi Williams and Prof. W. J. Gruffydd gave us in their first handling of it. But apart from the lighter and slighter efforts of Miss Kate Roberts, the Rev. R. G. Berry and the Rev. Seymour Rees, the new literary factory has apparently been closed.

On the whole it would appear that Dr. Jones' criticism has not been unfavourably received by the majority of thinking Welshmen who can see a quicker emancipation in a more complete realisation of the difficulties to be confronted. Many of them, too, are placing a firm faith in the ultimate benefit to the principality and its language and literature of the recently constituted departmental committee set up to enquire into the education system of Wales.

## [20] "I HAVE HIM TO HIS FEELING."

'I think Jackson's conduct is reprechensible. He evidently feels it now. I am glad he does. No one can envy the feelings of a father who assaults his own daughter, so young as this one, who had probably in consequence taken her life. I leave him to his feelings and record a verdict that she died of drowning and killed herself while of unsound mind.'

So said Mr. Ingleby Oddie, the Westminster coroner, at the conclusion of the resumed inquest into the dentifi of Ellen Jackson, aged 19, of Latchmere-grove, Buttersea, whose body was recovered from the Thames on May 2.

At the last bearing her father, James, Joseph Jackson, a steel worker, said his wife died on March 6. He had four other children, and since then Ellen had been "trying to run the home."

Mrs. Ada Helden Burmur, who lives next door to the Jackson family, said that on April 24, she heard Ellen cry out: "Daddy, don't." Next morning she asked the child why her father had been beating her, and she replied: "Daddy has been up to granny's, and granny has been telling him tales." The girl also added that her aunt had also told her father tales.

The Coroner: Did she say she was fired of life?—Yes.

What did she say about the Thames?—She said: "You will hear of me being at the bottom of the Thames, Mrs. Burmar."

The father went into the witnessbox and said that he had only smacked Ellen once, and that was on the Monday previous to her disappearance.

Why did you smack her?—I could asked a question as to money did she could not give me a satisfactory answer, so I gave her a smack.

Did she try to run away?—Yes. Did you pull her back?—I pulled her back by her clothing.

Did you pull her back by her hair?—No.

If you say you never punished her and only gave her a smack in the face with your open hand, how do you account for the bruises on her arm?—I have no idea of it.

I can tell you how it probably happened. When you were smacking her face you seized her left arm?—No.

*Aren't you sorry now you'll it this little girl?—I am very sorry.
The Cramer: She was only little girl, you know.

The father replied that she was nearly 14 years of age and was a big strong girl.

No one's heart, he declared, could ache more than his did.

Then you have a heartache, have you?—I have had it all the week with all this scandulising [he had said he had received scandulising letters from neighbours.]

What about the treatment of the little girl? Has that affected you?
—It might have had something to do with it.

The coroner, summing up, said that Ellen, at a time when she also had to go to school, had all the anxiety and responsibility of the housekeeping and she was mourning the loss of her mother. Surely that was not the time for the father to assault a little girl such as she was merely because she got mud-dled with the housekeeping money.
