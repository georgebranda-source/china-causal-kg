## [1] A WAR DISCLOSURE.

**GERMAN THREAT TO INVADE AUSTRIA.**

### ↓

How Germany nearly added one of its own Allies to the countries already bringing against it in the World War is revealed by Dr. Otto Elover, the Austrian Socialist leader and Foreign Secretary in the first Republican Government, in his just-published book, "The Austrian Revolution" (Teomird Pursons, 10s. 6d.).

On the thesis that the whole modern history of German Austria is coloured by the opposition between German and Austrian mentalities, Dr. Bauer argues that this antagonism was only neutralised by the fact that, from 1897 onwards, Austrian patriotism saw its Empire threatened by the financing agitation of the Slav races within its frontiers.

How artificial was the apparent resulting unity, and how fundamentally different the outlook of German and Austrian, was clearly shown in January 1918, when, following the Brest-Litovsk Treaty, the Austrian people demonstrated against the continuance of the war against Russia to fulfil that Treaty, and, by a great general strike in Vienna, presaged a revolution that would enforce peace.

## [11] "THE TWO PINS CLUB"

**LAST FURNISS BOOK.**

Stories of leading characters in the sporting and dramatic world in the last half-century are told by the late Mr. Harry Furniss in

"The Two Pins Club," which, illustrated by the famous artist himself is published by Mr. John Murray, at 10s. Gd.

"The Two Pins Club" was a riding club, whose members were the famous Lord Chief Justice,

Lord Russell of Killowen; Sir Frank Lockwood, K.O., Solicitor-General; Lord Burnham; Sir Charles Mathews, Director of Public Prosecutions; Sir John Hare; Sir Frank Burnand, editor of "Punch"; Sir John Tenniel, the cartoonist; Linley Sambourne, the artist; Rudolph Lehmann, and Mr. Furness.

The club was named by Sir Frank Burnand after "the two knights of the road, of course," John Gilpin and Dick Turpin, and members rode out of town each Sunday possible to lunch in some attractive place near London.

Mr. Burniss reveals in this book "a little mystery that puzzled the Pressmen of the time, and was known to very few members of the Garrick." At a critical period, "a short" while before the Balfour Government died of ennui," the Turner Councils within the Cabinet Council were held in the strangers' room of the Garrick Club. Nobody knew where the discussions took place, and so little known was Balfour at the club that his presence there passed unnoticed.

The story of a sedate housekeeper who became a "bookie" is told by Mr. Furniss. A doctor in a provincial town died and left his house to his faithful housekeeper, so that she might take in lodgers.
But the doctor had been an ardent sportsman and keen racing man, and the quiet, uncommunicative housekeeper had learned more than her employer would ever have believed. After his death she used her knowledge to set up to business as a bookmaker, and in time built up a prosperous concern.

Mr. Furniss died as this, the last book he wrote, was passing through the press.
