## [13] IMPORTANT STATEMENT

### (Continued from page 1.)

### Mischief Makers.

"There has been before the war, and it was undoubtedly greatly stimulated by all that has taken place during the war, a great growth of national spirit, and if it can form a united China of civilised principles; strong in its own strength and able to secure order within its borders and do justice to the foreigner within its gates, then no one will wish it more success than His Majesty's present Government. (Cheers). At the present there is not that unity. There is not that unified force in the government, there is general 'unrest, a general trouble which is being used by some of those who create it and by others to inflame Chinese opinion against all strangers within their gates.
"That is what has happened in the strike that took place in a Chinese factory. I do not stand here to defend conditions in the factories. (Cheers)"

"If that strike had been confined, or if the movement had been confined to a movement for amelioration of conditions the despatch which I read in my opening observations proves that His Majesty's Government would have lent the movement all the support they could command. But the factory conditions, however bad they be, and glad as we would be to see them altered, do not justify the advance of a murderous mob crying 'Kill the foreeigner!' on a police station containing large stocks of arms. It was not until—according to information I have at the present—that the police station was in danger with all the arms it contained that the order was given to fire."

Not Britain Alone.
“If that be the case then I say that the order given to fire saved bloodshed instead of causing it. Let me say at once His Majesty's Government and other Governments concerned—remember in these matters it is an international body which is acting in Shanghai as it is an international body. the Diplomatic Corps, which is acting in Peking, and that they are acting with more knowledge than any of us have here at the present time under circumstances of responsibility of which no one here can relieve them, and let us give them credit which we would ask for ourselves for moderation, for humanity, and for a desire to bring a peaceful issue out of these disastrous troubles,—it is in these circumstances we all of us feel that into the circumstances there should be the fullest and frankest enquiry.”

Labour speakers: (We all accept that.)

And in the meantime accepting that, I beg hon, members not to pass judgment in advance of receiving the evidence. (Cheers.) That enquiry is the first point.
Coming Conference.
In the second place we are anxious to enter on the full investigation of the best way to improve relations between China and the Powers in the spirit of the Washington Conference. Which is to meet China in accordance with the customs treaty signed at Washington which would give the opportunity for such investigation. We are therefore anxious that the conference should meet with the least possible delay and we propose to consult the other powers concerned as to the most practicable way of expediting this meeting.

## [14] Peking Quiet.

The situation is most quiet. There have been no large demonstrations of students. Attempts to institute a boycott has hitherto had little success. Street collections for Shanghai strikers have practically ceased.

## [15] OXY-ACETYLENE, AND

"The Association of College Professors on Foreign Relations" advises giving up collections altogether as they are liable to alienate the sympathy of the populace.

"Here I may observe that such a disposition on the part of His Majesty's Government and other Powers concerned may be helped or hindered, or even rendered impossible, according to the attitude of the Chinese authorities, central and local. It may be an attitude of goodwill and conciliation or the reverse." Insofar as the Chinese Government and local authorities in China fail to show their good faith by repressing this agitation—and there are parts of China where word from a Governor has prevented all this agitation—by stopping agitation strikes and boycotts, steps to hasting the meeting of the conference may become more difficult. I trust, therefore, that not only among the Great Powerly Spirit of conciliation and helpless may be found, but among the Chinese also. We may be met by a similar spirit of conciliation and goodwill and that being so, by working together may help to pilot China through her difficult transitional period and establish her Government on firm foundations of equity and justice to the foreigner and independence and liberty at home. Upon that basis, we and they together may build our common prosperity to the advantage of the world." (Cheers).

## [17] COUP AT SWATOW.

**GENERAL FLIES CHAN'S FLAG**

**NO FIGHTING.**

Private messages received in Hongkong today indicate a coup in favour of General Chan Kwing-ming at Switow. Rumours are current that whilst no fighting has taken place, the port is once more in the hands of pro-Chan rulers, who are opposed to the Cantonese now holding Canton.

Before war broke out between the Cantonese and the mercenaries, some of Chu's former detachments were absorbed in General Hsu Shung-chi's Knomintang army. Since then the nucleus of that army has been making its way back to Canton. General Hsu arrived in Hongkong, and yesterday "afternoon" left with five of his staff by the s.s. Chuen Chow for Macao, where two of the staff were immediately despatched to Canton by the s.s. Shing Cheong.

According to the reports to hand, Commander Hung, who is a Chan Kwing-ming man, surrendered to General Hsu when the cry "Canton for the Cantonese" was taken up. When Hsu left Swatow, Hung is said to have been left in charge and the news today is that he has gone back to Chan by flying the latter's flag at all the Government offices.

The foregoing has not been confirmed. Absence of telegraphic communication hinders the rapid transmission of authentic reports.

## [21] AL FRESCO CONCERT.

**K.C.C. ENTERTAINMENT TO-MORROW.**

The weather is undoubtedly on the mend and conditions tomorrow should be ideal for the first of the Kowloon Cricket Club's Al Fresco concerts. A programme suitable to the occasion has been arranged and the well-known Pharies are to give that class of entertainment which has made them famous and which should suit the conditions of an open-air concert to the proverbial T. The printed programme before us reveals items that should meet the taste of all. There are 21 items in it, but as they all suggest encores, the number should swell to 42. A treat is certainly in store for all who attend, and all should attend if only to encourage the K.C.C. in their meritorious work.
