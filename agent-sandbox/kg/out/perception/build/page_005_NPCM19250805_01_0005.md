## [1] TYPHOON DAMAGE.

**IMPORTANT SUPREME COURT ACTION.**

**HON. MR. H. W. BIRD'S EVIDENCE.**

The Hon. Mr. H. W. Bird was crossed-examined this morning in connection with a Supreme Court action for the setting aside of an award made by him as arbitrator in connection with houses built in Kennedy Road and which were damaged by a landslide caused by the typhoon of 1923.

The case for the plaintiff is that under an agreement the contractor who erected the houses to plaintiff's order was liable for any such damage and had no right to claim, extra in respect of it. Mr. Bird, in his award, had found for the contractor.

Mr. F. C. Jenkin, counsel for the plaintiff, asked Mr. Bird if he had not put the following question to the plaintiff when the arbitration proceedings were proceeding: "If the damage to a contractor through typhoon damage was something like $200,000 would you still expect him to shoulder the loss?"

Mr. Bird: Something to that effect.

Mr. Jenkin: And your reply to that was "Oh, no, you cannot break a man like that."

Mr. Bird: Something like that.

Mr. Jenkin: It would be in your mind that it was an unjust thing to penalise a contractor where the damage was very large and that therefore it was not when the damage was small. Must you not keep to the principle in adjudicating on these matters?

Mr. Bird: I tried to bring the matter home to him.

Mr. Jenkin further suggested in the course of cross-examination that Mr. Bird showed great haste and impatience with the plaintiff in the course of the arbitration because of the insistence with which he struck to his ground that "according to contract he should not be saddled with such a large sum of money."

Mr. Bird replied that he had shown no undue haste but that he had made it quite clear to the plaintiff that it was no use arguing that particular point as he had already made up his mind about it.

Asked why he did not at the time of the interview look at the particular clause in the agreement referred to by the plaintiff Mr. Bird said that he was already fully conversant with it.

In a declaration sworn to quite recently by Mr. Bird, said Mr. Jenkin, Mr. Bird had corrected a statement made previously that only one party had been present at the hearing and had amended it so that it read both were present. The statement was not even yet clear, said Mr. Jenkin, for it would be a fair reading of the amended statement to infer that both had given evidence.

Mr. Bird: Both were present but Lam. Tuk (the contractor) was not called upon to say anything. Why I did not say so in the first statement I cannot remember. It was over a year ago.
In the course of evidence Mr. Bird had stated that there was no need for Lam Tuk to be called upon as his case was simply the presenting of a bill of extras.

## [3] SHARE MARKET

**FINANCIAL DEPRESSION.**

Seen by a “China Mall” reporter this morning, a leading stockbroker said that there was little to be done in Ice House Street where the accumulated business had still to be cleared up.

“Even nominally,” he admitted, “arrears had not been made good and the only thing to do was to wait for a return to normal times. Financially, the position was no better off than when the trouble began although in other directions there had been vast improvement.”

"Money cannot be got out of the ground," the speaker replied to a query, "not even on gift-edged security."

## [4] VALLEY FLOOD.

**STEAM ROLLER WATCH MAN'S DEATH INQUIRY.**

Sitting us Coroner at the Central Magistracy yesterday, and assisted by a jury composed of Messrs. D. 'Temperley (foeman), A. D. Russell and C. J. Joseph, Mr. S. B. B. McElderry conducted an inquiry into the circumstances attending the death of a P.W.D. coolie named Tong Fat, who was guiding a P.W.D. steam roller, during the recent floods at Happy Valley.

Mr. St. W. Lo appeared on behalf of the directors of the Young Wo Nursing Home, and Mr. E. F. C. Brooks watched proceedings on behalf of Dr. Leung Cheung-man.

The Coroner said that the man was killed by the collapse of a wall, and there was therefore the question of responsibility. He asked the solicitors to refrain from asking questions which were not concerned with the proceedings as a death inquiry.

Dr. E. P. Minnett deposed to having conducted a post mortem examination on June 17, on the body of a cookie identified as Tong Rat. The ribs on the right side were broken and the liver and spleen were ruptured. Witness considered that death must have been instantaneous.

A Chinese constable said that at 1:30 a.m. on June 16, he received

a report that a ten-shed had been washed away by the flood. On arriving at the scene the found a man lying dead across the nullah on the rice course. The body was lying in water, and the arms were pinioned with stones. Another body was found half an hour later. Both the bodies were taken to the mortuary where they were identified.

Dr. Clau Wai-cheng, managing director of the Yeung Wo Nursing Home, gave evidence describing the state in which he found the Home on the night of the flood.

The next witness was Lo Shumchi, foreman for the Tung San contractors, owners of the site. After a series of questions put to this witness by the foreman of the jury and the Coroner, it was elicited that he was in charge of the building of the wall on a plan prepared by Mr. Raven. His instructions were to construct the wall with one part of cement and six parts of sand, the latter being taken from the hillside. There were also bands of cement concrete running through the wall, the concrete being composed of one part cement, three parts sand, and six parts stone. The wall was 20 feet above the level of the ground with a base of nine feet. Witness stated that the construction of the wall commenced in January and the work was finished three days before the acid化, but there had not been enough time for the mortar to settle down.

A report on the site was submitted by Mr. Boothby, engineer of the Kowloon-Canton Railway, and Mr. H. A. Goldsmith, engineer in charge of roads, P.W.D., gave evidence as to drainage on Stubbs Road.

The inquiry was adjourned until this afternoon.

## [5] CONTRADICTORY.

**CHARGE AGAINST A COOLIE DISMISSED.**

A coolie was charged at the Kowloon Magistracy this morning with depositing night soil at the back of Aimai Villas.

The complainant, Mr. E. C. Bell, a special constable, deposed to having seen the accused emptying a bucket into a surface gutter.

The accused denied the complaint's story.

Mrs. King, of No. 2, Aimai Villas, stated in evidence that she saw the accused, after cleaning her bathroom, put the night soil into the servants' lavatory and not into the gutter.

Other evidence was also tendered, which all went in favour of the accused.

His Worship said that he could not convict on evidence which was contradictory, and discharged the accused.

## [6] HOME CRICKET.

**34,000 WATCH HOBBS PLAY.**

**BANK HOLIDAY MATCHES.**

After the August Bank Holiday the counties have generally sorted themselves out in the Home cricket season.

Results which came in this morning show that Yorkshire (the champions) and Surrey (who are second) were both checked. However, they retain their respective places in the table, although Lancashire are only a fraction behind Surrey.

It will be seen that J. B. Hobbs has still to make that century to tie the record standing in the name of Dr. W. G. Grace, namely, 126 centuries in first-class cricket. The Oval record crowd mentioned by Reuter must have been sorely disappointed when their favourite failed and when Notts robbed the home side of four points.

No other team has deprived the White Rose of more than two points.
