## [8] THE NEXT STEP.

The League Council has fixed March 8 as the date of the extraordinary meeting of the Assembly to deliver an opinion on Germany's application for membership.

## [9] *(no headline: column 3)*

The papers, foreseeing that Germany will endeavour to raise discussions at Geneva with reference to the peace treaties, especially those relating to the Eastern Frontiers, insist on the necessity of giving Poland a permanent seat on the Council of the League of Nations as a counteracting measure for affording a equilibrium.

## [10] Plan Which Failed.

The "Daily Telegraph's" Berlin correspondent has stated that the plan to give permanent seats on the Council of the League to minor States has been dropped. If it had been persisted in Germany would undoubtedly have withdrawn her application for membership. Uneasiness here has now been allayed by official news from Paris.

## [11] *(no headline: column 3)*

The Foreign Secretary's answer in Parliament, yesterday to Mr. Ramsay MacDonald's question relative to the future composition of the League of Nations Council, has been followed by general discussion in the newspapers.
Sir Austen Chamberlain stated that until any claims to permanent neats on the League, other than that, which it is hoped to allot to Germany, have been formally preferred and fully considered, it is impossible to decide on a course best serving the interests of the Council and of the League generally. He emphasized that the matter was one upon which the feeling of the Council and Assembly was one of primary consideration and that common consultation and agreement would be impossible if representatives of those bodies were bound in advance by rigid instructions.

In their comments on this issue a most friendly tone is adopted by the newspapers towards nations who in addition to Germany are believed to have ambitions in this direction, but hope is generally expressed that such claims will not be pressed.

The "Daily News" takes the view that "It would make the mere mechanical working of the League extraordinarily difficult in future by exaggerating the size and importance of the Council and correspondingly depreciating the rightful importance of the Assembly."

## [13] A BANDIT'S HEAD.

*(Reuter's American Service.)*

The mystery of the disappearance of the head of Francisco Villa appears to have been cleared up by an admission allegedly made by some scientists here that the head was being brought to Chicago for examination. Experts' story is that an eccentric millionaire interested in criminology, financed the theft of the head in order that the brain and bumps of the late bandit might be examined, and analysed from a pathological standpoint for the benefit of science.

*(Röster's American Service.)*

Washington, Feb. 12.
The anthracite strike cost the miners over $150,000,000 in wages and resulted in the curtailment of the hard coal output by over $85,000,000 tons.
The Secretary of Labour, Mr. J. J. Davis confirmed, as "entirely correct," the Governor of Pennsylvania's announcement that the anthracite coal has been settled. The contract is for five years, wages to continue at the present level with provision for reopening negotiations within the industry whenever either side so desires.

## [16] *(no headline: column 5)*

Wage scale modifications may only be proposed once yearly to the Board provided, which must within ninety days decide all issues connected with wage adjustment.

The demands of the owners and workers on the question of co-operation and efficiency shall be referred to a Board of Conciliation exclusive of an umpire who shall work out a reciprocal programme. Mr. John Lewis will act as president of the Board.

As soon as the agreement was made known, the Miners Union made a statement expressing satisfaction at the terms and declaring that it was specially pleased that arbitration was avoided. One of the mine owners also made a statement forecasting that five years of harmony in the anthracite industry which would result from the agreement.
