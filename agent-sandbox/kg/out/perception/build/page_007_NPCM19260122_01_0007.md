## [2] GREAT DAM.

**YAST BARREN STEPFM CONVERTEL.**

**NOW THRIVING COUNTRY.**

*(British Wireless Service)*

*Rugby, Janv 21*

By means of the great dam, on the Blue Nile at Makwar, the opening ceremony of which was performed today by Lord Lloyd High Commissioner for the Soudan, a vast region in the Soudan is converted from a barren steppe and enhanced by a few semi-normal clans into the living agricultural country.

The immediate area brought into cultivation is 300,000 acres of black soil admirably suited for growing cotton. Crops are being grown on three years' rotation; soil being one year under cotton, second under "Habibia" and a third year Fallow.

The reservoir above the dam consists of a length of 30 miles and a maximum height of 40 metres. It is constructed of solid masonry, granite being obtained from quarries in the neighbourhood. The major portion is composed of masonry set in cement mortar with facing of Portland cement. Suice openings are lined with dressed ashlar. At the end of the masonry portions, the dam is continued on both east and west sides by an earth embankment with a heavy concrete facing wall on the upstream side.

This tremendous fact of engineering was actually finished just in time to catch last year's flood. The first train crossed the darm at the end of June and water was first let into the irrigation canals on July 15. The construction of those canals proceeded concurrently with the erection of the darm and necessitated excavation of approximately 15,000,000 cubic metres off earth. "There are in all about 1000 kilometres of main canals and 900 kilometres of branch and subsidiary canals.

## [3] BLESSING THE WATERS

### Ancient Irrigator Recalled.

In the presence of Sir Geoffrey Archer, two hundred European guests, and ten thousand natives, Lord Lloyd formally inaugurated the Makwar Danr by operating a special switch in the form of, a silver statuette of Amenhemat II, the first known irrigator of ancient Egypt, thereby opening the stulics, after which the inflowing waters were blessed by the Rt. ## [4] CROMER'S GENIUS.

### Soudan Reaping the Fruits.

At the opening of the Makwar Dam Lord Lloyd paid a tribute to the genius and patience of Lord Cromer, of which the Soudan was now reaping the fruits. He emphasised that Egypt had no need to be alarmed that the new dam would deprive Egypt of water. He said that there should now be no difficulty in reaching a settlement, simply guaranteeing the needs of Egypt and simultaneously allowing the Soudan to make steady progress and development.

Pasha Small Sirry, the Egyptian Minister for Public Works, who was present at the ceremony, voiced pride in the part played by Egypt in initiating the Gezirs irrigation project. He had no doubt that a water supply, artificially controlled, was simply sufficient for all their demands.

## [7] SPY'S SON.

**SENTENCED TO DEATH FOR MURDER.**

**CHISTMAS EYE TRAGEDY.**

*(British Wireless Service.)*

*Rugby, Jan. 21.*

Ignatius Emanuel Lincoln, aged 23, a Bombardier in the Royal Horse Artillery, was to-day sentenced to death at Devizes, for the murder of Edward Richard Brewers, traveller.

Richard was found shot in his garden at Trowbridge on Christmas Eve. Lincoln and a fellow-soldier named Ian Stewart were charged with the crime. Lincoln gave evidence how he and Stewart went to the house of Richards at night, whilst Richards was away, to try to get money, which they believed was in the house. They entered the house by an unlocked door, but obtained no money. They drank brandy and beer which they found, and were still in the house when Richards appeared at the door. Lincoln admitted that in an endeavour to escape he fired his automatic pistol several times in the direction of Richards, who cried out that he was hit.

The other soldier was acquitted of murder, but was re-arrested for burglary.

A notable feature of the case is that Lincoln is the son of Trebitch Lincoln, an alien who was at one time a member of the British Parliament and was during the war on his own confession found guilty of espionage and deported to Prague.

## [8] NO ALLIANCE?

**COMMENTS ON LLOYGE GORGE'S SPEECH.**

**YOUNGER LIBERAL DISAGREES.**

*(British Wireless Service.)*

Mr. Lloyd George's speech at Carnarvon yesterday, in which he declared that the Liberal Party would not at this moment negotiate an alliance with the Labour Party but would strive to increase its own strength, is the subject of varied comment to-day.

Mr. Charles G. Ammon, who was a member of the late Labour Government, declared that he did not think there could ever be a coalition of Liberal and Labour, but he believed that some of the Liberals would come over to the Labour Party.

Commander the Hon. Joseph Kenworthy, M.P., who is one of the leading younger Liberals, declared in favour of an immediate active working arrangement between the Labour and Liberal Parties in preparation for the formation of an alternative Government. He is reported to have added: "We cannot go on with the Conservative Party in power and no strong alternative Government. If certain leaders stand in the way we will find means of getting over that."

## [11] RUBBER NEEDS.

**U.S. WAR DEPARTMENT'S ACTION.**

**DEFENCE VIEWPOINT**

*(Reuter's American Service)*

*Washington, Jan. 21.*

Impressed with the gravity of the crude rubber situation from the viewpoint of defence, the War Department has charted four areas where they advocate plantations, namely, central America and Porto Rico (South America), East Africa, and the Philippines.

Mr. H. S. Firestone son of the tyre magnate, is leaving for Africa on Friday via the Far East for the purpose of superintending rubber plantations in Liberia. He said that rubber could be produced in Liberia at a cost of twenty cents per pound.

## [17] NO COAL CRISIS?

**LEADER'S AGAINST STRIKE**

**PRIME MINISTER'S APPEAL.**

*(British Wireless Service.)*

*Rugby, Jan. 21.*

A noticeable feature of recent speeches by miners' leaders is their earnest desire to prevent a strike when, in the month of May, the coal situation will come under review owing to the expiration of present subsidy.

Mr. A. J. Cook, Secretary of the Miners' Federation, has expressed strong hopes that an amicable settlement may be reached; and another member of the Federation, Mr. F. B. Varley, M.P., declared last night that he did not believe that a crisis was inevitable.

A similar view was expressed by the Rt. Hon. W. C. Bridgeman, who is one of the Members of the Cabinet, who took a leading part in the negotiations between the miners, mine owners, and the Government, when the dispute was in its more acute stage some months ago.

The newspapers now forecast that the Prime Minister, in his speech at Sunderland next week, will appear to those directly concerned in the working of the coal industry, to endeavour, as soon as the recommendations of the normal commission are available, to come to an agreement among themselves.

Great Britain pays, three hundred million pounds, or a billion and a half dollars, to her own subjects as interest on her war bonds, and a good measure of this she takes back in taxes. But her total debt service is not one-half her government expenditure. She has a budget of eight hundred million pounds, or nearly four billion dollars. This is much greater than that of the United States.

But she collects it all, and if she has a deficit this year it will be only because she has elected to pay twenty million pounds, or a hundred million dollars, to subsidise her coal interests while she takes time to solve the problem of her fuel supply and throw the Bolsheviks out of the country.

England was the great, war sufferer. She had most to lose in trade, shipping, coal, and manufacturing. But in seven years after the war, she has balanced her budget, advanced the pound sterling by fifty per cent. to its parity with the U.S. gold dollar, settled Ireland, and thrown over free trade.

American motors are not in evidence on English highways as they were a few years ago. Her motor companies, under a thirty-three per cent. protective tariff have sprung to the front and are putting two thousand motors upon the English highways every week. Certainly, from the American standpoint, this is not any evidence of Empire decadence.

This Enquire has not only four-fifths of the rubber and all the nickel that goes into our American motor-cars, but she manufactures at a profit both the lowest priced and the highest priced motor-cars in the world.

Capital is abundant in England and expanding. Blast furnaces are being relighted; pig iron production and steel production are increasing. England does not tax incidental profits arising from enhanced values. Then more than a billion dollars made the past year by the advance in rubber shares will go untaxed and will add to the capital of the country.

There are many factors in English progress. Chief among them are capital, labour, confidence, and security, with wise administration. In every one of these elements England is to-day making progress. Her troubles are behind her. Her standards are set. She is confident of herself and her position.
