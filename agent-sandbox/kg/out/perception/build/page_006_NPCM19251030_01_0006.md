## [13] PUBLIC SERVICE.

Our stipendary magistrates do well, when occasions arise, to pay tribute to those who perform acts of public service. Unfortunately the occasion too seldom recurs. More often than not the individual is moved to action when he has been placed in danger as the result of another's doings, and a prosecution has, more often than not, followed. In the more rare cases where the individual is not involved, but the community as a whole is concerned, the urge to act in the public interest becomes weak and is often ignored. Happily there are exceptions, and presentations made by the Government through the Police Department to those who have served the public interests by acts of personal courage witness to this fact. In other cases, a sense of civic pride has prompted others to take action to prevent wanton damage being done by trespassers, and to spend time in a police court to bear witness to what they have seen. It is this form of public service which is as worthy as any that can be made. If such rare examples could be followed generally, crime statistics and the work of the police would be much lessened, and there would be a saving of the Colony's natural beauty, and a saving in

money, where, as sometimes happens, the work of depredation has to be made good. Whilst we are not advocates of the community forming itself into bands of-what-may be called "Peeping Toms"—there should be a quickening of the civic sense on the part of the members of all the communities. It is time, for instance, that the proper usage of ricshas should be insisted on. There are byelaws, we believe, which decree that they shall be used for no other purpose than that of conveying human freight. Only once do we remember a case being voluntarily brought on a question of an infringement of this regulation. The street fountains are designed to save precious water during a famine. The waste of water on the part of unthinking users is a scandal and a shame. Of the bringing to book of "road hogs" there is nothing much to be said at this late stage. Those who act in a sense of righteous indignation in this connection do a service to the cause of what we may call clean motoring as well as to the general safety of those who have no desire to be propelled out of this world, or seriously injured, by a motor vehicle recklessly driven. Hitherto complaint has been made at the apathy of Chinese in coming forward to testify in cases of robbery and the like. The condemnation has alike been general and uncomplimentary. It has not shown much knowledge of Chinese psychology. It is probably easier to be brave outside a Chinese quarter of the city, than it is to be in one. Instruction in civic duty must come from the "elders" of the Chinese, and we should certainly like to see some action taken in this direction. But we are not certain that the foreign community as a whole accepts the implied obligation of citizenship, by being jealous of the privileges of citizenship.

## [15] SHAW'S WORK

**INTERESTING UNIVERSITY LECTURE**

**PLAYWRIGHT AND JOURNALIST.**

Before a large and very appreciative audience, Prof. Simpson delivered a lecture on Wednesday night in the University Union Assembly Hall on "Bernard Shaw." Prof. J. Fenton presided at the meeting.

In his opening remarks, Prof. Simpson dwelt on the life of the great author who was born in 1856, pointing out his Socialistic tendencies, his joining of the Fabian Society in 1884, and his activities both as a street-orator and as a pamphleteer. He took to journalism and criticised music and pictures. He found this too exacting and turned novelist but this resulted in failure and so he went on to play writing. The theatre having fallen on evil days, producers were looking about for fresh blood and Ibsen succeeded in bringing out his "Doll's House." This gave the stimulus and Shaw came forward with "Widowers' Houses" which ran two nights. In 1892, "The Philanderer" was written, but was found unsuitable for acting. Shaw's idea was to forsake the career of journalism and resort to writing plays of a new type.

## [16] NEW IDEAS

Another reason is that the play is the natural evolution of the essayist—the trouble is that he seldom lives through this because of the strain. Shaw survived and was able to write plays giving the amplest scope for criticism. He aims more at the propaganda of new ideas through his prefaces and explanations of plays than through actors; for he believes theatre-goers are unable to think sufficiently. Careful descriptions of scenes and appearances of actors are given. This style has been rapidly followed by such great writers as Sir James Barrie, Galsworthy and others.

Shaw being a follower of John Stuart Mills, did not believe much in intuition and tried to place before people the bare facts of life. Traditional feelings are attacked bitterly and rudely. Like all Victorians he is bent on forwarding Mill's Doctrine of Investigation.

"UNPLEASANT" PLAYS.
The first plays of Bernard Shaw were called the Unpleasant Plays. In "Widowers' Houses" he tried to treat on the economies of unearned incomes derived mainly from ill-constructed and poorly furnished houses let out to the poor. The socialists liked this but not so the independent income-earners.

“The Philanderer” treats of marriage and is without a plot whereas the third play, “Mrs. Warren's Profession,” attacks the employment of women and the low pay in those days.

In the next series, the Pleasant Plays, “Arms and the Man” and three others, Bernard Shaw was greatly helped by William Archer who gave him good reviews and this seemed to be the bridge that admitted him into West-end London and Society.

## [17] ON MARRIAGE

On marriage, family and sentimental romance Shaw has incurred the hatred of many because of his way of disparaging family sentiments. "The Philanderer," "You Never Can Tell," "Getting Married," Miss Alliance and "Over-ruled" well express his feelings on these matters.
In his ambition to become a philosopher, Shaw wrote, "Man and Superman," "Devil's Disciple," "Showing-up Blanco Postnet," plays dealing with ethical problems. These show the influence of Ibsen and Fabianism. Of the historic plays many mistakes were pointed out in traditional accounts. Here Shaw tries to make history real and living. In this he was foreshadowed by Thackeray. In Caesar and Cleopatra he makes Caesar a wise thinking man, not the great general as he is usually depicted, and Cleopatra a mere flapper. In "The Man of Destiny," Napoleon is pictured as having been aided by good luck in his early campaigns. "The Devil's Disciple" is another of this class.
By making St. Joan the ordinary country girl as she should be, Shaw has gained more in reputation than through all his other plays.

On science Shaw ridicules the modern tendency to take things without investigation. In his themes he tries to make people sceptical. "Back to Methuselah" violates all unities in play writing, commences in 4000 B.C. and ends up in 200 A.D. The theme centres on evolution especially the tendency for laissez faire in mechanical evolution. In the "Doctor's Dilemma" out of which extracts were read by the lecturer, Shaw tries to point out that specialists are ever prone to look for the diseases in which they specialised. Phonetics form the subject of his theme in "Captain Brass Bound's Conversion" and "Pygmalion." On politics he does not seem to have written much. "How she lied to her husband" and "Press Cuttings" are two of his attempts at writing for exercise and amusement.

It is generally conceded in England, Austria and Germany that Shaw is the greatest writer of this age. It must be left to posterity to judge between the respective merits of Shakespeare and Shaw.

## [18] THEOSOPHICAL LECTURE.

**OUR BROTHER MAN.**

At the last Public Meeting of Hongkong Lodge of The Theosophical Society, Mr. H. Lanepart gave an address on "God, our Brother Mum," one of the famous Sydney lectures of C. Jinnanjadhasa. He said in brief: — Looking down the ages, we find that there have been epochs in civilization when mankind for centuries found fascinating interest in the problems of God, of heaven and how to get there, and how man was to save his own soul. There was then very little suggested in the way of change of human institutions, of general improvement, of social reform. Mankind's very great activities then were in connection with the Church. But to-day, and during the last fifty years, a great change has come. Nearly all literature deals with man's activities in one or other of its phases. Suggestion after suggestion for bettering human conditions is there discussed. We see that a new era has begun where man is less interested in God and more in man. And the reason is that man has made a stupendous discovery, which is fundamentally changing his outlook upon life.

In religion man has been told that he was created by God and is therefore God's creature, but to-day he knows that his true relation to God is far more profound. He has discovered that his link with God is not only as a creature of God—a thing of city which God has breathed into and made a living thing, but that he contains within himself Divinity and can manifest it, that man has revealed God and can always reveal Him. This knowledge of the Divinity in man has ever been proclaimed in the sacred mystical techings and is brought out in the Indian philosophy of which it is an integral part. This was also once upon a time the teaching of the western religion of Christianity. To-day, the great Christian ideal is the salvation of the soul, but there were days when a complete ideal was presented—the Deification of the soul, that man should put on a Divine Vesture and partake of the nature of God. This teaching as to the Divinity of Man slipped away from Christianity during the middle ages, but it has come back again.

We find to day that church after church is slowly nibbling at this idea that man exists not "merely to be "saved" but to realise a more intimate relation to God as a Revealer of the divine nature. It is that supreme fact which anyone of you can ascertain for himself if you will step out of the dending grooves of orthodoxy and accept the larger thought represented by Theosophy and the allied movements, which proclaim the new year old idea. There is a new hope for mankind because man is other than we have thought him. As in India since ever the Gurl, the Teacher was God. Incarnate in Greco the Youth and in Europe

In the child and each man and each woman is slowly becoming to us the mirror of God. Mankind is beginning a great search for God in man, and there is scarcely one country in the world where the Theosophical idea of the Brotherhood of Man the Unity of Life and the Omnipresence of God is not becoming powerful in the press, on the platform and in the Churches. And the supreme advantage is that if we can gain some glimpses of God in another we shall realise Him in ourselves.

## [19] RACIAL PREJUDICE

But there are aspects of our civilization which prevent our discovering God our Brother Man, and one of them in the prejudice of race and colour. God cannot be a respecter of persons, the God of the British Empire only. He must have such a tenderness, such a love for all that has come forth from Him that He is the God of ALL mankind. And if there is One God there is but One Humanity, and the distinctions of race and creed mean nothing at all to God. So then, if you would find God, you must tear this prejudice of race and colour from your eyes. Do not turn your back on life in any of its phases. The presence of God, and He alone, is in the lowest, in scenes of suffering, ignorance, degradation, as well as in the great and glorious deeds of Humanity. That does not mean that you must yourself sin, succumb to temptation. You cannot help the weaker if you sin as he does. But look at the weaker—the younger in the manifestation of the God within—not as having less of the divine nature, but as in some way struggling to rise out of the mire into the full realisation of his own Divinity. Do not stand away from him, because his robe is muddy and yours is clean. Rather be contaminated by his mud as you help him, because you are strong and he is weak, be one with him in your common Divinity. In these ways you will discover God our Brother Man, and the supreme wonder of life is this, that out of a million faces, out of a thousand races, the great mystery, the wonder of God, is manifested. Open your eyes, my brothers, look at the streets, look at the faces of men, break through every prejudice which you have, look with clear vision on your brothers who are suffering, who are toiling, look on your brothers who are achieving, and you will see in their faces, and in the faces of little children. God our Brother Man.
