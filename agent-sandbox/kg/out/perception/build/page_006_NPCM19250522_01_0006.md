## [14] SANE RENTS POLICY:

However much tenants affected by the Rents Ordinance may be inclined to grumble at the increase of fifteen per cent. permitted under the new Bill, the first reading of which was passed by the Legislative Council yesterday, there must be general agreement with the equity of the measure as a whole. It would be difficult to find legislation affecting tenants based on such a keen sense of justice and protection. Everything that would even unconsciously play into the hands of the landlords of the unscrupulous type, who are naturally found in Hongkong as well as elsewhere, has been carefully eliminated. The Shylock and the usurer will get short shrift on conviction of any breaches of the new Ordinance when placed formally upon the Statute-book.
Benevolent legislation of this kind makes for the Government of the day many friends even amongst habitual critics. Thus, even in the provision for an increase of rent, it is wisely provided that it may not be operative at the roll blow that not all landlords are bad landlords to demonstrated from the remark of the Attorney General that landlords might not wish to increase

fifteen per cent. all at one time." And so both parties to the tenancy will be met by the permission to make the increase operative by easy stages, always provided that it does not exceed the fifteen per cent. over the standard rate. Then, again, the leasor will be obliged to give the lessee two weeks' notice in writing of his intention to increase the rent, at the same time stating the standard rent.

Another vital clause in the new Bill is the penalty providing against the practice, reported to exist here to a certain extent, of threatening a tenant with reconstruction unless he pays more rent than is recoverable under the Ordinance. Thus once again is the tenant's interest made the primary object of the new measure.

On the general subject of rents restriction and the wisdom of ultimately abolishing such here, there cannot but be sympathy expressed with the remarks of the Attorney-General as reported elsewhere in this issue. Under normal conditions there ought to be no need for rents restriction. It is economically unsound except in times of special stress, but so long as chaotic conditions prevail in China, resulting in streams of refugees coming to Hongkong, it would not be good business to abolish rents restriction. The good landlords have, perforce, to carry the burdens imposed by the bad and the vicious. The community must be protected from the evil practices of the latter, no matter what their nationality or class may be.

Furthermore, as Sir Henry Pollock pointed out, a repeal of the Rents Ordinance might create a considerable dislocation of business. At a time when the trading community is feeling the pinch of hard times, there certainly must be no thought of hasty legislation that would tend to accentuate the position. It will be seen therefore that the problem of "getting out" of rents restriction is a much more serious one than many people may have imagined. It has to come about in such a way as not to cause any serious dislocation either to the wage carrier or to the owner of business premises.

## [16] White Elephant Railway.

It seems an extraordinary fact that while land is not plentiful on the island, but is in Kowloon and the New Territories, the Government has consistently persevered in its determination to house most of its servants in Hongkong. A policy of housing in and beyond Kowloon, away into the New Territories, would have had one effect. It would have popularised the "waste places" of the Colony. It would have induced others to reside there, and to have made of these places the "lungs" that the Colony so badly needs. This admitted, a contention which follows must find acceptance. A populous New Territories would have proved a clientelle for the railway. That, at the moment, must be regarded as the Government's "White elephant." There is no sense of satisfaction in being told that in 1924 $341,645 was saved because a larger grant for rolling stock and constructional works was only partly expended. The huge debt owing by what was once described as "My Government" continues. With no desire to be "We told you so prophets" let us confess that we think this will continue for many years to come. Meanwhile expenditure races and overtakes income The Government is probably sorry it ventured in this "socialistic" enterprise. Whether it is sorry or not it has to be pointed out that apparently it had no faith in the land through which the railway passes; or, if this is somewhat extreme, that it had not the foresight to make a part of its own housing policy operate in the New Territories, thus relieving the situation in Hongkong and adding further to its revenue from land sales; and above all, helping the railway if even only to a small extent.

## [17] ARMS CHARGE.

An unemployed Chinese was charged at the Kowloon Magistracy yesterday with unlawful possession of a revolver and 200 rounds of ammunition.

The case for the prosecution was that the accused was searched by two Indian guards near the gangway of the President Van Buren on the evening of May 11 and that the arms were found concealed round the waist.

Only one of the Indian guards being present to give evidence, Mr. M. K. Lo (for the defence) deferred cross-examination of this witness until the next hearing a week hence.

## [19] COWARDICE.

(Captain Symons won the V.C. at Gallipoli, where he served with the Australian forces. He is now in charge of the staff canteen at Wembley.)

I am in favour of the death penalty, and think it is absolutely necessary to preserve discipline in time of war. It is true the death penalty was never enforced in the Australian Expeditionary Forces, but I am sure the majority of the troops were in favour of it. A coward is a man who deliberately shrinks from danger. A man came to me on the Peninsula and said he had been shot through the head. I asked him to show me the wound, but, of course, he could not. I believe he was convinced he was shot, but he could not help himself. He was morally and physically a weakling, but hardly a coward deserving the strictest military measure. I have often been frightened myself, and wished I was well out of it. If I had given in I should have been a coward.

## [21] JOANNA SOUTHCOTT'S BOX.

Human "nature loves a story of a box, remarks a London paper. For that black box which contained the marriage lines of Charles II. and Lucy Walters there was, though human eye never saw it, a huge vogue. The safe of Mandame Humbert commanded the credibility of men of money. And from generation to generation rational people have put their faith in the box of Joanna Southcott. Now we hear that somebody in Hammersmith has opened it. This seems to be an advance on all previous narratives, for hitherto the precise situation of the box and the name and address of anybody who has seen it have been hidden from us. The tradition is that when Joanna died in 1814 she left behind her a box which, as she said, contained divinely inspired writings, destined to help England in a time of danger, and which was only to be opened in the presence of twenty-four bishops. There is nothing improbable in this. It is just the sort of thing Joanna would have said. Further stories state that the box is now in the West of England guarded by four faithful disciples of Miss Southcott, and that there has been an attempt not wholly successful to muster the prescribed number of bishops for the opening ceremony. The gentleman at Hammersmith says that this box, black and coffin-shaped — which is a good touch—contained a ringlet of grey hair, a Bible of 1702, and a document signed "Joanna Southcott," the operative clause of which is this: "Bones of unknown creatures, as many as there are bishops, shall be dug out of the earth, and monsters of a lost world shall live and men shall be made to wonder." This does not seem to contain any

them, and some, as we are credibly informed, still do. Jonna Southcott was a Devonshire girl, born in 1750. She had a good many wooers, but "remained single. Until she was turned forty she worked as shop girl and servant, with a blameless character. She was rather more than normally given to religious observances. Besides attending every service at Exeter Cathedral, she was regular at Methodist meetings, till she surprised one of them by a confused statement that Providence had sent her to Exeter. The meeting was not inclined to agree with her. She went away, had ten days with the powers of darkness, and began to write prophecies in queer rhyme and queerer prose. Her sister told her she was losing her wits, but people who were not relations were more respectful. In ten years she had gathered many disciples from all over England, and issued to them sealed papers, which were to ensure their salvation. Whether she charged for these documents, and how much she charged, is disputed. The only difficult effort of faith required of her followers was to believe in her. The theology which she taught was otherwise orthodox. But in 1813, being then a woman of more than 00, she announced that she was about to give birth to a second Christ. She shut herself up, her disciples ordered a crib to cost £200 and spent £100 on paper spoons. At the end of 1814 she died of dropsy. There is obviously no reason to suppose that she was consciously a chent. Her fervent enthusiasm probably imposed on herself as well as on men of better education and better brains. But most from this quality of personal fervour there seems to have been nothing which we can now descry in Joanna.
