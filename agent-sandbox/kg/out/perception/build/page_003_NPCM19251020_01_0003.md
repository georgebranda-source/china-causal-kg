The movement now visible in China is part of a world-wide stirring amongst the "coloured" races against white domination. Whether the Chinese will continue to lend themselves to be used for this purpose long enough to suit the designs of Moscow is a doubtful question. In the case of Japan's awakening she proved her capacity to develop along the lines chosen by her, retaining foreign assistance up to the point at which it could be dispensed with safely. No discrimination was exercised against any particular European nation, and an absence of violence characterised the proceedings. Japan's circumstances were, and still are, vastly different from those of China. The latter country, with her vast area and population of over 400 millions, offers a wide field for industrial development. The construction of railways, the opening up of the great coalfields and other mineral resources would give lucrative employment to many Chinamen now on the verge of starvation, and there are extensive areas in the west which could be made productive by cultivation on up-to-date lines. The men who have made such a tragic muddle of splendid opportunities in Russia are not likely to lead the Chinese along a safe path to national regeneration. Japan could do so with a fair amount of success, but she would be certain to exact a heavy price. Britain, which is being assailed most vigorously of all the supposed enemies of the "New China," is the best qualified to be the friend of that country. If the present attitude of certain sections of the Chinese people is persisted in, it will be a tragic blunder from the point of view of the whole country, and the consequences will be regretted in days to come when it is too late. There is enough intellect amongst the Chinese people to avert such a cost of strenge if it is properly brought and led in the right direction.

surprise—and disappointment—of the appearance of lady pirates in the devious and muddy estuaries of chaotic China.

This is no development, however. Lady pirates have ever been a commonplace institution of the Far East, though their functions are less specialised than those of their male colleagues. Theirs is a pleasantly diversified and domesticated piracy—throat-slitting, rocking the cradle, setting captured junks ablaze, cooking lunch, opium smuggling, mahjongh, and the throwing overboard of slaughtered victims, interspersed with a quiet half-hour or so of gossip with the other girls, and a shampoo. True words, if spoken fippantly, may I assure you. There are numbers of lady pirates, too, among the ranging proas haunting the remoter byways of the Malay Archipelago, worthy wives and mothers, good plain cooks, skilled needle-women and navigators, deadly wielders of the dirk and most unpleasantly efficient with the cutlass.

Mary's "mother," who was married to a sailor, bore a son shortly after her husband left England on a voyage from which he was destined not to return. When the boy was a year old the mother took him away with her from her late husband's prosperous relations, with whom she had been given a home, for a stay with her own friends, who were poor. While away, the boy baby died. The mother, loth to risk being cast off by the family that had been pensioning her, adopted a girl baby—Mary—dressed her in boy's clothes and passed her off as a lad.

When her mother died, Mary, who was thirteen, got a job as boy attendant upon a French lady. Her next job was that of cabin boy on a man-of-war, after which she turned up in Flanders as a cadet in an infantry regiment. Failing to land a commission, she changed over to the cavalry and was getting on very nicely when disaster befell her. She fell in love with an army comrade. They both left the army a few months later and were married. Her husband lived only a short time. After his death Mary, impatient with domesticity, dressed again as a man, went to Holland and joined an infantry regiment. It bored her. She joined a ship sailing for the West Indies which fell into the hands of British pirates who fired her enthusiasm for the career. She joined the gang.

For a while the pirates, taking advantage of a royal amnesty, lived peaceably ashore. But money began to run low. Faced with the dreadful prospect of honest and ill-requited toil, they made strenuous efforts to secure some employment that would be more congenial and lucrative. For a while Captain Rackam's luck held. Then came an ill-fated morn when a British man-o-war caught him red-handed, "with the goods." The deck of the privateer became so palpably unhealthy an abiding place that everyone scuttled below, except Mary Read and Anne Bonny, with one male pirate (who was probably weak in the head). The man-o-war captured the privateer and the naval captain expressed to Mary his surprise that she should embrace a career that meant her going to the gallows if captured. She replied that all true pirates approved of that risk, as it kept out craven tramps and cowardly miff-raff generally from the profession.
Anne, too, gave her erstwhile lover a piece of her mind just before he met his end. Captain Rackam having been admitted to mission to see her on the morn of his execution, Anne remarked crisply that he fought like a man he would not be hanged like a dog.
