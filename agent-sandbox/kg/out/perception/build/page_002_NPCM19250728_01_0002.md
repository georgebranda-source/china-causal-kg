## [10] LETTERS TO A LORD.

**A BLACK MAILER SENT TO**

Sentence of nine months imprisonment in the second division was passed by Mr. Justice Salter, at the Central Criminal Court on June 18 on Samuel Arthur Taylor, 25, inquiry agent; who pleaded "Guilty" to a charge of attempting to obtain money from Lord Terrington by threatening to publish libels on Lord and Lady Terrington.

Mr. Percival Clarke, for the prosecution, said the first letter was sent by the prisoner to Lord Terrington on May 11, and in it he asked for £95, pretending that he had got information in his possession for which he had received an offer from some other person, and he asked that if Lord Terrington decided to have the information, withheld he should place a visiting card in his window. Lord Terrington saw the police on the matter, and, acting on their instructions, put a card in his window, and later sent a letter to the address which the prisoner gave. The prisoner was arrested. He had since written from Brixton tendering his most sincere apologies to Lord and Lady Terrington. Counsel said the prisoner had no information at all which could reflect discredit on Lord or Lady Terrington, and this was merely an attempt on his part to extort money. Nothing was known against prisoner's character. This was not a sudden temptation on the part of a man who wanted money. When the prisoner's rooms were visited by the police they found that other letters had been written by him in the name of Watson in December, 1923, to Lord Terrington, in which he offered to sell Lord Terrington a book for £5. Lord Terrington did not know anything about this book or Watson, and he declined to buy the book. The prisoner stated that he was engaged to Lady Terrington's sister, but that was quite untrue. Flooring his sister he got to know Lady Terrington's sister. They were quite young. When Lady Terrington got to know of the acquaintance she threatened to make her sister a ward in Chaneery if it continued. That seemed to have upset the prisoner, and might explain why he wrote the letters.
Mr. Mafelom Carter, addressing the judge, on behalf of the prisoner, said he had not been in trouble before. He really could not account for why he wrote the letters. It was a cross and foolish mistake, and he desired to express his contribution.

Mr. Justice Salter, in passing sentence, said that blackmail was one of the vilest crimes known to the law. Lord and Lady Terrington had had the courage to come forward and discharge a public duty. Victims of blackmailers had not always that courage, and it was on that that blackmailers thrived. It had been called a foolish mistake on the prisoner's part, but he (Mr. Justice Salter) did not believe a word of that. It was a regular blackmailing scheme.

## [12] MR. EPSTEIN'S PANEL

**SYMPATHY OF MR. BERNARD SHAW.**

Home papers contain much criticism of Mr. Epstein's panel in Hyde Park, one of the latest being that by G. Bernard Shaw, who writes in "The Times" as follows:—

On visiting the Bird Sanctuary on Saturday I found my way by an eloquently trampled path to a crowded railing, where I was confronted with a very remarkable sample—I use the word advisedly—of the great art of monumental wall sculpture which we rather feebly call bas relief. It was unquestionably the real thing, with all the power of stone and all the illusion of strenuous passion, and even movement, that live design can give. But it was a sample, and a ridiculously small one. It reminded me of a Quaker cloth merchant in my native Dublin, famous for the imperturbability of his temper. A young English officer, after making him take down every roll in his shop, at last selected a cloth, and gravely said he would take a halfpenny-worth of it. The unmoved Quaker cut out a piece the exact size of the halfpenny, wrapped it neatly in paper, and handed it respectfully to his customer.

It is evident that the Hudson memorialists, having collected what they could, were compelled to go to Mr. Epstein and say, "Please, sir, mother England wants four yards of your best monumental wall sculpture to put up in the Park for one of her famous sons." And Mr. Epstein solemnly delivered the four yards, which bear about the same proportion to what was needed as the Quaker's halfpennyworth of cloth to a suit of clothes. That is what comes of ordering a monument when you have only money enough to pay for a Christmas card. We get a monument to our national meanness in matters of high art. If it had been Wembley now! Next time, if we cannot afford to give Mr. Epstein carte blanche, we had better get the job done in the Euston Road in a thoroughly commercial manner.

I have a great deal of sympathy with the people who hate Mr. Epstein's sample. Some of them feel the reproach and the inadequacy for which they innocently blame Mr. Epstein. Some of them do not like monumental wall sculpture, and are in the grievous position of people who want a fox-trot and have a Beethoven symphony thrust into their ears. Why should not these people, who have a perfect right to their own tastes, have a monument and a bird sanctuary all to themselves? There is plenty of room in the Park for both. It need not cost much. There is a process called photo-sculpture, with an establishment in the West-End, by which very pretty reliefs can be made by the camera. If Miss Fay Compton or Miss Gladys Cooper would pose as Rima, with a stuffed pigeon on each wrist, the artist who touches up the photo-sculpture could throw in a few swallows, a robin, and a holly branch; and the result would be exactly what is wanted by the honest folk whose sense of beauty is outraged and mocked by Mr. Epstein's powerful proceedings. Why not please everybody when it is so easy?

### ཡོད་པའི་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ཡོད་པ་ The first Victoria Cross, ever awarded is a priceless relic left under the will of Mrs. Frances Russell Lucas, of Great Culverden, Tunbridge Wells, whose estate was proved at £10,637.

Mrs. Lucas was the widow of Admiral Charles David Lucas, who was the first man in either Navy or Army to win the V.C.

This was gained on June 20, 1854, when H.M.S. Hecla was bombarding Bomursunda fortress, in the Baltic. When the Hecla was within 500 yards of the principal fort a live shell, with the fuse burning, fell on the deck.

Lucas, who was then a'mite, picked up the shell in an instant and hurled it into the sea. Almost before it reached the water it exploded with a terrific roar.

Captain Hall of the Hecla, immediately promoted Lucas a lieutenant and three years later Queen Victoria decorated him with the Victoria Cross.
