## [12] *(no headline: column 2)*

"Cash on delivery" seems to be coming at last. It may mean much to British agriculture or very little, according to the scale of charges. England differs from most others in not having to seek any export market for its agricultural produce. In consequence, there is no sufficient incentive to co-operation among farmers for the purpose of marketing, which abroad includes the organisation of transport. Here, every farmer likes to sell independently if he can; preferably at the neighbouring market town. His stuff goes to the big cities, through the hands of a host of middlemen, which accounts for the much advertised disparity between what the producer gets and the consumer pays. At the same time a large trade is done direct, especially by small-holders, who contrive to deliver small consignments of butter, eggs, etc., to their personal acquaintances in town, on credit. This trade is severely restricted by the high rates of the parcels post. Cash on delivery might stimulate small-holdings enormously, but only if the rates are considerably reduced.

## [14] SHIPBUILDING PRICES

### —

With regard to the statement in the House of Commons recently that shipbuilding material has been sold by British works to Continental shipbuilders at 30s. per ton below the price ruling at home, the Press Association has authority from the British works in question to state that the actual difference in the price was merely 2s. 9d. per ton between the price delivered to the Dutch shipyard (which is on the coast and in a position to take the material free alongside yard) and the price delivered to shipbuilders on the north-east, const. The British quotation for these ships was stated to be £12,000 per ship above the Dutch. The tonnage of plates and sections in each ship is about 3,000. Thus the difference in the cost of the plates and sections accounts for only £412 per ship against the £12,000 above referred to. It will be remembered in connection with the ships recently placed by the Furness Company with a German shipbuilding concern that the difference between the British price per ship and the German was -tated by Sir Frederick Lewis to be no less than £60,000. These were similar sized bonds to the above. Consequently, if the plates and sections had been delivered free of charge this would not have bridged one-half the difference. It is not to the manufacturer of plates and sections that shipbuilders should look for relief in costs. Slopbuilders would be the first to admit that the prices of steel plates and sections, which are now at about 30 per cent, above pre-war prices, are cheaper in comparison than any other substantial item in their present costs.

## [15] KRAMERY

### 1

The Maypole. Dairy repout for the year 1924 is a disappointing one. At £218,341, the net profit is less than half last year's £439,793, which, in turn, was some £34,000 lower than that for 1922. The result is that the holders of the 21,583,000 deferred shares of 2s. each receive no dividend, whereas for 1923 an interim dividend of 3d. per share was paid on the deferred capital. In that year, however, the Board found it necessary to provide out of the reserve a sum of £250,000, or very nearly the amount paid in deferred dividend, to write off losses incurred in the West African business, so that if the fact be taken into account that last year no such draft was necessary the comparison with the previous period becomes somewhat less unfavourable.
Out of the present profits £10,000 is, in fact, placed to reserve and £10,000 to income tax reserve, as against only one appropriation of £10,000 last year, and after providing for the preference and preferred ordinary dividends there remains £137,387 to be carried forward, as against £49,576 brought in. The cash position is not a very strong one, but the directors say that during the second half of last year trading showed considerable improvement, and that they have every reason to anticipate a return to more normal conditions, in the current year.

## [16] BLUE

**RECKITTS 250,000) FOR EMPLOYEES**

### —

The shareholders of Rockitt and Sons, of Hull, who are manufacturers of starch, etc., approved of £80,000 being distributed among the employees as a prosperity bonus. This works out at 1s. 6d in the pound on salaries and wages, plus an extra allowance according to the number of years' service. The shares in the appropriation—which is £25,000 larger than that made a year ago—are 5,000 persons in Hull and 1,000 elsewhere.
The meeting also agreed to a final dividend of 1s. 9d. per share on the ordinary, making 4s., or 20 per cent., for the year, as against 171 per cent. for 1923. The report stated that the sales of the company's products continued to expand, and last year's profits were the largest it had ever made. It was decided to transfer £282,532 to the reserve, making that fund £1,500,000, and £248,080 was carried forward.

## [18] *(no headline: column 4)*

The Board of Trade's import and export returns for March make cotton manufacturing and the production of machinery appear to be highly prosperous industries. That, however, is only because the comparisons are with the trade done in a time of great depression. Cotton yarns and manufactures total nearly £10 millions, which is an increase of £3,108,000 on the corresponding month of 1924 and £4,426,000 on 1923. Three things have to be remembered, however—the first that the Egyptian cotton was about 1s.6d. per pound dearer during part of the month, although American was about 3d. per pound cheaper, the second that a large part of the exports represented orders received before Christmas, when trade was better, and the third that, even with the aid of these old orders, the mills spinning American cotton were as to rule only running 35 hours a week, where they were running at all, large numbers of operatives being on the dole. Proportionately, the machinery trade's figures look better still, with an increase of £954,000, the total being £4,172,000, but if we turn to the 1021 returns we find that the corresponding amount then was nearly £7,300,000. The coal trade, on the other hand, shows a decline of £1,883,000 from 1924 and £4,261,000 from 1923, the comparison being with a time when the French occupation of the Ruhr had caused the dislocation of the coal industry there. Last month Germany only received from the United Kingdom 336,000 tons, or barely more than half the amount last year, and little more than one-sixth of that in 1923. French demand has also fallen off materially, though not so stiffly as German.

## [21] *(no headline: column 5)*

Mr. Briggs, retiring president of the Manchester Wholesale Provision Association, suggests that the Government compel all imported butter to be packed in 56lb. boxes, instead of in tubs.

He points out that, until recently, Continental butter fetched 8d, a lb. more than New Zealand's best, though there was no differences in value, and says that traders explain the difference in price as due solely to the women of certain districts, who do not believe they are getting their money's worth unless they see the butter ladled out of tubs, and always think the best butter comes in tubs. It is impossible to change these old-established habits.

Mr. Briggs adds that New Zealand cannot ship butter in tubs owing to freight charges and the difficulties of stowing.
