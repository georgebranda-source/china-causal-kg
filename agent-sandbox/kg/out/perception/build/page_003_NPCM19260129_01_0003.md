## [2] THE FLOWER SHOW

**DECISION TO HOLD IT IN MARCH.**

### 一

The annual show of the Hong Kong Horticultural Society will be held on March 11, it was decided at the annual meeting yesterday.

Referring to the falling off in the membership from 237 to 175. Mr. J. A. Plummer, the Chairman, said that he believed that one reason for this falling off was the fact that the subscription was small—only $5 per annum—and in consequence people forgot or did not bother to pay. He was asked by the Hon. Treasurer to ask members, particularly those in outlying districts, to send in their subscriptions.

Members must hear in mind that without members they had no subscriptions, and without subscriptions there could be no show. The show itself did not pay, and exclusive of prizes, cost annually about $1,500 against an income of between $300 and $350.

As each member received two tickets for his or her subscription of $5, they could hardly expect to make much out of the gate receipts, but this did not matter so long as they had a full list of members.

He thought first year's show was generally voted a success, and the number of entries showed a satisfactory increase and the number of exhibitors was two more than in 1923. He would however like to see, a further increase in the number of exhibitors and some new blood.

He was sure there were quite a number of people who could exhibit, but did not, simply because they forgot to enter in time, did not think they had anything good enough or because it was too much bother.

Jile would like to offer a special prize this year for people who had never exhibited before, if the new committee could frame a rule which would specially encourage the small gardener.

Mr. R. A. Nicholson carried off the Challenge Cup for cut flowers open to all fast year with an excellent exhibit of roses. It was well deserved win upon which he was sure they all congratulated him.

Mr. Elwinmer added that it gave him great pleasure to announce that Mr. Nicholson had kindly offered to present the Society with a new Challenge Cup to take the place of the one he had won outright.

## [4] MURDER ECHO.

**AMERICAN ARMY OFFICHER TO HANG?**

**DECISION BEFORE PRESIDENT.**

### (United Press.)

"Lieutenant Thompson will hang" is the word that passed around the War Department to-day when the army board of review sent the court martial conviction of the Fort McKinley prisoner to the White House for final action. In spite of a concerted effort by friends and relatives of Thompson who have the sentence changed to life imprisonment, it is understood on good authority that the board recommended no change in the findings of the court martial—namely—that Lieut. Thompson was guilty of murder in the first degree when he fired five bullets at 16-year-old Audrey Burleigh at Manila, on April 6, 1925.

It was stated that executive elementary by the president was the only thing that would save Thompson's life, and this is unlikely in the opinion of those who know something of the president's viewpoint. The president will probably review the case without delay but it is not expected that his decision will be known before the last of February.

## [8] OLDHAM CRASH.

**LIFE BAVINGS GONE.**

The financial collapse of the Belgrave Mills Company, Limited, Oldham, of which Mr. William Greenwood, M.P., for Stockport was managing director until shortly before his death last August, involving a loss said to amount to £3,500,000, and of the Belgrave Standard Tyres Company, with a £2,000,000 loss, have brought run to many in Oldham.

"It is a calamity," said one of those involved to a reporter, "biggar than any that has ever descended on this town... There have been times of acute depression before and calls on capital, but Lancashire has never known anything to equal the Belgrave crash. It touches every part of Oldham, from those who have been publicly mentioned as having lost between £70,000 and £25,000 down to the little people whose all was £50 or £100 and who had it in the Belgrave concerns."

Here are a few instances, for which the informant vouched, illustrating the kind of tragedy that will be common in the town:—

A retired mill manager living on his investments will be bankrupt and virtually ruined by the share calls of the company, which are to be $24 on every $25 share spread over the next eight months.

A widow of very modest means to whom a wealthy relative made a gift of £1,000 losses half of it in the company.

A small tradesman who had accumulated $7,000 by many years of hard work and by his successful "boom" speculations had lost every penny of it either as loan holder or shareholder in the Belgrave.

Two small tradesmen loss savings of £1,400 and £900 respectively.

Employees at the mill who had put all their savings into it—us is common in Oldham—lose every penny.

Already demands for the first £1 of the unpaid £4 of share capital are being received. Many are unable to meet the first call, and few will be able to meet all the four £1 calls in eight months.

It is being suggested that the banks should grant a moratorium to save such people from destitution.

Southport, Blackpool, St. Annes, and Morecambe have their victims among the shareholders, while it is reported that creditors in Liverpool suffer to the extent of £800,000.

## [11] EVOLUTION OF MAN

**THE RACE IN ITS INFANCY.**

There was a large congregation at the luncheon hour service at Christ Church, Westminster, for an address by Sir Oliver Lodge on the subject of the evolution of man.

Sir Oliver said the order of evolution was first some grade of plant, then water beasts, then land beasts. The first truly land animals were lizards or reptiles, come of which took to the air and became birds, while others developed into mammals, which were in the line of human ancestry and were comparatively late-comers on this planet. It was noteworthy that no special day was allotted in the Genesis story to the creation of man. He was represented as originating in the evening of the day on which the mammals originated, the special distinction given to him being not in a special variety of form but in the soul. His superiority and intelligence were indicated by the dominion, granted to him. Thus man was raised far above his bodily ape-like ancestors and felt like a new creation. We could not really follow the whole process of man's evolution, which must have been very gradual; but at some stage a special kind

It was not easy to specify at what stage the developing creature might be called human, but he thought all agreed that it was only a case of thousands of years—perhaps 50,000, perhaps 100,000, but certainly a short time. So far as humanity was concerned, we were still in the morning of the times and the human race was still in its infancy. The human race began but a short time ago and might have millions of years before it on this planet.

Going on to speak of the development of the sense of free will, Sir Oliver said it probably arose very gradually, but we might conceive its apprehension by some early genius of the race whom we might regard as "the first man," whether we called him Adam or any other name. He said the story of the fall in the third chapter of Genesis was a crude legend, but it contained a great truth. The free will, being was liable to fall from his highest eminence, and, as all Christians believed, a lofty Being ultimately took flesh and dwelt among us to help humanity and share its weaknesses and burden. We could not examine the Incarnation in the light of science. Apart from all puzzles of theology we were immortal beings with a great destiny before us, and were surrounded by a host of helpers with whom, indeed, I know that it is possible to have communion.
