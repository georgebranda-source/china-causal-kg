The use of the motor bike in rounding up criminals is not unknown. Shepherding alleged lawbreakers by the same means is probably also not unknown. At any rate the unusual sight of a man being "walked off" to a police station with an inspector behind on a slow-moving motor cycle was witnessed this morning at Causeway Bay. The Shanghai Yacht Club have been trying hard to “carry on as usual,” but with the Commodore out with the Twelve-bore Company, the secretary doing duty with the Shanghai Scottish and the blue burgee with a Maltese Cross being represented in every unit of the defence forces, it has been found quite impossible to muster enough skippers for a race. In a foreword to the first issue of Safety First, Sir W. Joynson-Hicks points out that in Britain alone half a million persons are killed or injured annually at a cost of some £50,000,000. Industry is chiefly responsible for the enormous casualty bill, but, contrary to the general opinion, the vast majority of industrial accidents are not due to machinery and cannot be obviated by the provision of greater or more efficient guarding. "They are due," Sir William says, "to the failure of the human element. It will suffice to say that several industrial firms, who have gone in for organised accident prevention, work, have effected reductions of between 25 and 75 per cent. in their accidents. These results are achieved by educating the worker on the subject of accident prevention."

## [13] SACRED PLACE.

**SOME NEW FINDS IN MESOPOTAMIA.**

In a statement from Bughdad, dated March 8, and issued by the Director of the British Museum recently, the discovery of a remarkable work of art in stone is recorded. Funds of the joint expedition of the British Museum and of the Museum of the University of Pennsylvania ran out, but, by the generosity of friends in Britain and Philadelphia, the work was carried on for a month longer, and this last month produced the most interesting results and the finest objects of the whole year.

"We have found," proceeds the statement, "strewn over the floor of a courtyard which we were laboriously clearing, fragments, themselves large enough to be rekonced as monuments, of one of the greatest and most splendid works of art in stone that Mesopotamia has yet produced. Last year we laid bare the Ziggurat of Ur, the huge tower of the Moon God set up by King Ur-Engur about 2,300 years before Christ; now we have, beautifully carved in relief upon a limestone slab which when complete was 5ft. across and nearly 15ft. high, the portrait of its builder and his own record of its conception and achievement.

In one scene the King received from his god the order to build the tower; the god holds out to him the rod and line of the architect, the measuring reed, and the flaxen line with which Ezekiel, an exile by the waters of Babylon, saw planned out the city and temple of his dreams. In another scene Ur-Engur shows his obedience by appearing before the god carrying all the tools of the mason, ready himself to lay the first brick of the Ziggurat; in another, of which unfortunately but a few small fragments remain, we see the actual construction in progress, with the builders carrying the mortar up ladders which are set against the unfinished walls. In other scenes the King celebrates other of his good deeds; he was a great digger of canals, some for irrigation only, others for trade, that ships might pass from Ur to the Persian Gulf and take toll of the Arabian coast; the list of such canals is written on the stone, and the blessing that they brought to the people is symbolically shown by scenes wherein the King stands in prayer before the god, and above his head angels fly downwards holding out vases from which streams of water descend upon the earth. Scenes of sacrifice and of music illustrate the piety and the triumphs of the great founder of the Third Dynasty of Ur. Broken as it is and in parts much damaged, this stela ranks as one of the two finest works of Sumerian art known, and in dramatic interest is surpassed by none.

The discovery was made in the courtyard of E-dublnalakh, one of the most important of the ancient shrines of Ur. It has now been cleared down to the pavements laid by Kuri-galzu, King of Babylon about the sixteenth century B.C., and only the Ziggurat itself is a more imposing ruin. Through side chambers and gateways which still stand to over the height of a man the visitor passes into a great paved court, at one end of which the little shrine rises high on its pedestal of panelled brickwork to dominate the buildings all around. From a corner of the court a flight of steps leads up to the terrace on which the Ziggurat is built.
