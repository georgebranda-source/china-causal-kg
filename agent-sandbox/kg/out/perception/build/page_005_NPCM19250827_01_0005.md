## [1] FRANCE'S DEBT.

**BRITISH CABINET'S DECISION:**

**THE DIFFICULTIES OF THE SITUATION.**

*London, August 26.*

There was a surprisingly large attendance at to-day's special meeting of the Cabinet, considering the holidays.

Prior to the Cabinet meeting M. Caillaux visited Mr. Austen Chamberlain at the Foreign Office. Mr. Chamberlain had previously lengthily conversed with the American Ambassador, it is believed not merely as regards Debt Negotiations, but the Security Pact and the situation in China.

While Mr. Houghton has not been instructed to participate in the debts discussions, he is making unofficial enquiries as to their progress.

M. Caillaux, this afternoon interviewed Mr. Montagu Norman and Mr. Reginald McKenna.

The Government has authorised Mr. Winston Churchill finally to offer a settlement of the French debt on the basis of 62 annual payments of £12,500,000 on the sole responsibility of France subject to an agreement on details and governing the condition of proportionate and equal treatment by France of her creditors. M. Caillaux reserved his opinion but promised to submit the proposals to the French Government.

## [2] A Moratorium.

<sub>section under [1] FRANCE'S DEBT.</sub>

Mr. Churchill, commenting on the official statement embodying the offer, said negotiations had not been broken off.

M. Caillaux, in a statement says Mr. Churchill agreed to concede a partial moratorium until 1930 but only partly accepted by the French who demand this stipulation which M. Caillaux opined is essential to any arrangement. The transfer of annual payments should be suspended in the event of their threatening to disturb the exchanges.

M. Caillaux expressed the opinion so that guarantees should be given that France should in nowise have to pay the allied and associated Powers higher sums than she received from Germany.

He remarked that conversations were merely interrupted and he did not doubt there would be a final conclusion to an arrangement.

## [3] France's Responsibility

<sub>section under [1] FRANCE'S DEBT.</sub>

An official statement embodying the British Debt offer says negotiations entered a new phase three weeks ago when the Cabinet authorised Mr. Churchill to propose to the French Government that France's net obligation should in principle, be halved, which involved the payment of £16,000,000 annually during 62 years of British payments to America.

The British proposal contemplated a portion of the French payment being dependent on the yield of the Duwes annuities, and it was in response to this offer that M. Caillaux visited London where it was proposed that France pay in principle a third of her debt to Britain which means 62 annuities of £10,000,000 on France's sole responsibility.

## [4] Britain's Concern.

<sub>section under [1] FRANCE'S DEBT.</sub>

Though the proposal is a noteworthy advance on previous French suggestions, the gap between the two proposals remains wide and unbridged.

The position is further complicated by impending negotiations of the payment of the French debt to America on which Britain at the outset made it perfectly clear that Britain must receive from France proportionate partipation payments. France might make to America. In this respect therefore, and, Franco-British agreement could only be provisional pending the outcome of other negotiations. Britain however is deeply concerned in the revival of the appeasement of Europe, and felt bound to respond to the ally's offer and authorised Mr. Churchill to make the offer already cabled.

## [6] RUSSIAN TRADE.

**PLACING ORDERS IN ENGLAND**

**T'S X'FILM MACEINURY WANTRD.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*London, August 20:*

A delegation from the Textile Syndicate of Russia expect to conclude in a few days negotiations with leading Lancashire firms for textile machinery, after which some proceed to France whose Minister of Commerce they say offered them every assistance in the matter of credits. Others remain in London to continue negotiations with Lancashire and Yorkshire firms.

## [7] FRENCH REVERSE.

**STRONG POSITIONS EVACUATED.**

**HEAVY LORNICH.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

Jerusalem, August 26.
It is reliably learned that following a heavy engagement in which the Druses forced the French to evacuate Khirbostelghazali in the north of Damascus, there was another heavy engagement last night. A French column, advancing south east to Izrina, was compelled to evacuate strong positions, the Druses following up the retiring French. The latter's losses were heavy. An armoured car and aeroplane were destroyed. French aeroplanes are bombarding a village near Izrina.

## [10] DRUG CONFERENCE.

**CHINESE GUNBOATS. TRANSPORT OPIUM.**

**JAPAN AND MANUFACTURE.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*Geneva, August 27.*

The morning session of the Advisory Committee on Oplum was chiefly devoted to a discussion of the subject of Indian hemp, At the afternoon's session, replying to Sir Malcolm Delevingne's remarks alleging that a large scale manufacture of cocaine was going on in Japan, a big quantity of which must remain on balance, large supplies finding their way to other countries, and therefore not required by local consumption.

Mr. Tsurumi, of Japan, declared that the Government would investigate the matter and if the statement was proved, would reduce the manufacture.

He further stated that the production of cocaine and heroin was confined to medical and scientific purposes.

The question of anti-drug propaganda raised an interesting discussion; resulting in the Committee agreeing that propaganda was more harmful than otherwise.

Sir John Jordan, referring to present-day difficulties of securing fuller information of the cultivation of opium in China, said that Chinese gunboats, instead of seizing opium, were employed in transporting it.

In regard to the 'Canadian American Treaty for extradition in respect of offences against Drug Laws, the Committee decided to draw the attention of the League Council with a view to similar treaties with other Powers.

Sir Delevingne, said the question of a similar Anglo-American Treaty would presently be considered by the British Government.

## [11] ALLIES CONVICTION.

**GERMANY AND THE LEAGUE.**

**FRANCE'S EARNEST HOPE.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

London. August 26.

The text of the French reply to the German Note of July 20, on the subject of security, which has now been published, reiterates the declaration of the French Note of July 16 that the Security Pact cannot affect the provisions of the Treaty Peace relative to the occupation of the Rhine land. France and the Allies are convinced that Germany's entry into the League of Nations is the only solid basis of a mutual guarantee and European agreement, but Germany must enter on the same conditions as other nations, and the Allies are not qualified to discuss the conditions of Germany's entry which the Conneil of the League decided.

Referring to German reservations that arbitral conventions be concluded with Germany and her neighbours, the reply emphasises the primary object of the allies is to render a fresh resort to force impossible therefore compulsory arbitration is an indispensable condition of any Pact. The reply concludes by dwelling on the difficulties and delays involved in the exchange of Notes, and therefore, invites Germany to enter negotiations, earnestly trusting they may result in the conclusion of a definitive treaty.

**DEPARTATION ORDERS MADE.**

**RAILWAYMEN THREATEN.**

*Melbourne, August 27.*

Deportation summonses are being issued against four persons connected with the Seamen's strike.

Two hundred and sixty-six thousand tons of British shipping are now idle in Australian ports.

The Governments of Tasmania, Victoria and South Australia, replying to Mr. Stanley Bruce's request, have promised to take necessary steps to protect seamen remaining loyal to agreements.

A general railway strike is threatened at Brisbane on, the 31st inst. unless a demand is met for the restoration of the recent five per cent, reduction in wages.

## [16] FILMS AND FACTS.

(To the Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—"It is always dangerous, even in the interest of truth, to disturb the traditions that cluster around romance, but it is equally dangerous to disturb truth in the interest of romance."—Frederick Orin Bartlet.

The above quotation will, I hope, serve as sufficient apology for my encroaching on your valuable columns.

As a compatriot and admirer of the hero, it was with no little interest and enthusiasm that I crossed over to Kowloon the other night to see the film version of the life of the world famous Persian poet, astronomer and philosopher Omar Khayyam (the Tentmaker) at the Star Cinema, little suspecting the utter disappointment which was to be my ultimate fate.

I am not concerned here with the quality of the acting of the various personages or the artistic values of the sceneries, though I consider both of these to have been quite good. What I wish to point out is that those who are responsible for the production of this film have shown an utter disregard for historical facts. They seem to have absolutely forgotten that they were dealing not with mere beings of the imagination but with real men who actually lived, did great things and left their mark on the world.

I will now enumerate briefly some of the incidents of the film version which are not supported by history and by the various biographies of Omar's life and in doing so I have no other purpose but that of endeavouring to prevent some of our readers who have seen the film from carrying with them some wrong impressions concerning Persian History and the life of this great astro-nomer-poet of Persia.

(1) That Omar fell in love and eloped with his Professor's daughter is pure fiction. In fact

## [21] history does not even mention whether he married at all.

(2). Hassan Sabbah, one of Omar's two old school-friends, never turned out the common robber and cut-throat that he is represented in the film and he was never appointed to the Governorship of Naishapur. As a matter of fact he was an exceedingly clever man, though rather too ambitious, was given some post by the Nizamul Mulk at the Royal Court in consequence of their old pledge, intrigued against his patron, lost his post, fled from the Court and founded the fanatical religious set known as the Ismailians. There is also nothing in history to show that he ever persecuted Omar with whom he was always on the best of terms, though it was at the hands of one of Hassan's followers, that the great Nizamul Mulk met with his death.

(3) That the contemporary monarch, Alap Arsalan, stole the lawfully wedded wife of one of his highly-honoured subjects is a great calumny on one of the greatest rulers of Persia whose extraordinary bravery won for him the title of Arsalan, the Lion, and to whom and his immediate successors Persian literature owes so much.

*Hongkong, August 26, 1925.*
