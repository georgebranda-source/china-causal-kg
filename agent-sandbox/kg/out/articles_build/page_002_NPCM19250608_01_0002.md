## [12] COMMERCE AND FINANCE

**HELP FOR RUHR PRODUCERS**

### Non

A blow aimed at the British coal industry, which has been threatening for some time, has fallen at last. It is officially announced today that the German railways have finally decided to reduce the tariff on all coal transported from German mines to German ports.
Shipments to Hamburg are to enjoy a reduction of 30 per cent. of the present tariff, shipments to Bremen 15 per cent, and Shipments to Emden 12 per cent.

## [13] RUHR OUTPUT.

All these ports and other North Sea ports receive the Ruhr output. Correspondingly proportional reductions will be made on shipments to Baltic ports from the Silesian mines.

This measure is merely the final move in a great scheme, which has been long on the horizon, to create gigantic trust, embracing the coal, iron, steel and shipbuilding industries; and protecting them from foreign competition, especially English competition.

Similar tariff reductions are already operative for iron, steel, and shipbuilding parts from German factories to the yards.

The tariff reduction on Ruhr cost is obviously directed at British coal, which hitherto came within the radius of most German North Sea ports, on account of the cheaper transport by sea. The Germans will now be able to extend their radius in order to exclude the English market.

A result of the agreement reached recently is that practically all the Ruhr mines are now syndicated so as to be able to control their prices according to the price of foreign coal.

## [15] A LARGE RETURN

The Birmingham Small Arms Company further improved its position in the financial year which ended in January last, and, for the first times since 1921, the directors are able to recommend a dividend on the ordinary shares. The rate proposed is 5 per cent. less tax—the same as that for the eighteen months to January, 1921, except that the latter was free of tax. The profit reported now is £305,320, and, with £131,027 brought forward, there is £435,347 available. Interest at 6½ per cent. on the 12-year notes for £2,500,000 issued in 1920 and the preference and ordinary dividends will apparently require an aggregate of £250,200, and £177,147—an increase of $46,000—will be carried forward. For 1923-24 notes interest absorbed £125,260, and the preference dividend (which has been paid continuously during the depression) £24,044.

## [16] CANADIAN TRADE

**SOME INTERESTING ITEMS.**

A considerable quantity of alfalfa seed produced in Algeria last year was sold to United States dealers, indicating the demand for northern grown seed. It is estimated that over a quarter of a million pounds were sold altogether. Algeria seed growers also sold 100,000 pounds of Timothy seed.

## [17] ST. JOHN'S NEW FOUND LAND.

The sensor's kill of the Newfoundland sealing fleet amounted to approximately 127,000 seals, which is considered fair in comparison with other years. Tenvessels were engaged in the hunt this year. One vessel was destroyed and this tended to lower the combined catch of the fleet.

## [18] OTTAWA, ONTARIO.

Civil aviation in Canada has developed rapidly in the past two years. Actual flying time increased from 188,008 hours in 1923 to 294,778 hours in 1924, according to the report on civil aviation which has just been issued by the Department of National Defence. More than 9,000 persons were carried in aircraft in Canada last year, while in the previous year there were 5,324 persons carried. Exclusive of the Royal Canadian Air Force, 32 licences were issued for aircraft operation, while the total licensed personnel qualified to take part in civil flying was 201

Canada's exports of non-ferrous metals have risen from a value of $42,000,000 in the twelve months ending February 28, 1923, to $88,000,000 in the year ending February 28, 1925, according to a report published by the Department of Mines. Gold finds in northern Ontario account for a large proportion of this increase, and the new finds this spring indicate that it will not be long before Canada is mining more gold in a year than the United States.

Approximately 2,000,000 motor vehicles entered Canada during 1924, as compared with 1,250,000 in 1923, according to a report issued by the Canadian Parks Branch of the Federal Government. The increase in cars entered for a period longer than one day during 1924 was 32 per cent of the 1923 entries and 105 per cent of the 1922 entries.

Egg exports from Alberta increased 33 per cent during 1923, compared with the year previous, according to a report of the Provincial Department of Agriculture. Three years ago Alberta was almost entirely an importing province with respect to eggs, but last year it exported more than 60,000 cases, or 133 carloads.

## [20] MONTREAL, QUEBEC.

Shipments and manufactures of paper from Canada during the year ended March 31, 1925, amounted in value to $90,041,910, an increase of about $3,000,000 over the year previous, according to Government reports. The United States was, the heaviest buyer, accounting for approximately 80 per cent of the total exports. The United Kingdom also purchased large quantities of Canadian paper, while smaller shipments were dispatched to countries in all parts of the world.

According to Canadian official returns, the exports of live cattle to Britain during 1924 amounted to 79,435 head, as compared with 56,672 head in 1923. Exports to the United States were 97,847 cattle, 35,178 calves and 18,745 sheep, as compared with 96,873 cattle, 24,074 calves and 28,748 sheep in 1923.

## [21] THE SHIPBUILDING POSITION.

The influence which is being exerted by the oil engine on the shipbuilding industry throughout the world is strikingly illustrated in an analysis published by "The Motor Ship." The countries in which an improvement in shipbuilding is shown, compared with a year ago, are those where motor ship construction is proceeding proportionately more rapidly than elsewhere. In the United Kingdom the tonnage of ships now being built is 300,000 less than in April, 1924, and the percentage of motor ships to steamers is 45. In Germany, Holland, Italy, Denmark and Sweden more work is in progress than was the case a year ago, and in these countries collectively 75 per cent. of the vessels now being constructed are to be equipped with oil engines.

It is, however, satisfactory to add that the largest marine Diesel engine yet built has just been completed in Harland and Wolff's Belfast works. This is a 10,000 h.p. unit and is one of two sets for installation in the Royal Mail Steam Packet Co's 22,000-ton passenger liner "Asturias".
