## [8] LOCAL AND GENERAL

The members of the "Our Cabaret" Company were again in fine form last night and submitted another change of programme to the immense liking of the audience. The Company complete their Hongkong season this evening with a complete change of programme and commence a short season to-morrow at the Star Theatre, Kowloon.

The remarkable experience of suddenly losing his power of speech befell an Edinburgh man residing in the South Side of the city. I seems that he was reading a newspaper at home shortly after ten o'clock, when he suddenly became conscious of the fact that he could not utter a coherent word. On Sunday the man was able to say a few words, and hopes of his recovery were entertained. The only cause to which the man's misfortune can be attributed is an experience of shell-shock during the war, when, it seems he was thrown a considerable distance by the explosion of a shell.

Glasgow Town Council has decided by a large majority in favour of the demolition of the steeple along with St. Enoch's Church. During the discussion Treasurer Graham pointed out that it was in the interests of the city to take this step and deal with the congestion of traffic. They had no option, he said, but to clear the square and make it of some use to the citizens. Councillor Stark Broun argued that they had bought the church and the steeple at £36,000 simply for the purpose of getting rid of them. All the protests which were made now bought to have been raised at the time negotiations were taking place. It was not the Corporation who should be spoken of as vandals. It was the Church of Scotland, who for sordid gold sold the church and steeple.

A specimen of Depmeptera alivacea, a rare species of Sphingidae (Hawk Moth), was taken recently by Mr. G. F. C. Woodlett in a Sandakan bungalow. It has been presented to Mr. Preston Clark of Boston, Massachusetts, who is reputed to have the finest collection of Sphingidae in the world.

In view of the return of M. Callaux to office it is interesting to note that a short time ago he published a book entitled "Ou va la France"? Ou va l'Europe?"—a work which is a challenge to all thoughtful students of European politics. This book was translated into English and published by Messrs. T. Fisher Unwin, Ltd., under the title of "Whither France? Whither Europe."

There has been in Hankow recently the representative of a strong British firm, says the "C. C. Post," on the mission of ascertaining from the Municipal Councils whether they would sanction a scheme for supplying Hankow with a tramway service. The firm is prepared to face the whole expense of the undertaking. What sort of reply will be given there has not yet been time to learn.

American motorcar statistics are always interesting and an authority quoted in the "Literary Digest" (New York) treats them in a new way. California, it appears, in the matter of automobile density, stands in a "class by itself, having one vehicle for each three inhabitants. Next comes Iowa with 41, then Nevada 42, and Kansas, Nebraska and Oregon, 44 each. New England seems to lag far behind these wealthy Western States, Vermont being the nearest with 5.7, while there are some States in the South so impoverished that they can only muster one car to every ten inhabitants.

## [9] SOCIAL AND PERSONAL.

After a health trip to Japan and North China, Mr. A. Morley returned to the Colony by the s.s. Nagoya on Sunday.

Mr. H. R. Phelips, of the Government Audit Office, left Hongkong for England yesterday on the Blue Funnel s.s. Rhexenor.

Mr. Edgar Warwick and his company which recently played in Hongkong, is at present enjoying considerable success in Australia.

Marshall Tuan Chi-jui is reported to have ordered the Ministry of Finance to find money for the expenses of General Hsu Shutseng ("Little" Hsu), High International Commissioner to Europe and America, while he is abroad. If we remember aright, $800,000 was sent to Hsu about two months ago, says the "N. C. Daily News," which adds the query: At that rate, is it really worth the former Anfu leader's while to come back?

Mr. Hugh S. Miller, who, for the past few months, has been in charge of the U.S. Consulate-General at Singapore, has proceeded to the United States on leave, accompanied by Mrs. Miller, on the Steel Navigator. Mr. George F. Dickins, who was formerly Vice-Consul in charge at Penang, has been appointed the Acting American Consul-General for Singapore. Mr. Miller was formerly a member of the "China Mail" staff in Hongkong.

Reporters cable that Prince George arrived at Shanghai yesterday, and is staying at the Consulate General. He leaves for Weihaiwei to day about the D. N. S. Tungchow.

Dame Clara Butt, who had been engaged to sing at Devonshire Park, Easibourne, on May 15 had to cancell the engagement on account of ill-health. In January last Mme. Butt underwent an operation in a Leeds nursing home.

'The Prince of Wales' horror of putting on weight was probably, at least partly, the reason for that visit of his to the hottest part of the Repulse, when the ship was crossing the Equator. He is not above a joke on the subject. When told he was again to appear in butter at Wembley this year, he reminkled! "I don't mind a bit as long as I don't spread!"

"A question I'm occasionally asked is: 'What do the majority of people suffer from mostly?' says Mr. Sandow. Well, it isn't any particular 'ism' or 'itis' or 'tion', but want of proper exercise, fresh air, proper food and sound sleep. These are Nature's medicines, and for the majority of common illnesses no others are needed. There isn't a drug known that will do all that these simple at-hard remedies will do, and they cost so little."

Dr. Li Chi-fan, M.D. (Berlin), who was summoned posthaste to Dr. Sun Yat-sen's bedside when the Generalisimo's condition became critical in Peking, has returned to Canton, writes the "China Mail" correspondent. Dr. Li was called for when Dr. Sun's Peking medical advisers pronounced that there was no more hope. Prior to his departure, Dr. Li telegraphed back instructions and took the first boat but was too late as Dr. Sun had died before he arrived. Dr. Li was accompanied by Mrs. Li and his private secretary and they subsequently proceeded to Japan for a holiday.

Mme. Suzanne Despres, the celebrated French actress, has refused the Legion of Honour. Her husband, M. Lugne, Poe—himself a Legionaire—the well-known author and playwright, has notified the present Minister of Fine Arts that she does not desire the honour which was recently offered to her. Mme. Despres states that she desires above all to be a womanly woman, to live through the world modestly and unperceived, simply carrying out her dirty as she conceives it with our laws, as it has little publicity and humility as possible. Mme. Despres is the well-known author of the Legion of Honour.
Mr. W. H. Perry, for two years on the staff of the Hongkong and Shanghai Hotels, Ltd., and for the last eleven months resident manager of the Hongkong Hotel, has resigned his position with the Company and is proceeding to Shanghai to take charge of a small catering establishment.

Those who remember the visit of the Scottish basso, Mr. W. Heughan will be interested in the following which has been received from his manager, Mr. David Kennedy, from Dunedin:—

It will no doubt interest your readers to learn of Mr. William Heughan's great success in Dunedin—the Edinburgh of the South, where his reception has undoubtedly been the greatest and the most enthusiastic ever given to an artist. From his arrival, when he was met at the station by thousands of expectant enthusiasts, until the night of his twelfth and last concert, the enthusiasm was intense and the press and public alike have been unanimous in the opinion that he is the greatest and most versatile artist who has ever visited the city. At many of his concerts, large numbers were unable to gain domision, the hall being packed to capacity. The inhabitants of the city have done their utmost to make his visit a memorable one, the Civic Authorities' Clubs and Societies, together with the general public entertaining him in what the local papers describe as a right sort, manning the life in proceeding with the New Zealand tour and is everywhere being received with the greatest enthusiasm.

Sakhalin, the northern end of which has just been handed back to Russia by the Japanese, is as big as Scotland and has a population of about thirty thousand, mostly convicts or ex-convicts, and is also inhabited by bears, wolves, hairy Ainus, and pelagic seals. Its value is purely strategic, for though not uninhabitable, the climate is anything but Mediterranean. Doubtless the Soviet Government will restore its ancient fame as a convict settlement.

A special commissioner of the "Observer." in the course of a series of articles on "Scotland To-day," describing the "Glasgow Sunday," says: — The "Glasgow Sunday" was much as ever—a day when the stimulus of work was withdrawn and the combined weight of dreary architecture and negative morality—sat heavy upon the soul. "Glasgow is not a city to dream in, and when its industry imported the Irishman, who must have his dreaming-time, it took to itself a full cargo of social discord. A scheme of life based on "gear and grace" ("Let Glasgow flourish by, the preaching of the Word") could not assimilate a race touched to lighter, if not to finer issues. The Irish submission was a slow and sour one, mitigated by shebeening and its crude consolations. But there were others who could not fill the void of the Sabbath with gluttonous "diets of worship," and became the sullen prisoners of the inelastic code. Unction and ugliness turned many a mild and harmless bohemian into a social malcontent.

There were five generations at the festivities following the wedding at Romford of Miss Gladys Starling, of Prospect-place, the oldest being Mrs. Louisa Bull, who has just reached her hundredth birthday. The Duke of York as Earl of Inverness was elected Chief of the Scottish Clans Association at the annual meeting on May 16. As indicating His Royal Highness's profound interest in the activities of the influential body of which he will be the head, it is interesting to note that, immediately on his return to White Lodge he sent a letter to the present Chief expressing regret at his inability to attend the annual meeting, but hinting that he and the Duchess of York might be able to attend one or other of the functions of the Association during the coming year. The three events from which he is likely to choose are the Burns Concert, the annual dinner, and the visit which the clansmen pay each summer to the Royal Caledonian Schools at Bushey.

Princess Mary celebrated her twenty-elighth birthday very quietly, but was the recipient of a pile of congratulatory messages. One charming feature were several greetings, each signed by little groups of women associated with helpful social works in which the Princess has taken a warm interest. A couple of days before the happy event Princess Mary saw the anniversary mentioned in a newspaper programme of the week's birthday, and although she was not the only lady in the diary, she was alone in having her age stated: "I shall never be able to keep my age as secret," she smilingly commented. It is one of the penalties of Royalty. Other women may attain to thirty-and stay there, but the whole world kunns the age of a princess. Therefore, when Princess Mary escaped being called "Diamond," as King Edward wished in honor of the Diamond Jobline, she did not escape the penalty attached to a dated name. Happily she has not caused to work about the years for though it is after life, the same very mild and armoured place marriage she has acquired.
