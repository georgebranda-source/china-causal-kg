## [15] DYNAMO MAGIC.

**INVENTIONS FOR THE HOME**

The proverbial "home from home" acquires a new acceptation from the suite of rooms which the General Electric Company have fitted up at Magnet House, their headquarters in Kingsway. By an ingenious concentration of appliances and effects a set of showrooms has been given the semblance of a modern flat, and every up-to-date device of electricity has been displayed to advantage in its proper place. A representative of "The Daily Telegraph" was shown how far the magic force engendered by battery and dynamo has been adapted to every household use in the way of heat, lighting, and power. So far as the living rooms are concerned, the endeavour has been to heighten the scale of comfort, eliminate dirt, and promote picturesquences. By the use of a brightly painted landscape with artificial flowers a few foreground effects, an unsightly yard, or a dead wall outside a window can be converted into the semblance of a garden in full flower, with the effect of sunshine or moonlight at will. If space forbids even this the window can be covered with amber silk, and the room be flooded with a rich suffused light. In the dining-room a series of well-plugs in the skirting-board converts the fireplace into a source of comfort without hand-labour, and transforms even screens and footstools into the means of warmth. It relieves the demands on the kitchen by actuating a chafing-dish or a hot-plate, a coffee-percolator or a ten-kettle, and even a cigarr-lighter after dinner for the gentlemen. Here, as in the drawing room, a standard lamp can afford, by alternative switches, a localised or downward light, and an upward light reflected from the ceiling and generalised through the whole apartment. Another invention worth noting is a smoke absorber, which counteracts tobacco smoke by generating a pleasant aromatic perfume.

Beyond the familiar ventilation fan and the radiator in many forms, the kitchen and scullery benefit by cookers and stoves possessed of many powers, a variety of hot cupboards, table grills and toasters, a new type of vacuum cleaner of almost universal application, and even a "drink mixer" for reducing the cocktail prescription to a minimum of time and trouble. Bathroom fittings, from the colorifier to the gown-rail for drying, are constructed in stainless metals, so as to withstand the action of steam, and the electric shaving-cup guarantees an outfit of hot water in less than a couple of minutes. Mere description pales before the corundum-lined cylinder which pares 1lb. of apples or potatoes per minute, and 56lb. in the larger sizes. The main thing is that these hundred-and-one inventions are advanced as certain in their action, economical in expenditure of power, and reasonably "fool-proof" in their manipulation and control. It is very certain that the demonstration is one which will command profound attention from the public when they are admitted.

## [16] TROTSKY'S RETURN.

**"REDS" AND THE PEASANTRY.**

The latest advices from Soviet Russia shed some additional light upon the probable effect of M. Trotsky's return to Moscow. While it is true that he repudiates any weakening of his faith in Leninism and its economics, it would seem to be a fact that he does propose to influence the policy of the Soviets in at least one new direction. His intention is, above all things, to improve the conditions of the peasants, who, he considers, have latterly been sorely neglected in favour of the urban workers. Owing to this neglect the State was bound to suffer, especially such a country as Russia, whose staple industry has always been and remains agriculture.
There is yet another reason, namely, that the peasant is the backbone of the army; and it is the army first and foremost of which the head of the Red militants is for ever thinking. He means to strengthen his personal position in this way by finding new supporters in the ranks of the country against the extremists, who are chiefly drawn from the towns.

It is gathered that Moscow is by no means enamoured of the quality of the results recently achieved by the Soviet Embassies, Legations, and Trade Delegations abroad. Consequently, what might be described as a kind of diplomatic Cheks in being appointed, a voting commission whose hisiness it will be to pay visits of inspection to the Embassies and Legations in question, and report on their labours. ## [19] ACADEMY PICTURE.

**18-YEAR-OLD ARTIST'S SUCESS. THE MARCH OF TIMES**

Stanley Shepherd, the boy artist whose picture, "The March of Time," in the Royal Academy, was favourably commented on by Mr. Frank Butter in the "Sunday Times," is still a pupil at the Harrow Art School under the Middlesex County Council.
He is a native of Harrow, his father, a professional violinist, living at 96, Sussex Road. The boy takes the signal honour that has come to him early in life quite composedly, and when soon recently he seemed more excited as a result of the favourable criticism by Mr. Butter than at the knowledge that he had had a picture accepted by the Royal Academy.
Asked by a "Sunday Times" representative how he got his subject for "The March of Time," he said he was sketching trees at North Harrow, where development is taking place and it struck him that the tree still standing, the houses being erected, and the railway line, showed that time was marching remorselessly on. Hence his little oil painting, which shows green pastures being transformed by enterprises, Builders into suburban dwellings.

A BOHOLABBSHIP BOY
Stanley Shepherd in 16 years of age, and was educated at a local elementary school, from which, with the aid of a scholarship, he passed to the County School for Boys, where he gained an art scholarship, and for the past three years he has been studying at the local Arts School under Mr. F. T. Mummer. He has shown at the annual school art exhibitions and has been a member of the local school of art.
