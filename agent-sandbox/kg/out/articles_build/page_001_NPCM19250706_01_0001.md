## [8] "CARRYING ON."

**URÓPEANS AND ESSENTIAL SERVICES**

**IDLERS TO GO**

—

The week end was without event apart from the performance by almost every European in the Colony of unusual duties in connection with servantless households, but in spite of these a cheerful spirit was abroad and a larger number than ever seemed to find time for relaxation in the way of a game, of golf or a dip in the briny. Large crowds were taken to Repulse Bay by bus, car and motor cycle.

Throughout the galety there would sometimes enter a note of discord, such as the week of extra work and duties yet to come, but with the advent of this morning things were found to give less cause for anxiety than had been anticipated by some.

As regards numbers the most noteworthy absentions this morning were Chinese employees at the Naval Yard. Some of these had already been affected, but this morning the remainder came out to a man bringing the total of "strikers" there to something like 2,000. They were all warned before they left.

Kowloon 'bus drivers left their work this morning, also a few taxi drivers on the Kowloon side only, but the services are being maintained.

The notification of large rewards to be paid for information leading to the arrest of intimidators is having good effect and in the Court this morning some cases were dealt with. Seamen refusing to work were this morning sentenced to four weeks' imprisonment and two days' loss of pay.
An order has been gazetted dealing with the unemployed.
The export of coal is prohibited except under licence.

## [9] Naval Employees.

<sub>section under [8] "CARRYING ON."</sub>

That the Naval Authorities had anticipated the defection of further men is shown by the fact that over the week end notices were posted up in the workshops and in prominent place about the yard informing all men that any who absent themselves from the yard without giving notice were breaking their contract with the Admiralty and that after six consecutive days of such absence they were by contract automatically discharged.

The notice also pointed out that in the event of any whose six days' absence had expired presenting themselves for re-employment the Admiralty had the power to reserve the right of saying whether or not such men's previous service should count toward gratuity or pensions and whether or not such men would be placed on the minimum rate of pay for newcomers whatever their previous experience.

## [10] Kowloon Buses.

<sub>section under [8] "CARRYING ON."</sub>

Early this morning the 'buses on the Kowloon side were running as usual but about the time that they would be in the greatest demand the drivers and conductors left their posts to a man. The excellence of the arrangements made by the police for any such eventuality may be gauged from the fact that within a very short time indeed Indians were taking the place of the strikers and running quite a good service. This will be extended in due course.

## [11] Ships Cleared.

<sub>section under [8] "CARRYING ON."</sub>

Whether the prosecution of firemen refusing to obey orders had anything to do with the matter or not it would be difficult to say with any degree of authority, but the Cheong Shing, one of the boats of Messrs. Jardine, Matheson & Co., which has several times attempted to clear but returned, left again this morning. It was learned that the number of firemen had been supplemented considerably and there was no question of the ship returning. This is the third Jardine boat to be cleared.

## [12] Seamen Sentenced.

<sub>section under [8] "CARRYING ON."</sub>

Two Chinese quartermasters and three Chinese-seamen—all in the employment of Messrs. Butterfield and Swire, appeared at the Marine Court this morning charged with disobeying orders.
The prisoners all belonged to the son of Chin Hue and evidence against them was given by Captain Ross Lewis, the master, and C. B. L. Stringer, the Chief Officer.
The Chief Officer said the host

swain came to him on Saturday morning and told him that members of the crew wished to leave the ship. Permission was refused them, and on their refusal to work the master read out their articles to them explaining that they had signed on for six months.

The master said that permission was at first given some of the men who had asked for leave to go ashore, but later this had been withdrawn. The accused had then refused to work and after warning had been given of the consequences which would follow their action the police flag was hoisted and the men were taken into custody.

One of the prisoners, speaking also on behalf of the others, said that they found they had no money and no food. Their request for shore leave was refused and they were asked if they were going to work. They replied that they would, providing they were given food and pay. The prisoner was delightfully vague as to the actual conversation which had taken place between the master and the men as the following note taken by the Harbour Master shows "it is not possible to get out of the accused what he actually replied as he evades a direct statement."

The men were sentenced to four weeks' imprisonment and two days' deduction of pay.

## [13] China the Retail

**WILD RUMOURS.**

**OFFICIALLY PROVED BASELESS.**

**AGITATORS' TRICKS.**

Many wild rumours are circulating in the Colony. The "China Mail" learns that they are all entirely baseless.

Yesterday it was stated that several people had been killed in various parts of the Colony. Nothing of the sort has taken place.

Inquiry at the Colonial Secretary's Office this morning elicited the fact that two people had died but their deaths had no connection whatever with the strike.

An unknown street character" died opposite the Central Market, cutting his head open as he fell into the roadway. The cause of his death beyond doubt was apoplexy.

Another Chinese was found dead at West Point. Actually he had died in a house from beri beri, but his body had been carried into the street and so placed as to give the appearance that intimidators had been at work.

Almost every day in normal times, bodies are found in the colony's streets, and the public could be well-advised to treat all stories of killings and woundings with the greatest reserve. Such stories are only spread to create vischief.

At 10:15 o'clock last night the armed robbery alarm was sounded at the Central Police Station. The alarm came from Pottinger Street, and on arrival at the spot it was found that the call was a false one. What happened was that a Chinese attempted theft by putting his hands through the bars of a window, but he escaped before the arrival of the police.

## [14] Empress Volunteers.

<sub>section under [13] China the Retail</sub>

The Empress of Canada has arrived from Manila and is discharged cargo, taking on board silk and other transhipped goods from South China. Stewards are all that are required for the liner's trip to Vancouver and volunteers have been tumbling over themselves at the Canadian Pacific offices. Many more applications have been received than there are vacant posts. The Canada is an oil burner and the engineers are sufficient to attend to the comparatively little work such engines entail as compared with coal burners.

The Colony's principal docks have not been affected to anything like the same degree. Taikoo and the Hongkong and Whampon Dock have lost more fitters and the latter more coppersmiths and boiler makers, but with European and things are going on very much usual. The s.m. Engraver Castle for instance, was docked over the week and at the Cosmopolitan Dock painted, and a little carpentry work done, and she was out again to day.

## [16] HONGKONG, MONDAY, JULY 6, 1925.

**CAUGHT.**

**REWARD FOR INFORMER.**

At the Central Magistracy, a Chinese youth was sentenced to one month's hard labour and twelve strokes of the batch for trying to force a servant boy but of his employment at the Soldiers' Club.

It transpired in evidence that the servant was assaulted by the accused on his refusal to leave. A report was made to the police and the accused was arrested.

From official circles it was learned that the reward of $250 offered by the Captain Superintendent of Police for information leading to the arrest of an agitator will in this case be paid to the informer.

## [17] SERIOUS CHARGE.

**WHAT A EUROPEAN SAYS HE HEARD.**

、

Two Chinese were brought before Mr. S. B. B. McElderry at the Central Magistracy this morning on a charge of intimidation.

Mr. A. W. Millar, clerk of works at Messrs. Leigh and Orange. Witness stated that he first noticed the two accused standing near Messrs. Jardine. Matheson and Company's office building at Des Vaux Road at 8.30 o'clock on Friday morning, and saw them

still there when he passed several times during the day. At 3.15 p.m. witness saw the two men approaching a Chinese who had just alighted from a tramcar and he got close enough to overhear what was being said at the same time removing any suspicion by bending down and pretending to rub his foot.

Mr. Millar, who speaks Cantonese fluently, heard the first accused tell the passenger that he would be stabbed if he travelled in a tramcar again. The second accused, then told the passenger: "You know us now; beware."

The accused denied the charge and one of them stated that he was waiting there to meet a man from the Kwong Sang Company who had offered him work at an American Hotel run by Chinese. The other man said that he was there to meet a friend.

His Worship adjourned the case for enquiries to be made at the addresses given by the accused.

## [18] SUSPECTS.

**. WANDERING ON THE PEAK.**

Two Chinese were found wandering on the Peak yesterday morning and are now in the hands of the police undergoing investigation. The police suspect that the men came from Yaumati for the purpose of ousting the remaining workers at the Peak houses.

On Saturday, two others suspected of being intimidators were detained by the police when they were found on the Peak. Investigations showed that the men were ex-soldiers from Cantor and were strangers in the place.

A correspondent who signs himself "Anti-Striker" (Sung; Yukman) writes to the "China Mail" as follows:—

Chinese Strike Tickets have been very much in evidence today, intimidating Chinese passengers for using the tram-cars. Two men were stationed at each of the following places: - Causeway Bay, China Sugar Refinery, Percival Street, Tin Lok Street, No. 2 Police Station, Spring Garden Lane, Seamen's Institute, Garden Road, Lee House Street, Hongkong Hotel; and the Post Office. The Police should arrest all idlers at these places for vagrancy and deport them to Canton. Some strong measures should be adopted to stop this intimidation being carried on any further by these Bolsheviks.

## [19] IDLERS TO GO!

**NEW REGULATION IN FORCE.**

**POWERS TO POLICE.**

The following regulation made by the Governor in Council under section 2 of the Emergency Regulations Ordinance, 1022, Ordinance No. 5 of 1922, on the 5th day of July, 1925, is published in a Government Gazette Extraordinary:—

“The Captain Superintendent of Police and any Police officer authorised by him, either generally or specially, shall have power to arrest and detain any person who appears to him to have no regular employment in the Colony, and the Captain Superintendent of Police shall have power to order any such person to leave the Colony forthwith.

"Any person failing to comply with any such order may be deported by order of the Governor."

## [21] ILLICIT FIREWOOD.

**PLANTATIONS DAMAGED.**

With most of the forestry staff being assigned to other duties, considerable damage has been done to plantations with the wholesale cutting down of trees on the hillsides, mostly by women earth coolies, for converting into fuel.

On Saturday afternoon the hillside above the University rang with the echoes of hatchefs. A member of the University staff quickly sent a telephone message to Divisional Inspector Peter Grant, of the West Point Police Station, and this officer proceeded to the scene with a few others. A large number of the woodcutters spied the approach of the officers and made their escape, but by a persistent watch eighteen persons were arrested.

When charged before Mr. McElderry at the Central Magistracy this morning, eleven women and four men were fined $10 each or two weeks' imprisonment. Another woman was fined $5 or one week. One man failed to appear and had his bail of $5 estreated. A small boy was ordered four strokes of the birch.

In another case from the Wan-chai district, a boy was caught sawing a large pine tree. He was given six strokes.

## [22] CANTON AFFAIR

**SHOT FIRED AT GOVERNOR WU**

**ASSAILANT KILLED.**

Reports have reached Hongkong of the attempted assassination of Governor Hu Han-min in Canton. Fortunately, so the report goes, he was not seriously wounded and is expected to recover in a week.

Governor Wu was returning from the inaugural ceremony in the Public Park. He was in General Hsu Shung-chi's car when a man opened fire at him in the city. The Governor was wounded in the knee and the General was slightly injured by glass splinters. On the General's body guards returning the fire, the alleged assailant was wounded and captured.

## [24] STRIKE ITEMS.

### -

Many Chinese businesses, even clubs, have been affected by the strike, the latest absentee's being the servants at the Chinese Y.M.C.A.

Houschelders with loyal boys should beware of blind Chinese singing in the locality. A number this week end were found to be singing intimidations.

The majority of Chinese boarding houses and hotels have been affected to an appreciable extent through the staffs leaving. The number of residents has also dropped...

- Europeans connected with the Sanitary Department were noticed putting...in...heavy... and sustained work this week end. A good service has been maintained, all things considered.

An "unofficial" embargo on the shipment of foodstuffs to Hongkong is unmoured to have been enforced in Canton. Nevertheless fresh vegetables and fruit is still coming down in old lots.

Three hundred thousand dollars a day is the estimate of a local Chinese merchant quoted by a vernatendar paper, of the loss sustained by the Colony's trade on account of the strike.

About a hundred passengers on an in-coming steamer from "Canton were," on examination, refused admittance to the Colony and sent back because they could not give satisfactory reasons for coming here, says a Chinese paper.

Although inactivity in the markets is on the increase, the position is counter-balanced by the additions to the marks of temporary hawker's who are to be found everywhere setting all kinds of meat, vegetables etc at competitive prices.

The situation is generally quiet in Macao but food prices are shooting up owing to the threatened shortage of supplies. Macao is better off than Hongkong in respect of vegetables and fruit as it is adjacent to a fertile area devoted to agriculture.

## [26] 一

<sub>section under [24] STRIKE ITEMS.</sub>

The Chinese revenue workers under Chief Preventive Officer S.J. Clarke were to be seen helping with a will in clearing refuse from the Harbour Office this morning. Besides continuing with their own work they are even volunteering to give a hand to the hard-worked Sanitary department in regard to the disposal of refuse.

There is practically no commodity of food—except that sold by the well-established firms which are really public institutions—which has not appreciated in value since the strike began on the night of June 19. Profiteering there certainly is, but this has been checked to a small extent by enterprising small traders who visit the markets in the New Territories and buy up produce for retail needs.

An instance of opportunists being stung when trying to exploit the Chinese community is forthcoming from the retailers of Chinese preserved foods known commonly as marine delicacies. Before the strike began prices went up, old stocks were cleared and the shops replenished their supplies by purchases from wholesalers. The latter also put up their prices and the retailers now have considerable quantities which they are selling at a loss as the demand has diminished after the first tide of hoarding.
