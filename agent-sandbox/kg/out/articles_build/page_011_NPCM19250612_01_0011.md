## [1] CANTON FIGHTING.

**SHOTS FIRED AT LOCAL STEAMERS**

**BLOCKADE ATTEMPT?**

**ELEVEN FOREIGN WARSHIPS AT CANTON.**

"Usual heavy machine gun and rifle fire in the early hours of the morning—no big engagement"—these and similar messages are all that Hongkong firms with interests in Canton have received this morning. The ferry between the Oriental Hotel (West Bund) and Honam is no longer in operation but the ferry between Shameen Bund and Honam is carrying on a limited service. Special police from the European offices are on duty on Shameen Apparently, the condition of stalemate continues in Canton city. Troops stationed on the opposite banks of the Canton River are remaining under cover and taking pot-shots at their adversaries when opportunities present themselves. Of most concern to Hongkong is the firing on river steamers in Chinese territory. In the early hours of yesterday morning when the Canton-bound vessels were passing Whampoa, fire was opened at them from the shore. All the steamers successfully ran the gauntlet but a "boy" on the Sal On was wounded. He was brought back to hospital in Hongkong. The Sai On and Paul Beau bear bullet marks and one shot went through the Kwangsi's galleys.

The firing is from territory in the hands of the Cantonese. The Cantonese are trying to keep up a blockade and it is believed that they wish to prevent shipping from going to Canton or, at least, to search the vessels for so-called contraband and possible recruits for the non-Cantonese holding the city.

*(From Our Own Correspondent.)*

*Canton, June 11.*

Neither side has yet ventured on a big offensive to land in enemy territory and firing from machine-guns, mausers and rifles continues practically day and night.

The Cantonese reinforcements at Shek Wai Tong have made “feelers” to test the strength of the Yunnanese outpost at Wongsha but are remaining under cover.

Other reinforcements, comprising mostly “Cadet” detachments under Russian officers, have arrived at Honam and it is believed that as soon as conditions are favourable, the Reds will try to cross the River. On the Canton side the Yunnanese remain on the quivive.

Now that the withdrawal from exposed points have been completed, the Yunnanese hold a small area comprising the city on the North side of the River, the heights overlooking the approaches and the eastern and western lines of communication. They have the advantage of being able to move their troops from side to side, rapidly and without interference. However, they are not besieged as they have ample supplies.

Hongkong's food supply is only slightly affected by the Canton fighting and the Shanghai trouble.

As very little fresh-water fish and Canton vegetables can be sent down, prices for these commodities have gone up.
A portion of Hongkong's egg imports is from Shenzhou.

## [5] CHINA'S

**THE BEST USE FOR THE INDEMNITY**

Mr. Fred Tyler frites to the "Morning Post" of April 20, as follows:—

Between the Yellow River and Peking lies the great plain of ever impending disaster. Through this the half-dozen rivers debouching from the Western Hills run on elevated ridges as the results of centuries of dyking. The plain, with its millions of inhabitants, thus lies below the level of the river beds. Athwart the course of those rivers runs the Grand Canal, constructed, regardless of its evil consequences, to convey tribute rice to the capital. It also has an elevated bed. The only access to the sea by these rivers is through one small opening in the obstructing canal, and this opening is insufficient to take the flood volume of even one river.

The result of this condition is necessarily floods, and, when the spates of several rivers occur at the same time, us happens to an extra serious degree about once in ten years, the flood renders millions of pepants homeless, and results in the starvation to death of hundreds of thousands. And, if there be no flood, it means a drought, when again hundreds of thousands die of hunger. Within this area of some 30,000 square miles is concentrated no small fraction of the total of the world's misery.

## [6] IRRIGATION AND SILT.

The problem of remedying this evil condition has peculiarly attractive features. The regulation of the rivers to give suitable access by their waters to the sea could go hand in hand, with an irrigation system, and with a system of controlled silt deposit, which would convert what is now an area of disaster into one of unsurpassed fertility and prosperity.

The financing of this undertaking—whether by loan or otherwise—is, however, utterly beyond the capacity of the Chinese Government, and will continue to remain so for a very long time to come; and so, barring some exceptional opportunity for obtaining the large sum needed and its allocation exclusive, by for the purpose, the misery and stupendous loss of life must go on indefinitely.

Such an opportunity exists to-day. The relinquishment of the Boxer indemnity renders available for the benefit of China a large fund, and the question of its allocation is now under consideration. It seems to be taken for granted that the greater part of the Indemnity will be devoted to educational purposes. Education is, however, already fairly well served, and there are many sources from which funds for that purpose are possible.

He had deliberately deferred his visit to the engine-rooms until the Repulse arrived on the Equestor; for, as he said, "I want to see the hottest part of the ship under its hottest conditions."

So to-day he made a thorough tour of those sweltering, machine-crowded depths where the Repulse generates her 127,000 horse-power.

The range of temperatures he passed through was precisely that of the sequence of the rooms of a Turkish bath—only, unlike the stokers of the Repulse, one does not spend hours on end in a compartment of a Turkish bath where the thermometer registers 120 deg. Nor is there four hours spent relining on a couch, but in clambering and stretching and wriggling among devilish machinery from which they are separated—only by a guard-rail that scorches if you touch it with the naked hand.

Dressed in singlets and shorts, their dripping skins, gleaming like silver under the electric light, these young engine-room ratings, many of them on their first cruise, are taking this stifling steel-walled ship through the sultriest seas on earth, where one perspires even in the fresh air of upper deck. They do it without complaint: even without loss of energy for joining in the ship's amusements.

As the Prince stood on the little steel platform halfway down the ladder to the engine-room a fierce air rushed past him like the blast from a blast-furnace. In the boiler-rooms, where oil fuel is sprayed into white-hot cones, he found the temperature "only 105." Here, at any rate, the men escape that constant labour of shovelling bunkers and trimming which used to be the stokers' lot.

## [17] SLAVE OF DESIRE."

**A SPLENDID FILM.**

"Slave of Desire" is a screen version of Balzac's world famous novel, "The Magic Skin," which has been made into a spectacular romance. Honore de Balzac is aniversally regarded as the greatest novel writer the world has yet produced. He is ranked with Shakespeare in his knowledge of the human heart and soul; his mastery of the art of fictitious narrative is said to be surpassed by no other novelist. At any rate, he wrote a series of novels of life in the France of his generation which are the glory of the world's literature. One of the most popular of these was "le Peau de Chagrin," literally translated as "The Wild Ass's Skin," but which is usually known in English as "The Magic Skin," from the magic skin which its hero gets from an antiquarian. This skin is capable of granting his every wish, but grows smaller as each wish is fulfilled; the man's life ebbs with the shrinking skin. In making the screen version a new title, "Slave of Desire," was decided upon as more fitting to the underlying theme of the novel.
