## [10] GOVERNMENT GAZETTE.

**TYPHOON REGULATIONS:**

It is notified that His Excellency has given directions for the rescission of Order No. 152, proclaiming Hohow to be a place at which an infectious or contagious disease prevailed.

His Excellency the Governor has been pleased to appoint Mr. Thomas Dallin to act as Auditor during the absence on leave of Mr. Hugh Richard Phelips or until further notice, with effect from June 1, 1925, and Mr. Robert Andrew Dermod Forrest to act as a Police Magistrate, with effect from May 25, 1925.

His Excellency the Governor has been pleased to appoint, under section 9 (5) of the Liquors Consolidation Ordinance, 1911, Ordinance No. 9 of 1911, Mr. Walter Leslie Pattenden to be a Member of the Licensing Board during the absence from the Colony of Sir Catchick Paul Chater, Kt., C.M.G., with effect from June 4, 1925, and Mr. Thomas Henry King to act as Captain Superintendent of Police and Chief Officer, Fire Brigade, during the absence on leave of Mr. Philip Peverill, John Wodehouse, C.I.E., with effect from June 6, 1925.

The “Gazette” contains rules governing the Protection of Life and Property in the Harbour during Typhoon. Weather.” These state:—

1. When there are immediate prospects of the weather conditions becoming so bad as to place the native craft in the Harbour in danger of being unable to make shelter on their own account, a Red Ensign will be hoisted at the Harbour Office indicating that two Naval tugs will be available to assist to tow such craft to shelter.
2. These Naval tugs will fly the Red Ensign at the masthead and will proceed down to the Central and Southern Fairways to the limit of the shipping, and all mercantile or private craft which desire to avail themselves of a tow to shelter should be ready with their ropes to be taken in tow as the tugs return.

3. All craft will be towed towards Yaumati Refuge.

4. If the wind is blowing from the Eastward, assistance to the shipping at the Western end of the Harbour will be rendered first.

5. Native craft are not to depend on these tugs entirely but should endeavour to make shelter on their own account, as early as possible.

6. The Naval tugs can only be made available when all naval movements are completed and will be used only in emergency during the hours of daylight. On no account will they be used for this work after dark.

7. No responsibility for damage done during towage will be taken by the Government or by the Naval Authorities;

## [11] NOT IDLE THREATS.

**SHIPPING COMPANIES AND PIRATES.**

Threats from pirates that if sums of money were not paid ships would be mised were referred to at the monthly meeting yesterday of the Chinese Chamber of Commerce when Mr. Li Yau-chuen the Chairman, read a joint letter from shipping companies trading to Wuchow in which the threats were referred to and protection asked for. That the threats were not idle, the letter stated, was proved by the fact that after the expiry of the week the letter stipulated would be given the Companies concerned to pay the money the s.s. Wai Hoi had been pirated.
The Chairman said that the Chamber had been asked to request the Hongkong Government for further protection. This they had done and the Hongkong Government had written to the British consul in Canton asking him to request the Canton Government to give protection to British teamers plying on the West River.

## [12] MABEL GARRISON

It Madam Albani sings—or did sing—"Home Sweet Home"—presumably there is no reason why Miss Mabel Garrison should not sing: "A Lill Alabama Coon." But it came as a shock to genuine music lovers to have this—an encore—as a wind-up to last night's recital at the Theatre Royal. Still it has to be admitted that Miss Garrison invested this relic of the coon age of the music hall with such charm as to merit another encore. But Miss Garrison should know that there are American negro spirituelles worthy a place in the folk song section of her programme.

It was this section which "pleased mightily" especially the German and Scottish ones: For the rest the items were of a diversified nature—from Grand Opera to drawing room ballad—all serving to show the range and flexibility of Miss Garrison's voice. Rossignol (song without words) by Saint-Saens was oddly interesting. Seemingly an exercise for the voice, it had strange touches of melody. Mr. George Siemon accompanied in artistic fashion. His setting to Christina Rosetti's happy lines beginning "My heart is like a singing bird" was most interesting. It lacks nothing in the way of musical technique and is in keeping with the sentiment of the verses. Miss Garrison received a warm welcome and gave a number of encores.
