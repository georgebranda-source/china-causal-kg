## [1] CLUBS IN MALAYA

**SOME FINANCIAL PROBLEMS.**

**MORALITY OF THE CHIT SYSTEM.**

"Indging by the long reports of its meetings which appear in our Singapore contemporaries," says the "Strat Echo," the members of the Singapore Cricket Club have no objection to discussing their affairs in public, so it is permissible to take a recent meeting as the text for a few comments on club finance. The meeting concerned the vexed and long-standing question of chit. The committee of the S.C.C. had notified the members of the framing of a new bye-law which provides that all members' bills shall be payable on the fifteenth day of the month following the end of the month to which they supply. Twenty-one members, apparently scentings an encroachment on cherished privileges, requested the calling of a meeting to explain the necessity of the new bye-law, which has the effect of shortening by about a month the period of grace previously allowed to a member in which to scollids account. At the meeting the twenty-one were different in expressing an opinion. Only one of them spoke, and his contention was merely that the change should have been affected by an alteration of rule and not by the passing of a bye-law. The large majority of those present supported the committee, who according to the chairman, "had spent many weary hours on the question of the framing of the bye-law as the only solution that they could find."

The Singapore Cricket Club is certainly not the only institution of its kind in Malaya which has trouble regarding accounts. More than one club posts a list of defaulters for amounts running into very respectable amounts, and it is rather melancholy reflection on the spirit of the times that to be posted in a club does not now seem to be thought quite the shame and disgrace that it was in the old days. In some cases the question of defaulters became so serious that it was decided to make an entire sweep of the time-honoured Eastern chit system and institute either cush deposits or payment for drinks as ordered.

Two notable examples are, of course, the Penang Cricket Club and the famous "Spotted Dog," of Kuala Lumpur, where the credit system was abolished several years ago with apparently benefits which outweighed the inconvenience caused to members who pay their duties regularly. Other clubs catering for junior men have hesitated to follow a lead which has, we think, very much to recommend it. They argue that members should not be robbed of their credit privileges, while in some cases it is even urged that a cash system would cause members to spend less and the club would not be able to pay its way. Such an argument is surely not to be taken seriously. A club which depends for its maintenance on members spending more than its good for them at the bar—especially a club which is supposed to exist to promote the interests of sport—has very small claim to the right to exist at all.

There is a good deal to be said for the view that subscriptions and other dues should cover a club's overhead charges and drinks should be sold at its near cost price as possible. The custom of ordering drinks "for the good of the house" as is done in some hotels, is surely not applicable to our clubs. The treating habit and the fatal ease with which bills can be run up is a cause of regret to those who take the time to think of these things, and while no one wants to see a young man become a hermit and a excuse it is generally admitted that "shouting rounds" has been and is a great deal overdone. Most of us know from experience the extraordinary ease with which a club bill of a hundred dollars and more can be accumulated: Is it right for a young man with a salary of three or four hundred dollars a month to be led into habits like these?

## [2] A HEALTHY CHECK.

Having to make a cash deposit and to renew, it when the amount is exhausted has a healthy check on undue expenditure. The member benefits and the club, even if its receipts on paper are not so large, profits considerably by having no bad debts.

The problem of chits is a very old and hoary one, of course, but it is always with us and it has not been solved yet. Hotel and store managers say they would like to see chits abolished and we give their stated opinion for what it may be worth; personally, we are not quite sure. There may be something in the assertion that at the prices' charged the honest customers pay for the losses on the others. In any case the custom still goes on. A young man new to the place can give a large order in a store, and if he is known to be in employment and is presumably of good standing he can obtain credit. The same young man can go to a hotel and give a dinner party to half

a-dozen people, complete with champagne and accessories. If he is not on the wrong side of their books already, the obliging management will trust him for the bill. We suppose it works out all right, and we do not presume to teach hotel and store managers their business; but the effect on the young man cannot be other than unhealthy. Unless he is more than usually strong-minded and has a sense of proportion it is very likely to get him into a wrong way. If living and working in the East will be to his detriment instead of to the advantage. He cannot do it at home; why should he have these extraordinary facilities for getting into debt out here?

## [4] WHAT EACH GETS.

Mr. Justice M'Cardle delivered his judgment in the Dennistoun case. In brief Colonel Dennistoun wins on the bigger issue, and his former wife wins on the question of money lent (except that barred by the Statute of Limitations). "I am bound to apply the rules of the law as I conceive them to exist," said the Judge.

Damages amounting to £5,000 were awarded to Mrs. Dennistoun by the jury in Mr. Justice M'Cardie's Court as compensation by reason of Colonel Dennistoun's refusal in January, 1924, to support her in case of necessity. This was a pendant to the jury's decision that Colonel Dennistoun had made a verbal agreement—although not the one pleaded—to support her in necessity if she did not claim alimony from the French Courts.

In the jury's view this was intended to be a binding contract, and it was not part of a collusive arrangement for divorce.

## [7] 一

Dr. Frederick Cook, the New York physician who claimed to have discovered the North Pole and scaled Mount M'Kinley, entered Fort Leavenworth Prison in Kansas to serve 15 years as a penalty for using the mails to defraud.

The man who bluffed the world with his imaginative story of discovery left Fort Worth, Texas, where he was tried and convicted, smiling as genially as when enthusiasts. New York crowds shouldered him and placed garlands of roses about his neck. It was "an revoir, not good-bye," he said, and declared that he was looking forward to employing his time in prison with profit to himself and the State, as he hoped he would be permitted to practise in the prison hospital. When his time is up he will return to Texas, "the scene of his bitterest trouble," to take up life anew.

For some years following Cook's exposure as a fraud by Admiral Peary, the real discoverer of the North Pole, the doctor disappeared in South America, and little was known of his activities. A few years ago he returned and conceived the plan of organising into the Petroleum Producers' Association a number of concerns owning worthless oil lands and selling the stock to gullable "investors." For the past 16 months Cook has been in jail in Texas awaiting the result of his appeal from his sentence, which was based chiefly on Judge Killett's excoriating denunciatory address describing the former explorer as one of the biggest lars in the world's history. Cook still has many friends who intend to petition President Coolidge for his pardon.

## [8] AN ELUSIVE MOON

**MYSTERY OF SATURN'S TENTH SATELLITE.**

The planet Saturn has a mysterious moon which has engaged the attention of many.

The moon is called Themis, and is officially known as the tenth satellite. For 20 years or more controversy has debated whether it really exists.

The late Professor Barnard, who was one of the most skilled of American observers, could not find 'Themis with the 40-inch telescope at Yerkes.

The satellite is described in scientific sources of Information as a body of the 17th magnitude (which is exceedingly faint) and as being placed at a distance from Saturn of over 900,000 miles.

Many astronomers believe the object to have been merely a spurious effect of the photographic plate, an effect which was interpreted as the image of a satellite.

The only observations of it were made by Professor Pickering in 1903, and these were entirely photographic. Since 1903 Themis has persistently refused to reveal itself.

## [9] MAN OF WEALTH

**4 WIVES AND 70 POLQ PONIES:**

The Maharaja of Jodhpur, the wealthiest man in the P. and O. liner Narkunda, homeward bound from Bombay with such a passenger list of wealthy people that it has been called "the richest ship that ever left India," arrived in London on a five months' visit. A special train rushed him and his party across France from Marseilles to Boulogne, and another from folkes to London. The Maharaja brought four wives with him, and great privacy was observed when they disembarked. When every body else had left the wives were carried direct to a saloon carriage in closed sedan chairs. Native servants surrounded the sedans; known as "polti," the wives being "purdahnusheen," meaning that no man may see them.

The Maharaja, besides a suite of 20, brought seven polo players. Fifty-one polo ponies and retinos have already arrived in England, and are now being stabled at Minehead. Somerset

Fifteen polo ponies for the Maharaja's team arrived at Plymouth by the British India liner Pomale, making a string of about 70. They travelled well, only once refusing food owing to rough weather. Each pony is worth about £450.
