## [1] STRIKE EXTENDS.

It should be noted that for convenience sake all meals at the Hotel are being served in the grill room and that they are of the very best. To-day, at tiffin an air of cheerfulness pervaded the room, aided at times by the band. Mrs. Taggart and others were prominent and the Hotel is to be complimented on what it is doing.

## [2] Exodus: Continues.

<sub>section under [1] STRIKE EXTENDS.</sub>

Long before sunrise, in fact within a short time of the incoming river steamers discharging their passengers, hundreds of Chinese had swarmed on to the wharves determined to obtain a passage for Canton or the native village. At daylight this morning the vessels due to leave this evening were absolutely full of passengers. Little or no cargo was worked, but a constant stream of men and women, all carrying luggage, were turned back. A conservative estimate places the number shut-out at 10,000. Many of those going down were domestic' servants, ostensibly from foreign households.

This morning some households still had their servants and were able to get their meals, but the majority of the more fortunate employers were informed that their employees would, reluctantly, as in nearly every case, have to leave about tiffin time.

## [3] Scouts Helping

<sub>section under [1] STRIKE EXTENDS.</sub>

The Hongkong Boy Scout's Association is doing its "bit" and arrangements in hand by which this help will be considerably extended if necessary. At present there are several qualified Scouts working in hospitals and many others are holding themselves in readiness for all sorts of other work, down to working in lifts if necessary. The Rev. G. T. Waldegrave, Commissioner, informed us on enquiry that all Scouts had been asked to report to Headquarters. The parade arranged to take place at Government House on Wednesday had been cancelled.

## [4] Rumours About To-morrow.

<sub>section under [1] STRIKE EXTENDS.</sub>

Many are the rumours about tomorrow. Those who correctly predicted on Saturday night that the trams would stop on Sunday now say that the beef and other stalls at the market may have to shut down tomorrow and that the engineeringers and firemen on harbour ferries may stay away.

Vernacular papers suspended publication this morning but extras were issued.

Hundreds of workers are crossing the Territories to travel by train to Canton, A China Mail reporter was informed that had it not been for the rain yesterday and to-day, a much larger number would have trekked miles but refrained for fear of weiting their personal effects.

## [5] Earlier Developments.

<sub>section under [1] STRIKE EXTENDS.</sub>

The strike which commenced on Friday has now developed considerably and affects all classes and almost all industries and businesses.

The boys at the Peak Hotel began to leave shortly before tiffin on Saturday and the remainder went after tiffin. The exodus, from the Peak Club took place on Saturday afternoon, but this did not interfere with the dance in the evening, refreshments being served and the necessary washing-up done with skill and dexterity, by the members themselves.

The Chinese staff at Repulse Bay did not leave until tiffin on Sunday. It was stated that between four and five hundred Chinese engaged in the two hotels—the Peak Hotel and the Repulse Bay Hotel—had gone. A few of the boys from the Hongkong Hotel left during the course of yesterday morning, and last night all but one or two had left. At all the hotels, however, careful and well-planned arrangements have been made to "carry on" as well as is possible in the circumstances.

The boys began to leave the houses in the Peak district on Sunday morning. Previously there had been no suggestion of a strike.

During the night, however, the following notice, in Chinese, was stated to have been left at a considerable number of residences:—

Gentlemen,— I his is to inform you that in consequence of the recent incidents in Shanghai, causing workers and students to suffer cruel deaths, our corps has determined to carry out a general strike on and from Sunday next. Anybody who fails to join the strike will get from our corps extraordinary treatment.

This precious document is said to have been found on the doorsteps of the houses early in the morning. Its effect was extraordinary.

When the employees of the Tramway Company finished their work at Saturday mid-night, there was no reference to any possibility of a strike. The drivers and conductors, however, failed to put in an appearance on Sunday morning and consequently yesterday no trams were running.

## [6] *(no headline: column 2)*

At the tiffin given in honour of H.E. the Governor at the Hongkong Club on Saturday, His Excellency made a passing reference to the possibility of his approaching departure being postponed on account of the strike. "If this nonsense continues," H.E. declared, "I am going to see it through, and I do not think the arrangement will be a comfortable one for those responsible for my delay." In view of the developments, since this speech was delivered it seems highly improbable that H.E. the Governor will leave on Saturday as arranged.

## [9] WHAT IS ART?

**LORD SUMMER'S APHORISMS**

The outstanding feature of the annual dinner of the Royal Institute of British Architects, held at the Trocadero Restaurant, was the happy wit of the speech in which Lord Sumner proposed the toast of "The Arts."

What, he asked, is an artist? He had written it down, for this reason: there was a minor art, unworthy of being included in the arts, which was called the art of dialectic, or argument, and the secret of that art was wisely to choose your axiom, to proceed rapidly and unexpectedly to conclusions drawn from the question, and to know the precise moment when to beg the question. (Laughter.) That was the true secret of political reasoning. "An artist," proceeded Lord Summer, "is one who creates beauty by the outward expression of his inmost self through a medium which he has selected. What follows from that? First of all, by very definition, an artist is a monstrous egotist. He wishes to express his inmost self and stamp himself on the world. It follows that his highest pleasure is to do so, and, like the blossoming aloe, when once in a hundred years he has achieved that object, the time has come for him to pass away. (Laughter) I think another thing follows from that. His sole pleasure is the creation of beauty. Beauty when created is property of no one; it belongs to the world; it is the artist's gift to his fellow, but inferior, men. (Laughter.) The opposite of it is utility. In proportion as the artist makes anything that is useful he ceases to be an artist. (Laughter) "But that which is the property of all the world," continued Lord Summer, "ought not to be sold for a great or even a small price; it is like pure air or pure water, and on the other hand it is only that which is useful which mankind can reasonably be called upon to pay for. It follows if an artist should sink to such a depth that he can sell his work he is ceasing to be an artist. (Laughter). He is entering the ranks of the profession (Laughter.)
"Art is the presentation of the ideal by the manifestation of the impossible. It is a production of poetic illusion for prosaic material; it is the achievement of the subjective reality in the objective garb of plastic constructive materials. I thought that covered the ground until I came across another aphorism: 'Art is a subject on which all mankind talks the most absolute bosh.' (Laughter.)

What is the democratic view of art? I think it is summed up in this proportion: 'Art is what gives me pleasure, great art is what gives me great pleasure. Provided I enjoy it, it doesn't matter whether it gives other fellows pain or not. (Laughter.) That is why the cubist, futurist, or vorticist painter is so truly democratic. (Laughter.) The agony he inflicts on those who are rash enough to cast an eye over his deathless works is pleasure to him. There are similar successes in architecture. (Laughter.) I have seen in the Press a large number of most interesting pictures of houses that are being built in this country to make homes for heroes. All appear to me to have the one great element of utility that they enable tenants to get houses which can never by any possibility pay the owners of them; but there are some of them, I grieve to say, which appear to have been built for blinded heroes — (laughter)—and which it would be inhuman to call upon anybody else to occupy. (Laughter.)

"Is it not true to say that the Arts are a civilised nation's pride? The Arts, however, we may understand, or misunderstand them, are the things, each in its different way, to which all of us send out our hearts. They are the soul of life; they are things without which we would rather die—and without the Arts the nations will die. (Cheers)"

Sir Frank Dicksee (President of the Royal Academy) and Sir Lionel Earle (Permanent Secretary to his Majesty's Office of Works) responded.

Earlier in the evening Viscount Peel (First Commissioner of Works), submitting the toast of "The Royal Institution of British Architects and its Allied Societies," said he thought the Government had shown in a very practical way its sense of importance of architecture by including for the last three or four Governments the First Commissioner of Works in the Cabinet. He regretted to think that there were no architects in the Cabinet; they had artists—(laughter) literary men, and even poets, but no architect, and he doubted if there were many, if any, architectural either in the House of Commons or the House of Lords. But he did not think anybody could attack the present or any other Government for being other than generous in its architectural tastes. The House of Commons carried on business under what he might call British Gothic; the judicial limb of the Government undoubtedly had some connection with the French Gothic of the fourteenth century; but our administrative offices were very curious lot indeed.

## [10] PRINCIPAL TENANTS:

An important judgment was given in the Summary Court this morning, the effect of which is that a principal tenant cannot terminate the tenancy of a sub-tenant for any illegal act on his part.

The Rents Ordinance empowers the lessor to do this if "any person residing with a tenant" shall have been convicted of using the premises for an unlawful purpose, but Mr. Justice Wood held that this definition could not be applied to a sub-tenant of a lessee residing on the premises.

Under the Ordinance, Mr. Justice Wood pointed out, an application for possession could succeed if the domestic tenement was reasonably required for occupation as a residence for the lessor or "any person residing with him," but it could scarcely be held that the lessor could claim this privilege in respect of any sub-tenant of his own.

His Honour said that it might quite rightly be held that his construction meant that any attempts of the owners to suppress illegal use of their property might be rendered abortive, but nevertheless it seemed clear that the legislation purported to regulate only the relations of the lessee with the immediate lessor and peersons under his control.

## [11] DEFECTIVE BRAKES

**CAR DRIVER WARNED BY THE MAGISTRATE.**

A licensed motor car driver named Chan Kwai was charged at the Central Magistracy this morning with a breach of traffic regulations and driving a car with defective brukes. The prosecution was a sequel to an accident in which a small boy, a woman, and an old man were injured.

The case was settled by the accused undertaking to pay a total of $30 as compensation to the injured persons. The accused received a warning from the Bench to be more careful in future.

## [12] ARMS SEIZURE

**TWO CHINESE REMANDED.**

An arms seizure of considerable size was effected by the Police on a search of the s.s. Benvrackie. The haul comprised ten Mauser pistols and stocks; two automatics, 22 revolvers and 5,402 rounds of ammunition.

Two Chinese members of the crew were charged at the Kowloon Magistracy this morning with the unlawful possession of the arms and remanded.

The Water Police executed a search warrant at the Kowloon Hotel yesterday and arrested a Chinese, who had in his possession a revolver and five rounds of ammunition.

## [16] COLLEGE BAZAAR.

In spite of adverse circumstances the faith of the promoters of St. Stephen's College Bazaar in carrying on with the arrangements was justified and the Building fund will benefit considerably as a result. The Bazaar concluded on Saturday afternoon and evening.
At three o'clock Sir Henry Pollock, K.O., who was accompanied by Lady Pollock and Miss Middleton Smith, introduced in the Great Hall Mrs. Chow Shou-son and spoke of the keen interest she had always taken in the welfare of St. Stephen's. Mrs. Chow Shou-son acknowledged through Mr. Li Chor-chi and in opening the Bazaar wished it and the Building Fund every success. Mrs. Chow Shou-son was then presented with a memento of the occasion by Mr. Chan Cheung-nin, who has been largely responsible for the general arrangements of the Bazaar.
The Rev. H. W. Hewitt, Principal of the College, thanked Mrs. Chow Shou-son both for her presence and the interest she had always taken in St. Stephen's. He would also take the opportunity of thanking all who had worked toward the success of the undertaking and had given unsparingly of their time and labour.

The outdoor entertainments were the same as had proved such an attraction the day previously and they drew another large crowd in spite of the heat. The stalls in the Great Hall were also extensively patronised and the appearance of the Colony's champion ping pong players in a specially arranged tournament for a cup was the signal for a general rush to the room in which it was held.

In the evening a variety entertainment and cinema show were given to a large "house."
