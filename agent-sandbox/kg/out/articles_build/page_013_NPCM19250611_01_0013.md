### Pictures on the Wall.

It is a safe rule always to hang pictures at eye level (says the "Manchester Guardian.") Above that height small pictures look out of place, and large pictures reflect so much light that it becomes impossible to see the subject. To keep a picture hanging straight make a twist in the picture cord before having it on the book.

Never tilt pictures by propping them on a nail. The large frame that hangs from the rail or cornice will have sufficient title of its own, while the very small picture should rest absolutely flat against the wall and be suspended from neat hooks, hidden behind its own frame. The very heavy oil painting should be suspended upon chains; for no picture cord will sustain the strain of a really heavy frame indefinitely. Use two chains, one, from each side of the picture, and run them up to two separate hooks. A couple of large-headed nails, one at each end, will also minimise the strain. Where the walls of a house are damp, or there is fear of the pictures marking the wallpaper, screw in two large-headed nails so that the picture rests against them and not against the wall. Smart clothes in the great world of London are quite, quite done for the daytime, to the distress, I suppose, of the more gaudy dress designers and to the makers of silks and satins and embroideries. Even for a very formal lunch, or for that now rather rare thing, a sea party, people wear glorified golf clothes (writes Viola Tree in the "Evening Standard.") There is limitless field for greys and browns and for accessories like stockings and handkerchiefs and belts to contrast and to harmonise. But gone are my dear black charmeuse and the charming Russian embroiders of yester year. A straight blue serge can be helped out with a frill, but honestly it is not really the way to dress. A helmet hat rammed on the head and a loose coat over a few other knitted coats in the smartest, and tailor-mades, rather severt, are worn by the young with their high-collared sweaters like jolly Jack Tars. They look nice, but so very, very hot, one tight sleeve over another. Extravagant lingerie is not barred, but it must never show with sports clothes—a shoulder strap or a piece of creeping filet would be absolutely fatal to the ensemble. Everyone to her taste, but where is the fun of the moors and marshes country if one goes about London just the same, looking as if one had a gun concealed under the elbow?
Love Romance of a Nm.

A romantic love affair of a nun is reported from Bergamo, on the Swiss-Italian frontier. An old and wealthy Italian woman who was suffering from an incurable disease, and knew that she had not long to live, asked the Mother Superior of a neighbouring Catholic convent to send her a nun daily in order to read the Bible and pray with her. A pretty nun twenty-three years of age was chosen for the purpose, and during her daily visits frequently met the woman's sob, an officer in the Navy, and the couple fell in love with each other. The officer proposed marriage, and was supported by his mother. The nun, who was in a terrible dilemma, as she had to choose between her love and vows, recently confessed her "crime" to the Mother Superior, who took a lenient view of the affair. "Child," she said, "remain in your cell as a week and carefully examine her demands of your heart and con science, some vows, and then tell me your decision." The marriage will take place shortly.

## [15] G.B.S. And Stage Licences

<sub>section under [13] ODDS AND ENDS</sub>

Mr. George Bernard Shaw, in an interview published in the London. "Evening Standard," suggests a remedy for the cleansing of the "decadent" stage. The public can and inevitably will decide, he says, whether plays are really shocking or disgusting. They may go to see plays that make current morality ridiculous and hold up something better. But they won't consent to be shocked or disgusted or take their families to see disgusting plays. The theatrical manager who produces pieces that are merely vicious will be ruined in no time. It might be well if theatres were licensed exactly as our public-houses are. Such a system need involve no interference with plays as such. Similarly, the licensing of theatres should be in the hands of some public body, with plenty of access for anyone desiring to lodge a legitimate complaint. And a theatre licence should be revoked only for some judicial reason; not for any puritan reason, such as that the theatre is a gate of hell. The question of revoking a licence should be settled by a vote of the whole council. Though I have been attacking the British stage censorship for years, I have never suggested that impunity was the alternative to a censorship. There can never be absolute freedom for the stage so long as you have the police.

## [16] 'The Human Walk.'

<sub>section under [13] ODDS AND ENDS</sub>

Lecturing on Wednesday at 74 Grosvenor Street, W., on "The Human Walk," Mr. Clement Jeffery, the well-known spinal specialist, said that the cultivation of the walking pillar-box was as absurd as that of the Victorian hour-glass. "The lines of the human figure are exquisitely curved," continued Mr. Jeffery, "and present feminine fashions in shape as well as in walk are physiologically wrong." Mr. Jeffery added that the upright position was achieved by man only after countless ages of striving; it had enabled him to dominate all other living beings by affording him time to use his fore-limbs for the handling of weapons and tools. "The erect position," said Mr. Jeffery, "has a powerful influence on the intellect, and it has been observed that children paralysed in the trunk and lower limbs show an immediate improvement in their mental life when placed erect."
