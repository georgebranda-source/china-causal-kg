## [26] HUMANE SLAUGHTER

The Sanitary Committed of the Corporation of London recommend the passing of Clause 9(B) of the Ministry of Health's model by laws relating to slaughterhouses, which provides that animals for slaughter shall be first effectually stunned with a suitable mechanically operated instrument.

The question has been investigated for the committee by the medical officer of health (Dr. W. J. Howarth), the superintendent of the Metropolitan Cattle Market (Mr. J. R. Hayhurst), and the veterinary surgeon at the Central Meat Market (Mr. T. Dunlop Young).

They recommended that mechanically operated stunning instruments be used for cattle, calves, horses, and pigs, that is captive-bolt instrument be specified to the exclusion of one releasing a bullet, and that classes of instruction be organised for teaching slaughtermen the anatomy of the skull in different animals, and the proper use and care of the instrument.

A rider recommends the exclusion of sheep. Dr. Howarth, however, differs on this point, and appends a recommendation to include sheep.

## [30] MORE APPENDICITIS.

**50000 SERIOUS CASES A YEAR.**

In some districts to and around London appendicitis is unusually prevalent. There are large numbers of cases in all the great hospitals. The disease is steadily increasing year by year.

"There are more deaths now than ten years ago," said a surgeon. "In the past ten years 28,000 people have died of this disease. We have no means of knowing how many people are attacked, but calculating from the deaths there must be more than 50,000 serious cases a year."

Dr. James Sherren, surgeon to the London Hospital, said that a sudden severe pain in the abdomen is nearly always the first symptom of appendicitis. The need for submitting to instant surgical treatment is shown by results at the London and St. Thomas's Hospital published by Dr. Joseph E. Adams, in the "British Medical Journal."

In cases operated on immediately mortality was less than 1 per cent, when the inflammation extended and became local peritonitis mortality after operation was 6 per cent.; when there was further extension to general peritonitis mortality ranged from 20 to 29 per cent.
