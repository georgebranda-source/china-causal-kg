## [1] HOME CRICKET.

**ONE BEHIND DR. GRACE.**

Many cricket enthusiasts in Hongkong have been arguing, so I am informed, over the number of centuries in first-class cricket standing to the name of J. B. Hobbs, the Surrey professional and All-England player.

On Wednesday this week I stated that his total was 125, which is one behind that of Dr. W. G. Grübe whose record of 126 has stood for some years. This is correct, but the details I gave were wrong. I said that up to the end of last season Hobbs had made 110 three-figure scores in first-class cricket; that he made four more in Australia; and that he put up 11 at Home this season.

Now for, the correct figures.
"Wisden's Cricketers' Almanack," says that Hobbs had 110 centuries at the end of the last English season. To a friend who has proved his contention beyond doubt, I am indebted for the following. In the first, second, and third Test Matches in Australia last winter Hobbs made one century in each match. His only other three-figure score in the M.C.C. Tour was made against the Australian Juniors at Sydney which, as my friend points out, "cannot possibly be ranked as first-class." Therefore the total when the present season began was 113. Up to about the end of June Hobbs had made 10 centuries. Since then he knocked up three figures in one of the Gentlemen v. Players matches and the last century was against Kent at Blackheath, the result of which was published last Wednesday. Therefore the total to date for this season is 12. Added to 113, the total becomes 125, which is one behind Dr. Grace's 126. Accordingly Hobbs needs one more century to tie and two to establish a new record.

In this connection another friend has reminded me that Hobbs will put up another record if he gets two more centuries this season. The most hundreds made in one season is 13, a feat accomplished by Mr. C. B. Fry in 1901 by T. Hayward in 1906, and "Pat" Hendren in 1923. As Hobbs already has 12 centuries this season, two more would make his aggregate 14, which would be another record.

For statistic faddists I have also looked up the following:—Hobbs is a batsman who will take all kinds of chances when he has passed a hundred and his side is not in need of runs, and it is not surprising that his name does not appear under many headings in the records. Not including this season he has made four centuries in succession, this being in 1920. Other records up to the end of last season include an opening partnership of 352, with Hayward. for Surrey in 1909—by no means the highest as the best is 554 for Yorkshire in 1898—and another of 323 runs, with Rhodes, against Australia in 1911-12. Hobbs has never scored 3,000 runs in a season, his highest aggregate being 2,827 (in 50 innings) in 1920, nor has he ever topped the Englisha batting averages.

Of those who are still playing, C. P. Mead, the Hampshire professional, has the next highest number of centuries, viz., 80 up to the end of last season. Next come W. G. Quaife with 68, P. A. Perrin 65, F. E. Woolley 63, and J. W. Hearne 56.

W.P.C.

## [4] POWERS AND CHINA

**JOINT DECLARATION ON OUTLOOK.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*London, July 23.*

According to the “Daily Telegraph’s” diplomatic correspondent Britain, America and Japan have virtually agreed to the text of a joint declaration dealing with the present outlook in China, re-affirming the readiness of the Powers to assist China to realise her reasonable aspirations and undertaking most sympathetically to consider an early settlement of outstanding issues. It points out that at present the lives and property of foreigners are being threatened and apprehensions are even entertained, in some quarters, lest a movement should materialise to deprive the Powers of their rights under existing treaties. It hoped that such apprehensions are unwarranted, but as long as these disturbing conditions continue, they must necessarily impede the desire of the Powers to express active sympathy with Chinese national aspirations.

## [5] HANKOW INCIDENT.

**CHINESE ARRANGE. PEACE INTERVIEW.**

**FIVE POINTS TO SETTLE.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*Hankow, July 23.*

On the initiative of the Chinese authorities an interview took place this morning between the British Consul General and the Commissioner for Foreign Affairs with a view to calming public opinion and securing a local settlement of the Hankow incident.

## [6] *(no headline: column 3)*

The Commissioner submitted five points for consideration.

Points 1 and 2 referred to the protection and defence of the Concession. It was agreed, in principle, that a system of cooperation should be devised between the British and Chinese authorities with a view to the Chinese Police and Military supplementing the Concession Police in the event of serious trouble.

Point 3 referred to the compensation for Chinese killed and wounded on June 11 and was reserved.

Point 4, desiring the removal of Messrs. "Butterfield's" Sikh watchmen from the native city, was reserved.

Point 5 required an assurance that there will be no further mal-treatment of Chinese by the Concession Police.

The Consul General expressed willingness to issue warning notices of the necessity to refrain from violence but pointed out that corresponding notices by the Chinese enjoining good behaviour and non-resistance to police authority were desired.

## [7] A Chinese Report.

Tuchun Hsiao Yiao-nan has telegraphed to Peking suggesting that the first five points of the Hankow incident be discussed at Hankow and the last seven at Peking. The Chief Executive, Tuan Chi-jui, has referred the suggestion to the Foreign Affairs Committee for consideration.

## [11] AFTER 42 HOURS.

**PROGRESS OF RESCUED**

**FALSE NATIVE REPORTS.**

Various rumours were current during the week as to the fate of the 13-year old Chinese girl who was rescued from the ruins of Po Hing Fong early on Sunday morning after having been entombed for 42 hours.

The vernacular papers stated on Tuesday that the girl died the day after her rescue. According to the report, the cause of death was attributed to the girl having been given too much aerated water and discuits so soon after her enforced starvation, which brought on a swelling of certain organs! This report was later contradicted with an explanation that another girl of the same age died at the Tung Wah Hospital; and that a mistake was made in assuming that it was the rescued girl, Hung Mui, who had died.

Another rumour which had gained currency was that the girl's leg had come into contact with a body had had to be unputtered dying to blood-poisoning setting up

In the account of the rescue which appeared in the "China Mail" on Monday, it was stated that the girl was progressing favourably, and that according to the hospital authorities her injuries were not of a very serious nature. This progress has been maintained as a "China Mail" representative ascertained last evening at the Tung Wah Hospital, where the girl occupies a separate room. The girl's leg was wrapped in bandages, and she stated that she felt great pain in the knee, which was swollen and stiff. The swelling was apparently inevitable after her release as during the period of her imprisonment, a beam, pinned her leg down above the knee and stopped the circulation of blood. This in a way was fortunate, as she would otherwise have bled to death from the wounds in the lower part of the leg.

This girl is of delicate build, and the trials which she had to undergo have rendered her wan and thin. Since her rescue she has been kept on a special diet, and it is hoped that she will gradually regain her strength as she recovers from her injuries.

Put to her it was gathered that she was in total darkness the whole time and very little air found its way into her "prison." She stated that loud sounds of digging and voices reached her from above, and she had been shouting for a long time before she was heard by the workers.

As has been stated in the previous report, the girl's mother was killed in the disaster.
