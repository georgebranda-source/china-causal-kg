## [1] EDUCATION IN CHINA

**WHAT OF THI FUTURE?**

Sermon preached by the Rev. W. T. Featherstone, M.A., Headmaster of the Diocesan Boys' School Hongkong, at St. John's Cathedral, Hongkong, on Sunday, June 28, 1925, on Education in China.

1.--The Advantages inherent in the Chinese system of Education.

3.—The Future of Chinese Education.

The Advantages (1) State Education.—The old system of education in China is the oldest system of Government Education known to history. The schools described under the early feudal period B.C. 2357-1122 were state schools. Of these earliest ages Prof. James Legge says Education was state conducted. The importance of generally instructing the people was acknowledged even before Confucius 550 B.C. It is said in the Book of Rites B.C. 1200 "That for the purposes of education among the ancients, villages had their schools, districts their academies, department their colleges and principalities their universities. This, so far as we know, was altogether superior to what obtained among the Jews, Persians and Syrians of the same period. If we may depend upon these records the schools continued to be supported by the state from the Shang dynasty B.C. 1766 to A.D. 706.

## [2] Chinese Classics.

<sub>section under [1] EDUCATION IN CHINA</sub>

(2) The second advantage was the Uniform Curriculum—based upon the Chinese Classics.

When the Chinese records become entirely historical we find that the multiplication table, Arithmetic, Geometry, the Knowledge of the properties of the right angled triangle and the elements of trigonometry were studied. Instruments for calculations, sundials, and instruments for measuring distances were in use. But the principle course of study in the early curriculum was probably the Chinese Classics.

Confucius B. C. 551-478 made a Compilation of the Chinese Classics which have been used from that day to this and very probably used before that date. We have an impressive testimony to the high estimate in which they were held in the fact that when the schools ceased to be supported by the Government in A. D. 706 the Classical course of study had such a hold upon the people that the private schools continued to teach them as faithfully as the Government Schools. It is difficult to overestimate the value of a uniform Curriculum accepted by every school in the nation, for about 3,000 years in creating uniform type of civilisation.

(3) The third advantage was its Democratic Character. Wu Wang, the Founder of the Chow Dynasty 1122 B.C. ordered that in admitting students to degree no distinction should be made between high and low, rich and poor. His son and heir began his education in a village school. Vacations of 45 days were ordered in order that the land might be prepared for cultivation thus allowing students to assist in Agriculture etc. It was also democratic in the appointment of scholars to public officers.

## [3] Practical Character.

<sub>section under [1] EDUCATION IN CHINA</sub>

(4) The fourth advantage was its practical Character — While the Chinese have deemed a knowledge of Reading, etc. desirable, yet they have not deemed it essential to send to school a boy who was destined to work on a farm or in a shop. Consequently the overwhelming mass of the Chinese are almost destitute of any scholastic training. This does not imply that the common people have no education whatever. Professor Parker writes—“We talk about—“Jack” being a handy man he is nothing to the Chinese. The visual exceptions excepted every Chinese knows the time without a watch; can, at a pinch buy, prepare and cook his own food; wash, patch, if not make clothes; judge the weather; till fields, carry a pole, and its load, indicate the North, manceuvre a punt, sail a boat, catch fish, saddle a horse, tackle animals, walk or ride long distances, sleep anywhere at any moment, take no exercise whatever for any length of time—do anything, go anywhere.” We can see the practical side of education in the small boys working as apprentice craftsmen, painting, working in banks as money changers, etc. Moreover, such training was under the supervision of parents; it was and is often now a common life in the open with small opportunity for vice masters and workmen eat together and discuss problems; all these combined keep the number of degenerates down, result in the

more general spread of knowledge than would seem possible among an illiterate people and leads to a comparatively high level of efficiency in the art of earning a living and maintaining the existing civilisation. Thus there are four advantages in the old system, it was State Education, it was uniform, democratic and practical.

## [4] The Dangers:

<sub>section under [1] EDUCATION IN CHINA</sub>

1.—Its Private Nature.—In A, D. 706 there was a serious revolution in Chinese Education. An imperial Edict stated that in future the salaries of the teachers would be paid by the pupils and not by the State. The Government controlled education after it ceased to support it. Private education, generally speaking, is more narrow than State Education, it lessens the respect for the teacher, it makes the teacher subject to the student and the hired man of his patrons; thus it is often commercial and for personal gain.

2.—Corruption.—Early in the Christian era a proclamation was made saying that all persons who gave 177 lbs. of rice towards a Famine. Fund would receive a Literary Degree; later no less than three ranks of degrees could be bought; later still only those who held literary degrees could hold political office. This sale of degrees and offices has continued to a greater or less extent down to the present day and has been one of the prime causes of political corruption in China. There was bound to be official corruption because officials had to recoup their purchase money.

3.—The third danger is the inherent conservatism of Chinese Education. Education with its training in Classical literature and its reverence for institutions which are old, and, with its knowledge of the failure of countless reform schemes, tends, in general to timidity and conservatism. The schools of Egypt rejected Moses, the Schools of Greece rejected Socrates, the Schools of the Scribes and Pharisees rejected Jesus and Paul, the Universities of Europe opposed the Reformation, the leaders of the Church of Rome forced Galileo to recant, the Universities of France opposed the French Revolution, the University of Oxford rejected Methodism but nursed the reactionary Anglo-Catholic movement.

## [5] Aristocracy Of Learning.

<sub>section under [1] EDUCATION IN CHINA</sub>

But in no other country has education been so conservative a force as in China. And the exclusive exaltation of the learned class which created an aristocracy of learning has been much more subtle, insidious and far reaching and full of corruption than for example, the aristocracy of blood in Europe and the rising aristocracy of wealth in America. Thus we see four advantages and three dangers. I suppose it is the dangers of which we are thinking most to-day, the system of Private Education which has bad discipline and corruption, and the inherent conservation which produces stupidity and timidity. In these Strikes we see all these things.

Now let us briefly consider the Future of Chinese Education.

To do so we first of all require an insight or knowledge of its history.

Let me mention a few facts.
In A.D. 100 the Chinese knew
of the procession of the equinoxes.
In A.D. 301 Sun spots were
observed.

In B.C. 540 the Chinese had a fair knowledge of Botany, medicine and human anatomy.

In A.D. 286 the Caesarian operation was performed.
In the 12th Century inoculation

In or about 926 A.D. the Chinese Classics were printed, that is five hundred years before the Bible was printed.

Between the years 1644 and 1900 China got into touch with the West and during the last hundred years education was more widely diffused. The Jesuits gave excellent scientific training and the Protestants and the Roman Catholics opened Public Schools in many parts of China. Since the Chino-Japanese War in 1894 and the Boxer Uprising in 1900 there has been a great impetus to foreign learning.

In 1901 an Imperial Decree was issued about founding Universities, Schools and Village Schools; unfortunately this Decree had no results and existed on paper only.

In 1905 and 1912 Commissions on Education were appointed. Since 1905 the Education of the Chinese was taken over, nominally, by the Government. From A.D. 706 to A.D. 1905 China had relied on Private Schools.

To-day in China you will find several millions of Scholars in a large number of Private Schools in which, generally speaking, there is a great lack of discipline; secondly, there are about one and a half millions of scholars in the Chinese Government. Schools where there is liable to be�

very often the Teachers' salaries are from three to nine months overdue; and thirdly there are about 350,000 students in Protestant and Roman Catholic Schools.

Those figures are all approximate figures and cannot be regarded as exact. This short review furnishes us with some basis for a forecast of the future development of Education in China.

(1) The future Education will undoubtedly be supported by the Government, it will be democratic and should be practical. While Classical Education tends to a timid conservatism, Education with inspiration has produced such reformers as Moses, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Plato, Paul, Luther, Calvin, Knox, Bacon, Newton, Galileo, and Darwin, and those men of strong personality have received their discipline at the great seats of learning. The hope of China's future rests upon the adoption, gradually, of the opportunity of universal Education maintained partly at Government expense and partly by fees or Scholarships. The fees need only be very small for poor people. In England and America and in the Far East a great mistake has been made during the last fifty years of giving Education too cheaply. Too much has been given for nothing, consequently there has been lack of respect and loss of discipline. The Education in China above all things should be practical; it need not be compulsory. I would advocate a system of part-time schools where a scholar who is doing work could attend for a short time daily. A prospective farmer should not be forced to spend the whole of his time within the four walls of a classroom. Ricsha coolies should not be forced to become cheap clerks, poor people who sleep out of door under verandahs in the hot weather should not be compelled to sleep in stuffy cubicles for the good of their health. We should remember that people are generally much happier if they are left alone and not dragooned so much.

## [6] The Second Hope.

<sub>section under [1] EDUCATION IN CHINA</sub>

The second hope is the introduction of Western learning.

Western learning is at hand, a mental revolution is already in progress; but along with Western Science China must preserve the universal industrial training of her children.

The third hope and by far the largest hope for China is in religious and Christian education. With regard to religion it has been said that the Teachings of Confucius are a Providential preparation of China for Christianity even as were the Greek and Roman Ethics and the teachings of the Old Testament. So we ought to encourage a respect for China's Classics along with a knowledge of our own Religion. This religion of ours, its idealism, its purity, its hardness in that it is hard to live up to its ideals, its strength, discipline and spirit is what it wants to-day.

The almost hopeless mess in which China is to-day is due to the lack of discipline and the corruption in the home, in the school and in politics. For example, small boys are petted and pampered and often for fear of losing face, upheld against the master or quietly removed to another school for promotion to a higher class or to a class with a higher name or to a school called a College,—a mild form of corruption; and thus indiscipline and corruption are encouraged. With all charity I would say that it would appear that the greatest need to-day in China is a Father on earth as well as a Father in heaven who, with resolution and kindness without any semblance of bullying, and that has been our attitude in the past, will show no weakness, no hesitation and no doubt.

Let us pray that there will be no need for any very extraordinary measures and let us, calmly and strongly see to it that we, by our example in big and small things will help these three hopes of Universal Education, of the practical introduction of Western Education and of Christian Education.
