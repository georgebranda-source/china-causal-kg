There are four schools in the Colony—situated at Shaukiwan, Wanchai, Shamshuipo and Yaumati—conducted by the Sisters of the Precious Blood.

These schools are attended in all by not less than 250 Chinese children. Sixty-nine of these pupils received rewards on Saturday afternoon for diligent work last year. Mr. Y. P. Law, B.A., Inspector of Schools, presenting them with prizes in the hall of the Catholic Men's Society, Caine Road.

There was a large gathering present, including Sisters of the Precious Blood, Fathers attached to the Cathedral and other institutions, as well as a number of Europeans who take a sympathetic interest in the work of the Sisters and the education of Chinese in the schools under their charge. Needless to remark, there was a considerable number of Chinese, both grown ups and children present, and standing room only was available when the proceedings began.

The proceedings, which were in Chinese throughout, opened with an interesting and enjoyable musical programme given by pupils from the four schools. Those items included concerted choruses and action songs, and although sung in the vernacular, were none the less pleasing to those not understanding Chinese, as the children's voices blended well and provided pleasing harmony.

Following these contributions, Mr. J. S. Shek (Hon. Secretary of the Society of the Slaters of the Precious Blood) introduced Father Shek to read the reports covering the four schools. These showed that all four had a fairly successful year, and that a marked degree of success among the pupils attending them had been recorded. The report noted with satisfaction the number of pupils qualifying for prizes and the number of pupils who had graduated. The school showing the highest number of pupils graduating was Wanchal, with ten.

Mr. Y. P. Law gave a brief address in which he expressed his high appreciation of the work carried out by those who had been in charge of the children and remarked that he had been highly satisfied during his inspection of the schools with the standard reached and the results achieved. Mr. Law also warmly congratulated the successful pupils on their work and concluded by encouraging all to endeavour to reach an even higher standard of success in the ensuing school year.

After Mr. Law had presented the prizes to the children, the programme was concluded with an amusing Chinese play which was thoroughly enjoyed and warmly applauded.

At the request of the four Canton Chambers of Commerce, Mr. Chao-chu Wu, Mayor of Canton and member of the Nationalist Government, attended a special meeting of the members of the four leading Commercial organizations of Canton in order to explain what the Government had done towards bringing about the settlement of the strike, reports the "Canton Gazette" of Friday.

Mr. Wu, whose speech was on much the same lines as the statement issued by the Canton Government last week, sought to acquit the Canton Government of responsibility for the prolonging of the boycott. "Our Government had nothing to do with the strike which the Chinese workers at Hongkong started as a demonstration of protest against the British people in China and the British Government of which the Hongkong Government is a part.

"When the first indications of a strike settlement were mooted, our Government made it clear that if the Hongkong Government was desirous of a settlement, it could not be with the Canton Government but must be with the strikers and people. The Canton Government could not add had no right to intervene as one of the principals."

Nevertheless, continued Mr. Wu, "seeing that the aim and object of the popular strike had been achieved as a protest against the British, and mindful of the fact that the Chinese both in Hongkong and Canton were suffering by the continuance of the strike and boycott, the Canton Government placed no obstacles and rather encouraged or welcomed all attempts put forth to bring about an early settlement between the two parties concerned. Unofficial pourparlers were entered into on the distinct understanding that.

1. The discussions between the Government and Hongkong would be informal and unofficial.

2. The Government was not one of the parties in the desired settlement which had to be effected direct between the strikers and the Hongkong Government.

Our Government had to make this abundantly clear, Mr Wu said, because we did not wish to compromise the Government in the eyes of the world.

Mr. Wu then went into details concerning the Canton Government's part in the negotiations. Coming to the appointment of a Canton official to visit Hongkong. Mr. Wu said: —

"On receipt of the Hongkong Governor's letter, our Government deliberated whether we should or should not send one of our officials down to Hongkong. The first consideration was one of prestige, as on the face of it, there was no reason why we should send one of our officials to Hongkong at the request of the Hongkong Governor. However, on careful consideration, our Government decided that as we were desirous to have the strike settled at an early date, the sooner the better, so as to shorten the sacrifices that the strikers had borne, and the losses that our Chinese merchants in Hongkong and Canton were suffering, we would brush aside the matter of dignity and comply with the request of the Hongkong Governor."

The Canton Government, Mr. W. continued, thus showed its sincerity in desiring to have the strike settled. "Because the visit would be entirely unofficial and private, our Government did not send one of its diplomatic officials but sent a high official from another department.

“This official went down to Hongkong and after discussing the situation with the Hongkong Governor returned, accompanied by a high official from Hongkong, who stayed here for two or three days. Our Government showed the Hongkong official every courtesy and met and conferred with him repeatedly.”

It is surprising that no enterprising showman at Wembley ever realised the unrivalled possibilities of the "skidding basin" a remarkable innovation which may now be seen at the training school of the London General Omnibus Company at Chiswick. The basin, which is there put to severely practical use in the training of bus drivers, resembles a circus ring, except that instead of a sawdust covering there is a morass of lubricating oil, over which drivers have to steer their buses, the "hazards" being posts placed at intervals through which they must drive. The combined thrills of the Racer, the Whip, and the Witching Waves are inevitable on such a surface, but the "pleasures" are largely wasted on the tyro, whose only desire is to pass this severe test and enter upon this three months' probationary service on one of the quieter routes round London.

Approximately sixty men pass through this severe test every wack, and the ability and conduct of London General Omnibus Company drivers and conductors generally prove the efficiency of the school, which is always training new men to supply the needs of the service. As a rule drivers are selected from the ranks of ex-Service men; they must be physically at and of excellent character. There are other things besides actual driving that the tyro must learn. One of them is "courtesy of the road." This lesson was being taught to twenty men in a classroom which a newspaper representative visited recently. The instructor was showing the young drivers how they might assist, or, at least not hinder the flow of traffic at the particularly complicated crossing at Hyde Park corner. In another room the importance of showing courtesy to passengers was being impressed upon conductors, and in this connection Mr. H. Lanadowne, the traffic superintendent, remarked: "We are fortunate in the type of men we are getting. The time has not yet come," he added, humorously, "when we shall require scholastic degrees, but they have to come up to a high standard, and the good looks of the men are even entering into it." The education given at Chiswick also includes training of conductors in quick reckoning.
