## [2] HIVE CAREERS.

**LIFE TASK OF THIRTY-FIVE**

Some time ago a famous German entomologist, Professor Karl von Frisch, constructed a beehive with glass windows and watched the bees "talking" to one another by means of special dances.

His pupil, Mr. G. A. Rosch, using the same technique has been able to observe, the entire career of several bees, from the moment they emerged from their pupae cells to the day of their death.

As soon as the young worker been appeared they were carefully removed from the hive, and marked by "spots of specially prepared paint. They were then put back, and each became as she set about her career, could be watched and tried among the hundreds of her sisters.

It is now established that the life of the average bee is a short and a hard one. She goes through a routine very similar to that of a human being, though it is compressing within a much shorter span. She seldom lives longer than 35 days, though veterans aged 55 days, have been recorded.

He's first task, after she has thoroughly washed and cleaned herself, is to prepare the cells for those who will follow after.

At the age of 3 to 10 days the young worker helps the regular nurses to feed the larvae. She fetches honey and pollen from the common store, and she also busies herself with the cleaning of the hive by carrying all foreign bodies outside it, and often helps the young to creep out of their cells by biting through their lids.

When she is about a fartnight old she takes over a post of great responsibility. She becomes a sentry. With a sagacity almost equal to that of a fully trained human soldier, she keeps her guard, watching over all bees coming in or going out, and if necessary doing battle with rash or straying marauders who endeavour to gain access to the treasure within.

As a rule, the bee becomes a honey gatherer round about the 20th day of her life; but the period may vary a little.

The observations of Mr. Rosch have definitely proven that the routine for all worker bees is the same, and that each one, if necessary, and according to her age, can take over any job in the communal life at short notice.

## [5] SOUTH SEA LIFE

**INSECTS GREAT AND SMALL**

Over 1,300 rare species of insects, many of them undoubtedly now discoveries, have been brought to London by Mias L. E. Cheesman, curator of insects at the London 200.

She went to the South Seas with the expeditionary research ship "St. George," and after a year's voyaging among remote islands made a thorough exploration of the wild parts of Tahiti, Raia, and Bora Bora. "Hitherto insects had been collected only in the cultivated parts of Tahiti," she said.

"The astonishing thing we have to explain is how some of the species established themselves on these islands. Many are quite delicate creatures, and yet they contrived to cross thousands of miles of sea—in some cases, no doubt, in plants and so forth."

"Among them are the tiny Embids, which five in little silken tunnels in the bark of trees," and the brilliantly "coloured Plant Bugs, some only an eighth of an inch in size. Most of the species are small, and required a great deal of searching for." a hard morning's work would frequently produce only a dozen specimens.

Miss Cheesman found great danger from scorpions and centipedes. In places she encountered giant arum lilies with leaves as big as umbrellas and blossoms two feet in length.

In Trinidad, she said, she found a man who sold giant centipedes by the foot! Where they exceeded a foot in length, you paid for the extra inches in proportion.

## [6] IS SCIENCE CRUEL?

**CIVILIZATION AND THE DEATH-RATE**

**POISON GAS v. SEWERS**

It is really true that it is far more dangerous to life and limb to cross the Strand than the Channel, and a hundred times more perilous than a railway journey to Edinburgh, asserts a "Daily Mirror" writer.

The hall-mark of the highest civilisation, to motor-car, in its multifarious embodiments, has increased the death rate of road and street enormously.

The sense of disillusionment oppressing the world, proceeds the writer, arises mainly from a sense of profound disappointment with civilisation. It did not rise to the great occasion. Instead of science, for instance, mitigating the horrors of war, it appears to intensify them. So far from universal education having turned men into angles it seems to have had an effect the exact reverse. The civilised usages of warfare make the Romans, for instance, seem like philanthropists. Nevertheless, is there another side to the shield?

If civilisation kills, it also makes alike; if it is ruthless, it is also merciful. Even in the late war the proportion of those engaged who succumbed was smaller far than formerly, although the aggregate was so large. But had the microbe had its way, as in former wars, that terrible four years would have nearly depopulated the world of its men-folk.

Science may produce poison gas in time of war, but it is down on poison sewers, poison drains and poison food in time of peace.

Three important discoveries which Sir J. D. Bose demonstrated in Calcutta are considered by scientists as of profound significance in developing our knowledge of the evolutionary process of life. Fose, in the presence of the Earl of, Lytton and a distinguished gathering, explained his discovery of the muscular system in plants, and demonstrated the throbbing activity in the interior of trees.

Actual heart of the tree by means of an electric probe in conjunction with a sensitive galvanometer. As soon as the probe was put in contact with the heart, electric signals were automatically recorded by the galvanometer. The heart of plants is similar to the elongated heart of some of the lower animals, such as the earthworm. In an amazing demonstration, Sir J. D. ## [10] JAUNTY MUSIC.

**MODES OF ELDERS:**

**NOT TOO ELEGANTLY GAY!**

Jauntiness seems to be the mode with the youngest of the young musical composers these days. It is a reaction against the modes of their elders—the finished elegance of Ravel and the direminess of Debussy. Ravel is an antique. Debusslam is a cons away, along with nineteenth-century "souffulness," writes Richard Capell, in the "Daily Mail." As a motorist might look on a Romain chariot, so do they consider Wagner and Beethoven.

They declare war on the Profound, the Nobles and the Pathetic. They want a gay music—but not too elegantly gay; rather, cheeky and "legpulling." When the mode was romantic, naturally cheerful young men felt bound to affect a broken heart and to look consumptive. But such a 1925 musician as M. Georges Auric is never to be found in the hour of twilight leaning pensively over the wall of a cemetery. No; he would rather go off boisterously to the circus, and if the cornet in the band persists in misreading the key-signature, it is an inspiration.

The new jainty music is a protest against sham sentiment and the obscurity which conceals hollowness. It may be raucous and crude, but it is frank and unpretentious. Quite often it is very clever, and at its best, as in some of Prokoviev's music, it is clearly spontaneous and exhilaratingly merry.

## [11] WHISKY IN BIBLES.

**MILLIONAIRE DRUNK AS "BOILED OWL:"**

**LADY GOVERNOR TALKS.**

A somewhat humorous situation has developed in the State of Texas, with Mrs. Miriam A. Ferguson, its governor, as the central figure.

Her enemies, whom she identifies with the Ku Klux Klan, threatened to violate the rules of political chivalry by impeaching her for undue sentimentality in pardoning more than 1,000 convicts. To this she has replied by issuing a proclamation offering $100 reward for the arrest and conviction of any rich citizen committing crimes for which poor people have been sent to jail.

In the proclamation and in interviews explaining its necessity, after dwelling on the case of a labourer of 18 who was sent to jail for sharing a "thin pint of corn whisky" with a friend behind a hedge, Mrs. Ferguson thus describes a big party given by a multimillionaire oil magnate.

"Why, he had his garage fitted up like a regular bar, with brass rallings and sawdust on the floor. He gave a dinner at which 600 pints of liquor were drunk and to his guests he presented as souvenirs 800 imitation Bibles and 300 canoes each containing a pint of whiskey."

The governor proceeded to state that the same millionaire appeared at a football match on Thanksgiving Day, "drunk as a bolled owl and waving his came, presumably filled with liquor."

Mrs. Ferguson also publicly accuses a district judge of drinking boot-leg whisky a few moments after sentencing to imprisonment a batch of boot-leggers.
