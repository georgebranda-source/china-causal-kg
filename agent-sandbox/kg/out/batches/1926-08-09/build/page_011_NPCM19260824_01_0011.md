## [2] BRITAIN ACQUITTED OF BARTERING.

In view of the close interest manifested by the Russian community in Shanghai in the question of the rendition of the Mixed Court, a representative of the Russian newspaper, the “Shanghai Zaria,” sought an interview with Mr. Alexander N. Fishman, the well-known Shanghai barrister-at-law, who expressed some significant points of view on this all important issues which is how so much before the public eye.

At the outset of the interview, a reference to which was briefly cabled by "Reuter, Mr. Fishman emphasised that he did not claim to speak as the mouthpiece of the various Russian organisations existent in Shanghai, but expressed his readiness to be helpful to the newspaper in so far as his own individual opinions were concerned.

To the first question, "What will be the effect upon the status of Russians in Shanghai of the proposed rendition?" Mr. Fishman replied: "It may have a dual effect—legal and practical. Legally the Russians would acquire an entirely new and different status. In spite of the fact that the Russians have lost their extraterritorial rights, while they are living in Shanghai, they are under the supreme authority of the Mixed Court. With the rendition of the Mixed Court, it is expected that they will be under the jurisdiction of the purely Chinese Court in Peking and therefore generally will be within the competence of the Central Government. From a practical point of view, the position of Russians in Shanghai will, of course, be dependent upon the degree of perfection of the legal institutions which are to be established in place of the Mixed Court."

## [7] MR. WINSTON CHURCHILE - CAUSES CONSTERNATION.

London, August 8, — Members of Trade Unions within the Civil Service are in consternation at the announcement by Mr. Winston Churchill, in the House of Commons, "that the Government had decided that Civil Servants' Associations should not affiliate with outside industrial or political organisations.

This announcement was made in reply "to a question asked by Major E. Cadogan (Con.), who is a member on the official side of the National Whitley Council of the Civil Service.

The organisations indicated are the Trade Union Congress, the Labour Party, and the Civil Service Unions, whose membership amount to 126,200 from the staff side of the National Whitley Council.

## [9] A BURIED CHURCH.

**MERTON PRIORY CHURCH IN SURREY.**

Sic transit—if one may innoently use a phrase faintly controversial, perhaps, when employed in the present connection. Merton, Priory, in Surrey, England, fell into ruin at the time of the Restoration and became so completely lost that for centuries men have speculated as to its exact whereabouts. At length, however, the famous old structure has been discovered—or rather the lines of its foundations have been unerthed; and another (sure) goes to the somewhat heavily luden brow of archeology.

Sic transit—for in its day Merton Priory Church figured quite glitteringly in the worldly sphere of princes and sovereigns, of dynasties and statecrafts played, as a contemporary observe: in England recently phrased it, "no inconsiderable part in the national life. Here Archbishops were consecrated and King was renowned." That same monarch Henry III, in the thirteenth century, held in the church of Merton Friday his Grand Council of the Realm, "close predecessor of Parliament, that enacted the Statutes of Merton with which our statutes book opens." Thomas a Becket—afterward Archbishop of Canterbury and murdered—was educated at Merton, and there De Burgh took refuge when in "difficulties with the throne. Then the winds of fortune blew adversely. The Reformation brought about a whole pew orientation and Merton Priory sunk into the dust of the countryside. The last traces became obliterated. Time passed, many years. The little dreaming village grew large. A modern metropolitan area spread itself out about London, and commerce set its mark. But in the minds of the curious a memory, handed down from father to son, was not effaced. And the last three years have witnessed the successful termination of a long quest. Merton Priory has been found.

Even the Site Obliterated.
A bit of concrete foundation was exposed, quite by accident; and naturally this stimulated fresh archaeological activity.

It appears to have been a great church 300 feet long. The east end was square, a sturdy Norman face; and there was a thirteenth century extension with transepts and chapels. Small patches of stone paving have been laid bare, fragments of what was once a noble aisled nave. There used to be a cloister on the south side, and indications of convent buildings have yielded themselves to the shovels of research.

The diggers have by no means easy picking. Unfortunately this one-time haunt of Kings and Archbishops, of pageantry and politics does not lie buried beneath a convenient daisy-strewn meadow. The Southern Railway traverses part of the historic precinct. Indeed, we learn, in order to burrow down into the secrets of the past it was 'in one place necessary to

tracks—literally between trains, as well. The railway company—though not, conceivably, confessing any overpowering interest in antiquity or the genius of Norman builders—co-operated in this connection. Passengers looking out from the windows of a passing train no doubt mistook the learned delivers, lined up and resting on their spades, for repair men.

Vestiges of the southern half of the once majestic pile are, it seems, irrevocably entombed beneath roads, tracks and the modern station buildings. As regards the northern half, fate was more kindly disposed toward archaeologists, for this is privately owned land, purchased some years ago for a factory site. The projected factory scheme has not yet been fully worked out, and the structures thus far erected, designed as domicile for the Machine, lie east of what now proves to have been the main body of Merton Priory Church.

A movement has been started to buy up this land, snatching it, in the nick of time from the industrial octopus. It is proposed to turn the area into a public park, with the ground plan of the northern half of the church marked out as an enduring monument to brave days that are no more.

Funds for the purpose are scarce. The neighbourhood is a poor one, and those who would directly berefit by the creation there of a park cannot be called upon to donate very extensively. But archaeologists are by nature optimistic, despite that spirit of caution engendered by their science. Mertn is almost a part of London, and students, it is argued, would find the spot delightfully accessible. Regides, in great London, almost anything may, with a will, be accomplished.
