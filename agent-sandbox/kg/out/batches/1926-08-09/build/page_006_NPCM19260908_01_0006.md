## [11] Not Botany Bay.

<sub>section under [3] S. MOUTRIE & CO., LTD.</sub>

Prominence having been given to the fact that deportees from other colonies, are sent here, Hong Kongites may think they have just cause for being indignant. "This is a fine state of affairs," writes a correspondent. "Is Hong Kong a second Botany Bay?" he asks. No, this little outpost of empire is by no means a convict settlement nor a repository for undesirables. By reason of its geographical position—its proximity to the homes of deportees from neighbouring colonies—Hong Kong is a sort of distributing centre. That a never ceasing campaign is waged to weed out bad characters is evident from the number of persons—of both sex—deported when a conviction is not feasible. Then there are those banished after a term in jail. Further there are the mendicants who are packed off to beg in their own cities. Not so long ago, the usual term of deportation was five years. It is rarely under ten now. So much for what is being done to rid the Colony of pests in our own midst. People with no fixed means of abode or other underworld "characters" of Singapore and other places are rounded up and repatriated. The Hong Kong authorities, being in closer touch, act as receiving agents and then send the deportees on to their respective destinations. Of the Chinese who come back under guard from the South, it should be stated that the majority are not Cantonese, in the strict sense of the term. Some are Hokloq from the Swatow district. Others may hail from Hainan Island. Then there are natives of Fukien province. On arrival in Hongkong, the deportees are marched up to Police Headquarters where fingerprints are taken. A special man who is trained for the job looks after the emigrants and supervises their departures from our shores.

## [12] CORRESPONDENCE.

**THE Y.M.C.A. MEDICAL CAMPAIGN.**

**PRAISEWORTHY EFFORT**

[To the Editor of the "China Mill.] Sir, — The efforts of the Chinese Y.M.C.A. in attempting to inculente into the Chinese populace an elementary grounding in hygiene is indeed praiseworthy and if there were similar organisations capable of carrying on a campaign throughout the country a great reduction in the mortality figures, could be effected, as a large percentage of the deaths in China are avoidable.

In China, unhappy, effective sanitation is conspicuous by its absence and the conservation of infant life is something which has not yet been fully grasped by the Chinese. The result is that the rate of infant mortality in this country is abnormally high and much potential man-power is thus lost to the nation.

A case in point has been furnished in Shanghai where thousands have been attacked by the dread malady of cholera and a large percentage have succumbed to the disease.

The number of foreign cases of cholera, even in periods of the worst epidemic, as the “China Press” points out, is negligible. What is the reason for such a disparity between the Chinese and foreign cases? Why is it that foreigners, many of whom are alien to the climate of China, asks the same paper, contrive to survive the rigours of a severe summer whilst those indigenous to the soil and acclimatised to lqcal atmospheric conditions pay such a heavy toll whenever cholera rages in a particular area?

Is it not because the masses 'of the people refuse to adopt those elementary measures of' precaution, both as regards "food and drink, which are enjoined" on the foreigner and Chinese alike", but which only a slender proportion of the latter take seriously?

It is disheartening, to observe the pathetic faith which the majority of Chinese repose in quack doctors of the "kill or cure" order. As a rule he kills, in which case there is nothing to worry about, but if in other instances he contrives to cure his victim, it is not because of the proficiency shown, as the "China Press" points out, but in spite of him.

The work of the Chinese Y.M.C.A. in endeavouring to replace the faith of the masses in charlatanry by a grounding in the elementary laws of hygiene is a commendable one which will meet with the approval of all who have the best interests of the Chinese at heart.

*Hong Kong, September 7*

## [13] HEATING FOR SNAKI

**LUXURIOUS CAGES AT THE ZOO.**

After prolonged research work, a wonderful system of heating, lighting, and stimulating has been arranged for the snakes and alligators in the London Zoo's new Republic House which will be ready next year.

Dark heat-rays and tonic light-rays are part of the scheme, and when a snake dies, in future, the Zoo will accept no excuse save that of senile decay, or, possibly, cunibalism by a third party.

The cages will be glimpses of nature with beautifully "modelled backgrounds designed by Miss Joan Procter, who created the water-pictures in the Zoo Aquarium. The alligators will sun themselves on shelving "Florida benches" fringed with water-plants, and tropical vegetation. You will see no flower-pots in the "snakes" and lizards' enclosures to shatter your illusions.

All these cages will show themselves as "brilliantly-lit" spectacles to visitors who themselves walk in a pleasant twilight.

Each reptile will be given exactly the right amount of electric heat. If 82 degrees is prescribed, a thermostat (heat regulator) will be set to that pbint, and the warmth will be shut off automatically if it is exceeded. The sandy floor will be heated, the water warmed, and the air-temperature controlled in the same way. The failure of a thermostat will report itself electrically in a control-room.

Zoo visitors will find the atmosphere in the new Reptile House no warmer than that outside the building, and the old reproach of stuffness will vanish.

## [16] BOULDER SMASH..

**WAS REPORT MADE 2 YEARS AGO?**

**UNIVERSITY COMPLAINT?**

P.W.D. witnesses were questioned at the adjourned inquiry yesterday into the Pokfulam pumping house disaster as to whether their attention had been drawn to the danger from insecure boulders at that spot two years ago.

None of the witnesses in question had any recollection of any such report, they stated: if such had been made it would have been referred to the P.W.D. in the first instance and then probably direct to the Building Authority. The overseer in charge of the district would ultimately have been detailed off.

Mr. R. S. W. Paterson, engineer in the Building Ordinance Office, P.W.D., was questioned as to whether in the ordinary way such a report would have come to him. If it had been made two years ago, He stated that it probably would and in any case he would have been informed. If it had not come to him for action he would have handled it for filling purposes and either he or the overseer would have been deputed to the scene. He had no knowledge of any such report.

In reply to other questions witness said that he visited the scene on the morning of the disaster and climbed to the place where the rock hall come from. The ground was very sodden at that point but there was no evidence of a washout by water. Witness thought that many years ago the two boulders, one of which was still on the ledge from which the other had fallen, had formed one.

Mr. R. E. Lindsell, Coroner, said he had been informed that morning that Professor Earle, of the University of Hongkong, who was at present in Shanghai, had made a report of insecure boulders at the spot in question. He suggested that witness should communicate with Mr. Pearce, engineer of the P.W.D., telling him what he (the Coroner) had heard and ask him to come to the Court and make a statement.

## [17] GAS FATALITY

**JURY RECOMMEND USE OF MASKS.**

**ACCIDENTAL DEATH.**

A recommendation that gas masks should be used was made at the further inquiry into the death of a coolie from the effects of gas poisoning while working at the Pedder Street nullah.

The inquiry was conducted by Major Willson at the Central Magistracy yesterday, assisted by a jury.

Mr. D. S. Edward, assistant engineer, P.W.D. deposed to having issued a warning to the contractors prior to the accident to use precautions when the leakage of gas was discovered. He handed in a copy of the regulations concerning work of this kind, and said that "contractors were notified of the regulations.

The foreman of the jury said he understood that gas masks were used in such cases at Home. Witness said he had put forward the idea and some reflection had been cast on it, the question being raised of whether the coolies would understand the masks.
In reply to another question by the foreman, witness said the use of masks ensured safety but he thought some of the Chinese were not sufficiently intelligent to wear them. Major Willson commented that all recruits had to learn how to use masks.

Witness added that gas masks were obtained from the military authorities and brought into use when the work was held up.
Commenting on the evidence, Major Willson said the man was discharged from hospital as cured, but there was no evidence to show what he did or ate after his discharge.

The jury returned a verdict of death from coal gas poisoning adding that no blame or negligence was attached to anyone. They added a rider that in such cases gas masks should be used, the wearing of them to be supervised by a competent Chinese foreman.
