## [9] A SATURDAY CAUSERIE.

As succeeding military leaders have obtained supremacy in the North the new regime has been hailed as the forerunner of a competent and effective federal Government which will solve the questions outstanding between China and the Powers. Not for a long time had the prospects been brighter than when the Chang-Wu Pei-fu alliance outlasted the ousting of General Feng's Red Government from Peking and the defeat of the Red or Kuominchun forces. The South was keenly interested in the outcome of the struggle which it was generally understood would have a bearing on the local boycott question in that Canton would await developments before resuming the negotiations with Hongkong begun at the Boycott Conference. The assumption was that once Wu Pei-fu was in a position to turn his attention to the South there would be danger of the minor victories won by the allies of the South being checked and an extensive campaign entered into against the ascendancy of the Nationalist Government. Wu Pei-fu has, in fact, launched an offensive against the Nationalist forces on the Hankow front and reports indicate that he has been none too successful. Now comes news from a reliable source that he has been deposed by subordinates and held a prisoner. The Southern forces claim victories and whilst their claims as to the fall of Hankow and Wuchang seem without official confirmation it appears that the arrival of Wu Pei-fu's forces from the North, so far from establishing an immediate supremacy over the Southerners, has still further complicated matters. What effect it will have on the Southern situation it is difficult at the moment to state. It is doubtful if the treachery against his ally on the Hankow front had the acquiescence or even knowledge of Marshal Chang-but it is hardly likely (with his position in the North still far from being safely assured) that he is in a position to forsake the North to render active assistance.

## [13] War Effects on Hongkong

<sub>section under [3] S. MOUTRIE & CO., LTD.</sub>

Will General Chang Kai-shek now rest on his laurels or push northwards to Peking? That is a question which many who are "following" the trend of events in China's civil war are now asking themselves. Canton's soldier of the hour has declared that he will establish a joint government in

fact and in name, for the whole country. Politics in China being so liable to take the most unexpected turn, it is rash to predict what the future holds in store. Chang Kai-shek may tackle Chang Tso-lin or even Sun Chuan-fang of armed neutrality fame; or he may enter into an alliance with one or the other. Time was not so long ago when the Fengtian party and the Kuomin-tang were in alliance against Wu Pei-fu who was then in Peking. From the British point of view there will be much speculation as to whether the hostile attitude will spread to the territory newly conquered by the Nationalists. It is not denied that propagandists are accompanying the armies in the field to spread the new doctrine. The presumption that follows is that anti-British agitators will be at work. How far it will take root and what response the people will make remains to be seen. Hankow's trade is well known enough to Hongkong. There are Chinese merchants who do a fairly extensive business with that part of China. Sugar and medicinal herbs are two commodities which come to mind to speak of no others. Intermediate ports are situated between Hongkong and Hankow. If an embargo is placed against Hongkong we venture to say that it will not be so effective as, say, at Swatow or Kongmoon. On the other hand, British shipping may have to cope with pickets if the movement takes any root. The fact that a number of unruly students were executed only recently at Hankow by Wu Pei-fu may be taken to indicate that there exists fertile ground on which to sow the seed of feeling against Britain.

## [14] Wu Pei-fu's Eclipse.

<sub>section under [3] S. MOUTRIE & CO., LTD.</sub>

As far as can be made out Wu Pei-fu's present defeat is due not so much to military inferiority as to discounting his opponents' strength and to his not having his own troops. A correspondent of the "China Mail" suggests that the chief advantage on the side of the Nationalists lay in the rank and file having been trained to fight "for an ideal." With the Northerners—as was the case with Chan Kwing-ming—the troops fought for their living and the generals placed personal gain before the "purpose" that they laboured for. One vernacular telegram early this week mentioned a Northern general visiting the front and presenting each private and non-commissioned officer with a dollar, while the officers received a sword. Small though the gifts may seem to a western mind, such incidents remain in the mind of the Chinese man in the street. Intermittent reports spoke of heavy fighting on different parts of the front; but the impression gained was that the Southerners pressed forward by strategic moves rather than by combat pure and simple. Perhaps, as our correspondent suggests, the prestige of the Whampoa Cadets, who form the nucleus of General Chang's army, had a disquieting effect on the Northerners. The turning of Wu Pei-fu's subordinates in his hour of trial does not seem strange to the student of things Chinese. Rather than face capture or being disarmed, the minor generals often precipitate a crisis by going over to the other side. This is the second time that Wu Pei-fu has been defeated. The first was when the Christian general (Feng Yu-hsiang) revolted in Peking. Since then Wu Pei-fu has had to rely on former assistants, on whom he has had to be more dependent. The day seems passing in China when a leader with a name but not soldiers can lay down the law on the strength of loyalty from partisans.

## [16] SCIENCE'S LATEST.

**BRUNSWICK PANATROPE DEMONSTRATION.**

A demonstration of the capabilities of the Brunswick Panatrope — the latest arrival in the world of electrically controlled gramophone instruments— was given at the Club Lusitano yesterday afternoon.

The announcement attracted sufficient attention to draw a large number of musical enthusiasts who wore favourably impressed with the new instrument. Among those present were representatives from Messrs. Anderson Music Co., S. Moutrie & Co., the Rev. Father Riganti, Messrs. H. A., G. P. and L. E. Lammert, Mr. Felix M. Ellis, members of the Club Lusitanq and several ladies.

## [17] What It Does.

<sub>section under [16] SCIENCE'S LATEST.</sub>

Prior to the demonstration, Mr. P. A. Rosario explained that the Panatrope was the result of a few years of scientific research in the laboratories of the Radio Corporation of America, the General Electric Company, the Westinghouse Electric and Manufacturing Company, and the Brunswick Phonograph Company. He said it was different from other conventional makes of gramophone in that it had no sound box or horn. In place of the sound box there was a device called the magnetic pickup. The scratches on the record were conveyed by wires to a power amplifier and by electrical energy converted into sound and the sound amplified in different stages by means of radio valves.

## [18] Easy Process.

<sub>section under [16] SCIENCE'S LATEST.</sub>

It was also explained that although the inside of the instrument showed a number of wires running in different directions, they were set in such a way that there was nothing to get out of order. All that the operator had to do was to switch on the current.
A comparison was made with a record on an ordinary cabinet machine and the same on the Fanatrope. A feature of the new machine is that it has five graduations of sound, the highest being equal to that of an instrumental band, which would be useful at dancing parties. Soft tones are reproduced when the lever is set at number one; and from then on the sound increases although the clarity is retained.

## [20] CORRESPONDENCE

**HONGKONG AN APATHETIC COMMUNITY?**

**NO COMMUNAL CONCERTS.**

[To the Editor of the "China Mail."]
Sir;—I would like to endorse the remarks of "Music Lover" with regard to the apathy characterising the Hongkong community in matters pertaining to the communal welfare.

I have noticed that invariably, if the possibility of arranging weekly or fortnightly concerts in the City Hall—or elsewhere—is mooted in a company of even professedly music enthusiasts, the same old theory is advanced that "we haven't sufficient talent in a small community like that of Hongkong."

Yet in every instance where a local concert has been properly organised—and by that I mean that the organisers have taken the trouble to approach the right people—the result of their efforts has been a concert which it has been a real pleasure to attend, superior in many respects to ones which have been charged for by touring companies at far higher rates than the admission to local concerts.

Such a concert was that at the Naval Theatre on Thursday night organised by the R.A.O.B. And another such was that at Taikoo a short time ago. These concerts were organised by people who were keenly interested in the cause and sufficiently enthusiastic to take pains in its organisation.

If only such enthusiasm was more general there would be no difficulties in the way of regular concerts which would prove a real boon in the summer time when, failing such attractions, residents have to be content to remain indoors or waste their time and money in other directions.

## [21] FATAL QUARREL. ALL OVER A HAWKER'S BAGS.

**MANSLAUGHTER CHARGE.**

### -

How a quarrel between a scavenging coolie and a hawker as to two bags which had been placed in the street to dry resulted in "a fight and how later the hawker was found on a doorstep suffering from internal injuries which later led to his death was described at the Kowloon Magistracy yesterday when the scavenging coolie in question appeared on a charge of manslaughter.

Inspector Pincott prosecuted, while the defendant was unrepresented.

It was stated for the prosecution that defendant in the course of the fight used his broom and prodded deceased in the stomach. The defendant himself appeared at the time to have got the worst of it as he showed signs of having been struck below both eyes. It was further stated that the deceased accompanied friends of his from the scene and that it was not until they turned round a corner that they found the deceased missing. Turning back, they found him on a doorstep. He was still alive then but by the time his relatives were called he had died.

Dr. Newton gave evidence of having performed the post mortem examination. He said that the deceased had a scratch near the spleen just below the ribs. The scratch was very slight and might have been caused by a prod with a stick or even by the deceased's own finger nails. The spleen was ruptured, with a considerable amount of haemorrhage, which was the cause of death. After further evidence the case was adjourned.
