Mr. Eugene Chen proceeds to connect the "arrangements to end the boycott" with the intimidation of the intention to impose certain tariffs, for the authority for which he seeks to bind the "proper Chinese authorities"—presumably with their headquarters also in Canton. These special taxes follow in the main those laid down in the Washington Convention, to which nine Powers are signatory, and under the provisions of which the Tariff Conference has already held several sessions in Peking and other centres. A new tax, however, not mentioned in the Washington Convention, is the "special production or producers' tax on exports."

It is so obvious that proposals of this kind cannot be made with the Government having its centre in Canton, which for the purposes of the Treaty is studiously incorporated with “China,” the recognised capital of which is Peking, that they can be dismissed from further thought right away. Mr. Eugene Chen must be aware that no single Power can possibly acquiesce in the imposition of such tariffs without the unanimous concurrence of the other eight signatories to the Washington Convention. Great Britain alone cannot do so, even if the war situation was so to develop as eventually to warrant Mr. Eugene Chen speaking on behalf of the “Nationalist Government of the Republic of China.” Therefore, the only possible course of procedure for the Consul-General in Canton is to refer the letter to Peking for its consideration. And of course, Peking could only reply that the boycott engineered and carried out in Canton is no concern of it and that, moreover, Canton cannot be recognised as the official seat of the Nationalist Government of the Republic of China.

It appears that Mr. Eugene Chen, if he is still anxious to end the boycott on or about the date he mentions, will either have to consult the Strike Committee again or let the arrangements stand without any reference to Customs tariffs or the Nationalist Government of the Republic of China.

## [14] MOONCAKE FEAST.

**TO-MORROW'S BIG CHINESE FESTIVAL.**

**AN EMPEROR'S CONCUBINE.**

Mooncake Feast, or Mid-Autumn Festival, or Chung-chin chief, to give its classical rendering, falls this year on Tuesday (to-morrow), the 15th day of the 8th moon.

Tradition places its origin about 2,150 B.C. when lived, the famous archer-Emperor "Hao I" (Hao Ngai in Cantonese).

He shot nine of the ten suns illuminating the heavens when the sun-god besought him not to make a bull's eye of the tenth. For this boon he was given a potion which would make him immortal with a home in the sun.

Before he could take the potion which had to be prepared in an urn at night with devout prayers meanwhile, a naughty concubine by name of Sheung. Ngo drained the last drop while her spouse slept through the watch-night service.

Light as a fairy, the woman fled to the moon to escape the archer's wrath.

Every year she returns to mother earth and moon-cakes are eaten in her honour.

Thrown Out of Moon!

This episode has been commercialised into a favourite stage-play being one of the star-numbers of Mei Lan-fang's repertoire. Mei will be remembered as the famous Northern actor who was down here in 1922.

The legend has been slightly lengthened and ends up with Hao I being thrown out of the moon after a hot pursuit of his concubine.

It is a harvest festival in that the tea-houses reap their annual harvest. Moon-shaped cakes with wonderful ingredients are made months ahead of the festival and shipped to all parts of the world where Chinese are to be found.

Chinese delight in these delicacies. Another aspect is the making and sale of beautiful lanterns with which Chinatown will be decorated to-night. Actually the men-folk take little note of the religious side of the festival except to organise a feast at West Point.

## [15] DOUBLE SUICIDE.

**AMAHS END THEIR LIVES TOGETHER.**

**MOTIVE UNKNOWN.**

Two Chinese amahs living at Canton Road, Kowloon, entered into a compact to end their lives together and succeeded yesterday.

When the police were called to the second floor of the house they found both women dead in each other's embrace on a bed. One was found to have died from the effects of opium poisoning and the other from gashes on the throat inflicted with a razor.

## [17] CORRESPONDENCE

**WHY USE "MADAME" FOR "MRS."?**

**LOCAL APPELLATIONS**

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"]
Sir,—Can anyone explain why Hong Kong journalists apparently make a point of designating Mrs. Sun Yat-sen, Mrs. Chang-Kai-shek and the wives of certain other notorious Chinese militants. "Madame"? It certainly is a curious custom and it is difficult to find an explanation for same.

Equally curious is the practice adopted by the authorities of Hong Kong and the majority of the Treaty Ports who appear to make a point of naming thorough-fares after the cities of Peking, Canton and other cess-pools of iniquity.

One would imagine that the average pedestrian would welcome a change of names. To be constantly reminded that such places exist is not exactly exhiliating.

*Hong Kong, Sept. 20, 1926.*

## [23] WAR TERRORS.

**MINES TO BLOW OUT WUCHANG?**

**BESIEGED FOREIGNERS.**

It is reported that plans are afoot to mine the Wuchang wall. A tunnel is already underway. To-day the food situation in Wuchang is most critical. Twenty-six foreigners are confined in the city whereof. 21 are Americans, three British and two Germans. These foreigners have previously been allowed to send out letters to Hankow friends but after to-day all mail communication will be stopped by order of the local authorities. The Wuhan Chambers of Commerce have intimated to the Southerners that it is impossible for them to raise further loans as their coffers are already drained. Anxiety is now felt in the Chamber circles as to how the refusal will be met.—Reuter.
