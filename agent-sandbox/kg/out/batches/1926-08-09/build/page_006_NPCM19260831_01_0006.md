## [6] Hongkong, Tuesday, August 31, 1926.

Very soon the "London Times" will be retrieving the reputation for consistency that was associated with it for decades before the blight of the Northcliffe influence began to make itself felt. For the second time in succession it has advocated the policy of force, or concerted diplomatic intervention, in China, after previously posing as a warm champion of "peace and conciliation."
In its latest article on the subject, as cabled by Reuter, the "Times" dwells on the Bolshevik activities in China, and expresses the opinion—as if it was something absolutely original and never dreamed of by anybody else!—that the Bolsheviks wish to keep China weak, divided, and unstable by setting province against province and General against General. That, of course, has long been known out here, so that it is nothing in the way of a discovery that the "Times" has made now, although its reiteration cannot possibly do any harm.
Anything that can be said and done counter to the "Bolshevik propaganda is all for the good. It is apparent from recent happenings at Home that the full extent of the present and possible Bolsheviks mischief in the Orient is not dully appreciated or understood throughout Great Britain. If, as the "Times" says, China has hitherto lost the opportunity of forming a stable State, it can be predicted with equal truth that if Great Britain does not really awake to the dangers of the Bolshevik menace at Home and overseas, she may lose the opportunity of curbing their wings and limiting their activities whilst it is comparatively easy to do so. Therefore anything that can be published in the "Times" or any other Home paper to expose that danger is to be welcomed.

## [10] Embargo on Money.

As from to-morrow people may take out of the Colony any money they please without fear of a prosecution following. Such a construction can be placed on the “Government Gazette” announcement of last week. On June 22, last year, an embargo was placed—under the Emergency Regulations—on the export of Hongkong money. Since then residents going elsewhere have been only permitted to take with them, a certain sum—believed to be not more than $5 in coin, and the same sum in local banknotes. Europeans were not much affected because they carry little ready money, preferring to deal in cheques and other negotiable documents. Not so the Chinese masses. For them silver and gold were always preferable and notes of repute ran a good second. Police officers on wharf duty always had to explain regulations to people who did not always appreciate their kindness in doing so. In extreme cases, prosecution was

necessary. One way of getting over things was—if one was going to Macao for example—to change the money into Macao banknotes and silver. The same applied "to other destinations." Accusations have been made against the alleged harshness of this regulation. When the "strike" first became apparent last June it was necessary to prevent a "run" on local banks, especially the native banks. If bullion, specie and coin could not be carted away, what was the use of withdrawing it from the bank just because other people were in a panic? The order certainly had the desired effect. Conditions have now changed to an extent when the ordinary flow of money is unlikely to harm Hongkong—hence the notification that the regulation "shall cease to have force and effect." There is one point that we wish to draw attention to. We believe there is still some restriction regarding silver (Mexican, as they are sometimes called) Hongkong dollars, but we are open to correction. This has been in force for years.

## [11] CHINESE STUDENTS.

**INTERESTING ANNUAL CONFERENCE.**

The annual conference of Chinese students in Great Britain and Ireland which was held in mail week at Kingsmead, Selby Oak, Birmingham, attracted some attention. Although only between sixty and seventy in number, the conference had a big programme, the general theme of which, as stated, was "The Students' Share in National and International Reconstruction," with the motto taken from the sayings of Confucius that "Within four senses we are brethren."

In his speech at the preliminary welcome meeting on July 24, Dr. W. C. Chen, the Chinese Charged Affairs, after recalling his own student days in America, exhorted his young compatriots, who enjoy the privileges of Western education, to keep before them their responsibilities with regard to China's future. That the small body of students in Britain are fully alive to their responsibilities in this respect is evidenced by the nature of the discussions. Great interest centred in the debate, "That Socialism is Beneficial" to China. Among the addresses was one, "Our Aim in Life," by Rev. T. S. Tso, M.A., while Mrs. C. C. Chen, Vice-President of the National Committee of the Y.M.C.A. of China, spoke of "China's Challenge to Her Students." Among the foreign speakers were Dr. Lionel Giles, M.A., D.Litt., the Deputy Keeper of Oriental Printed Books at the British Museum, who gave an address on the "First Fruits of Tunhuang," illustrated with lantern slides; Mr. G. E. Lerrigo, Secretary of the National Committee of the Y.M.C.A. of China, who discussed "The Responsibility of Chinese Students in Foreign Lands," and Sir, Denison Ross, C.I.E., PhD., Director of the School of Oriental Studies, who spoke of "China and the West."

On July 29 and 30 the chief speakers were Mr. Nelson Britton, Home Secretary of the London Missionary Society, whose subject was "A New Beginning;" Dr. L. C. Martin, D.Sc., of the Imperial College of Science, London University; and Dr. C. Stubbs, who gave an address on the subject of "The Challenge of Christian Education in China."

## [13] CORRESPONDENCE.

**HONGKONG'S "PART" IN THE DELUGE.**

**ARE WE WELL KNOWN?**

### 1

[To the Editor of the, "China Mall"] Sir.—Until residents of Hongkong take more interest in making their views known I am afraid we shall be poorly served in the matter of publicity. During my term in Shanghai I found many making use of the columns to vent a grievance, real or imaginary. In this Isle of Fragrant Streams, I suppose, we all tight and...

Have you noticed what was sent Home about us when the flood visited Hongkong last month? Anticipating that scare news would get into the Home papers I cabled my people assuring them that although the disaster was a disaster I was safe. My people send me a cutting from a London daily. To say the least, it was very undesirable. Mention is made of St. Joseph's College, the University (without capitals), and Kowloon. Nothing in a general way is given about the extent of the damage nor are strangers told what the places mentioned stand far. "A Chinese resident was killed by lightning at Kowloon." Does this convey anything to people who have not been here?

Allow me to take this opportunity of thanking you for republishing the complete record of the flood. Without it friends at Home may think I have had a bit of the sun out in the tropics, verb sup.

*Hongkong, Aug. 31.*

## [14] A RADIO SET.

**SUMMARY COURT CASE.**

A claim in respect of a wireless set sold to a Chinese customer, By Mr. Bird, the Manager of the S. China Development Syndicate, was heard in the Summary Court this morning.

The claim was "for $266 still outstanding on the price charged, according to the claim.
Mr. W. B. Hind represented the plaintiffs and Mr. J. T. Prior, the defendants.

It was claimed on behalf of the defendant that a guarantee had been given that the cost of the set would be probably $200 or a little more and would not in any event exceed $300. The set was stated by plaintiffs to be complete but it was claimed that further parts had had to be bought and that considerable cost had to be incurred in endeavouring to get the set working. It was also claimed by defendant that a guarantee had been given by Mr. Bird to have the set installed "in working order and that he (defendant) should be taught. A guarantee had also been given with regard to a Government permit and assurance that the set would get Manila and certain concerts broadcasted at the time by the Hongkong Hotel.

The date of the sale was in dispute, plaintiffs stating that it was in May, 1924 and defendants that it was in June, 1923.

On this point, Mr. Prior had indicated that he would plead the Statute of Limitations in favour of the defendant but on Mr. Bird being examined and, referring to his books, stating that the sale took place in May, 1923, His Honour (Mr. Justice Wood), stated that he was satisfied that plaintiffs had made out their case sufficiently as regarded the date and Mr. Prior withdrew his pleading.

Mr. Bird also denied in the box that any guarantee had been given as to installation, permission from the Government, or as to the Hongkong Hotel broadcast concerts which had ceased shortly after the transaction. Defendant was the brother of the Company's treasurer and brother-in-law of the Company's compradore. Defendant had for a period attended often at the office but had not been officially connected with it and drew no salary. There was no contract in writing about the sale. Defendant had simply asked witness to get him a receiving set. The plaintiff knew as much about the radio business as he (plaintiff) did.
After hearing the evidence His Honour found that the plaintiff had succeeded in his claim and that it was no part of the plaintiff's contract to install the set, to obtain the permission of the Government, etc. Judgment was therefore given for $260.

## [16] SCIENCE'S LATEST.

**GRAMOPHÔNE PLUS WIRELESS MACHINE**

**BRUNSWICK INNOVATION.**

There is at present in the Colony an example of the latest that scientific invention has produced in machines for recording gramophone records.

With electrical apparatus, valves and an amplifier, the machine might almost be mistaken for the latest in wireless receiving sets. It can, as a matter of fact, be transformed into a receiving set for wireless quite easily but its greatest achievement is in the recording of vocal and instrumental music from ordinary records, which it does exceptionally well.

The most "welcome" innovation on the machine—which is known as the Brunswick Panatrope (local agents, the Brunswick Studio)—is the contrivance by which, without stopping the machine or performing any operation other than turning the handle to the number indicated, one can regulate the volume of the sound according to the size or acoustics of the room.

Whilst the machine is on the "loud speaker," the volume of the sound is too much for all but a hall or room of considerable dimensions but by the use of the regulator, it can be made suitable for room of the smallest dimensions.

"At a public demonstration to be organised at a later date, opportunity will be given for comparison between the new and the old machines. Yesterday at a private demonstration, a "China Mail" representative was given that opportunity and it can be said without hesitation that the new machine possesses advantages which bring within realisation solution of the problem of recording on the gramophone without loss of certain qualities present at the original hearing."

In orchestral selections it is not merely the “effect” which it is possible to get on the Panatrope but each instrument can be clearly heard and followed—to the exclusion of the others if so desired. The pipe organ record was reproduced in a remarkably faithful manner and vocal items were, none the less effective.

## [18] "Convincing Demonstration"

<sub>section under [16] SCIENCE'S LATEST.</sub>

For full justice to be done to the particular model which the Brunswick Studio have at present it should be heard in a large hall (this test it is hoped to be able to put it to on the occasion of the public demonstration). The private hearing accorded yesterday was in a more restricted sphere but the demonstration was undoubtedly convincing — particularly when the loudest of the older type of gramophones was tried in comparison.

The model in question was of five valves. The Brunswick hope to get others of greater and less volume according to the power required by purchasers. The operation of the machine, the motive power of which is electrical, is perfectly simple, the only complication of the model on which the display was given being that it was fitted for a 110 voltage instead of 200 and a transformer was brought into operation. For Kowloon, a 110 voltage machine would need no transformation.

For the benefit of the combined radio and gramophone enthusiast it might be said that the change of the machine from gramophone recording to wireless reception is also a perfectly simple operation.

## [19] FUNERAL ROUTE

**ARRANGEMENTS FOR THIS AFTERNOON.**

Arrangements have been made by the police to cope with an abnormally heavy rush of traffic during the funeral of Mr. Ho Fook this afternoon.

Congestion will be heavy in the business area where the procession will pass through various narrow streets.

Leaving 15 Kennedy Road shortly after 2 o'clock, the funeral will proceed through Garden Road, Queen's Road, Ice House Street, Des Vaux Road, Pottinger Street into Queen's Road again, and back to Des Vaux Road through Queen's Street. The procession will then make a direct route to Yat Pit Ting, Kennedy Town, arriving there about 4:30 p.m.

Squads of district watchmen and Chinese constables will precede the coffin, with European officers of the Force walking beside it. The Military Band and the Confucius Band will be in attendance.
