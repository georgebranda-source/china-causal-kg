## [15] Our Preventive Service.

<sub>section under [2] VICTOR RECORDS</sub>

Whenever there is a big optimum case pending in the local courts, the imagination at once turns to those "good old days" when smuggling was very rife, to the era when a chest of opium was bartered in Whampoa harbour for a case of tea, and to the mythical fortunes made by names associated with the past. Many people wonder how the authorities come to know about transactions in places so far from Hong Kong. It being their duty, we presume that the officers of the Import and Export Department are in constant communication with distant ports. By knowing what is going on in other parts of the world, it is possible to form an opinion as to changes in the traffic. There must be a great deal of work behind the scenes for which those concerned do not get all the credit. Big seizures of drugs and arms are not made every day. It is only the spectacular and unusual which appeals to the mind of the man in the street. People are hardly likely to hear about the many heartbreaking jobs undertaken not so much to lay hands on any particular shipment, but to prevent the stuff being smuggled into this Colony. Painstaking and watchful, the Revenue Officers like other public servants in similar positions — are always courteous. Tact is combined with enterprise so as not to give offence. Bearing in mind that Hong Kong is so adjacent to territory which lends itself to irregularities, much praise should go to the preventive force for the measure of success attained hitherto under difficulty and against ingenuity.

## [16] CHURCH IN CHINA.

**CONDITIONS REVIEWED AT SYNOD.**

**BISHOP'S ADDRESS.**

The difficult times through which the Churches had been passing since the last meeting of the Diocesan Synod in April 1925, were the subject of reference at the fifth meeting of the Victoria Diocesan Synod of the Chung Hwa Sheng Kung Hui held at St. John's Cathedral Hall yesterday, the Right Rev. Bishop of Victoria being in the chair.

The Right Rev. Bishop Duppuy said that the months which had elapsed since their last meeting had been a testing time for their Church. It had often been proved that difficulties were a prelude to progress and he thought there was much to encourage in the work of the Church during this period.

The Bishop referred to the faith in the future of the Church in China as expressed by Dr. John Mott at the "Conference on the Church in China" held in Shanghai at the beginning of the year. This faith was all the more to be valued as it was expressed by one who spent nearly forty years of his life travelling from country to country, holding Conferences with Christian leaders in different parts of the world, and who had paid six visits to China, each visit after an interval of five or six years. Reviewing the progress that had been made by the Christian Church in China since his first visit thirty years ago, Dr. Mott had said he was sure that we should face the future with hope, that if we steadily press on, in spite of our difficulties, a new area for the Christian movement in China will dawn; but very difficulties may be our salvation.

We are forced to consider many problems at the present time, the greatest question of all is "Has Christianity got a saving message for the world as it is today?" "My answer is that if you take any large view of human life, it is plain there is insistent need for the Christian ethic" and that unless our national and international problems are faced in the spirit of Christ, nation will continue to war against nation and class against class, until it may well be our modern civilization itself will perish. The only alternative to this incessant warfare is a recognition of the Father, Who has a place for each and all in His great family.

What I want to stress at the very outset is that the success of Christianity can only be a spiritual success. To get our organisation right is very necessary but it is not the all-important thing. I was talking this week with Bishop Roots, of Hankow (Chairman of our House of Bishops) and I asked him what he thought most needed stressing at such a gathering as this. He said the need for individual regeneration and for friendship. You may remember that at that Shanghai Conference to which I have referred some speaker said that when people wanted spiritual help they went to Buddhism and not to Christianity. The question we are forced to ask ourselves then is this: "Is the Church supplying men's spiritual needs? There is always a real danger lest we turn aside to side shows and lose sight of the main thing for which we exist. What we need everywhere are contagious personalities, people whose lives are really devoted to Jesus Christ and dominated by His Spirit.

### Church Pensions.

There is yet a further point which I think we ought to consider, namely the question of Pensions for Workers. I know that it may well be urged that to-day is not the right time to bring this matter forward; people are finding it more than they can do to meet their present liabilities quite apart from laying by for the future, and yet I believe that as a Church we should be wrong if we shelve the subject. My own idea would be that we should try to provide some scheme which should be set on foot say, in three years time. Our Church in England has only just inaugurated a general Clergy Pension Scheme. I would like to feel that from the first the C.H.S.K.H. in this diocese had some such scheme in existence.

This leads me to add that I would like to see during the next twelve months the Standing Committee of our Synod make suggestions as to the drawing up of a book of regulations for the Diocese, which together with the Constitutions and Canons of the General Synod, might be published in booklet form so that all might have available the rules and procedure which govern the work of our Church. I would also like to see laid on the table at each meeting of the Synod, a Property Register so that all might know exactly how we stand as a Church in the matter of property.

In conclusion, the Bishop referred to the meeting of the "General Synod to be held in Foochow next May, at which matters of considerable importance for the future of the Church would be discussed"; also to the delegation sent by the General Synod to visit Chinese congregations abroad, an account of the work of which would be given later by the Rev. S. Y. K. Lee who had accompanied Bishop Roots, of Hankow.

## [20] CHEAP RENTS.

**HONG KONG AND OTHER COLONIES**

**PERTINENT QUERY**

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"] Sir,—Why should settlers in Capetown be treated better than us in Hong Kong?

Please draw the attention of persecuted tenants and housekeepers to the statement of an All authority:—

Residents of the two principal towns in South Africa—Capetown and Joliannesburg—are at last experiencing a fall in house rents. In both cases the welcome change is due to the fact that recently a large number of dwellings have been erected. Flats are coming very much into favour, particularly with young married people.

It is estimated that Capetown rents are 10 per cent. below what they were a year ago, the reduction applying more particularly to houses favoured by the lower middle classes. There are still plenty of tenants for three or four-roomed cottages letting at from £5 15s. to £6 10s. a month.

The building of flats in Cape-town and suburbs has been carried on on such an extensive scale that the supply is in excess of the demand.

Yours, etc.

## [23] "AN ILL WIND..."

**MEETING STORM WITH ITS LEGACY:**

**HAPPY VALLEY DEBRIS.**

"It's an ill-wind that blows nobody any good," and although it would be stretching the point to regard the debris washed down at Happy Valley by recent storms as anything in the nature of a blessing, it is interesting to note that it is being put to good use—to defeat the storm through the agency of its own legacy, in fact.
The earth washed down is being used in filling in the available portions of Wongneichung village which has suffered so considerably in the past from inundation due to the storm. Certain of the villagers who have vacated their houses have been given house rent or quarters elsewhere.

Other more conservative of the residents who are attached to the district by sentimental ties extending over generations of family's tenancy are holding on to their tenancies as long as possible. Eventually, however, they will have to go as the scheme "of elevating the level of this quarter proceeds and as the plans for providing a settlement a short distance away on a higher level progresses.

Work on this latter part of the scheme has not yet begun but it is understood that the survey will shortly be put in hand and that contracts will be let out. The site of the settlement with which the villagers will be provided is some 200 yards away on a level higher by about 50 feet. It will lie immediately below the road up the gap to Repulse Bay. It is anticipated that this settlement will be ready for the villagers by the end of next year.

The Wongneichung village, by the time the reprovisioning scheme is complete, will all be levelled at a considerably higher elevation—it will be raised by over 20 feet in places. It will be on an inclined plane to allow of drainage into the nullah which has been constructed alongside and, extending as far as the Jewish cemetery, will afford sites for houses which will be safe from storm water inundations.
A considerable part of the earth for carrying out the filling in of the present village will come from the work of clearance carried out in connection with the road which is to join up with that to Repulse Bay at Bowen Road.

Trade in this article had not been good last year, when the amount exported was very large, and this year it was still worse. The average selling price during 1924 had been R. 1,400 per 100 cattles; during most of this year it was R. 1,200 to R. 1,250, whilst at the very end it fell to R. 1,000. These prices meant selling at a loss. This decrease in selling price is put down to the competition of artificial silk, which has gained considerable popularity in Burma on account of its cheapness.

The Kanal route between this port and Bhamo came into favour again sooner than was expected, after the Shan rebellion of the previous year had driven trade to follow the alternative Long-ch'uan route. The former is now adopted again by seven-tenths of the traffic. The accommodation en route is still much rougher than it was before the rebellion, but even so compares not unfavourably with that on the alternatives route, which is also longer and harder. The Kanal Shan chieftain took advantage of the disturbed conditions, which were the aftermath of the rebellion, to levy rather heavy illegal taxes on the trade passing through his domains, but these finally ceased in November, and a special deterrent to trade following this route was thus removed.
