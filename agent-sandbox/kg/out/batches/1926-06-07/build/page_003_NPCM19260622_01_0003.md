## [9] FORTY YEARS AGO.

**HONGKONG IN THE EIGHTEEN EIGHTIES.**

**COLONY'S EARLIER DAYS.**

Interesting Impressions of An ex-Governor.

Below are given a few generit comments from the autobiography of Sir William Des Vaeux, a former Governor of Hongkong. When passing through Japan he comments: —

"Our countrymen whom I met there seemed to me for the most part," somewhat aggressively British—in fact (if I may be permitted to indulge in canine (Latinity), ipsis Britannis Britanopiores—and to take apparently a pride in being entirely out of sympathy with the people among whom they lived and made their living. It occurred to me then, as it often did afterwards, that if members of the mercantile communities of the Far East were to make more effort to understand the point of view of the people of the country, whether Japanese or Chinese, it would not be the worse for either side—or for the world in general. One of the effects of this extreme aloofness is that strangers are apt to be most misled about the peoples of Japan and China by those who have lived longest among them, and whose accurate knowledge of them is therefore taken for granted."

Commenting, in reference to a visit to Shanghai, upon the fact that the Chinese dialect of one part of China is practically a foreign tongue to the native of another part, (although the written language is the same) Sir William remarks:

Languige Difficulty.

"The cause of this apparent anomaly is, I think, not difficult to imagine. The educated class throughout the Middle Kingdom having always been in communication with the seat of government and with one another, the same written language would naturally remain common to all. On the other hand, in a dense population, which appears to have remained much the same for over two thousand years, only a small fraction of people, living within a given area, would at any time have communication with any but their immediate neighbours, and thus language would tend to differentiate more than in other countries, where travellers would bear a larger proportion to the comparatively small number of inhabitants. We can see the working of similar causes in England, though its until recently sparse population and the much shorter period of our history have precluded them from having effect in the same degree. The agricultural labourers of Yorkshire and Cornwall have dialects so distinct that they would, I imagine, understand one another with difficulty. But for the rapid and easy means of communication which has been recently established, it is probable that when they had lived in the same localities as long as have the peoples of China, this difficulty would have become an impossibility. The distinct difference of dialect between the English of the European, natives of Australia and New Zealand and between both and any dialect of the mother country is a curious instance of the quickness with which spoken language tends to differentiate even within a very short space of time."

Sir William might have quoted as another example the differences between the American and the English language! He adds a comment on "the curious fact that coast Chinamen of different provinces are beginning to communicate with one another in 'Pidgin-English.'" This tendency has, of course greatly increased of recent years.

## [10] SCANDALS IN MANCHURIA.

**ALLEGATIONS OF CORRUPTION AMONGST RUSSIANS.**

Harbin, May 26th—The arrest and certain startling disclosures made in respect of a former chief of the detective police named L. S. Volkoff tend to whitewash the Chinese personnel of the Harbin police from charges of graft and callousness toward the interests of the community, and to bring out the fact that the Russian members are greatly, if not almost solely, responsible for certain phases that have characterised the activities of the Harbin police ever since the Chinese took control of the town and its affairs. The point again stands out that the wholesale employment of "White" Russians, who have been accustomed to easy-living, in the various Chinese government departments and institutions, in Manchuria tends to embroil the Chinese authorities in no end of unpleasantnesses.

Volkoff was arrested about three weeks ago by the Chinese authorities, but the reason for his arrest has been kept secret until yesterday when startling disclosures were made. It seems that on the examination of his private premises, the police came across certain papers which they had never expected to find. As a matter of fact he was arrested by the police and his lodgings were searched upon information, imparted by a kidnapper named Gromoff that he, Volkoff, had been interested in the kidnapping cases which have occurred in Harbin in recent years. When the police searched Volkoff's premises, amongst the papers in a cabinet they found a book and certain documents which brought out the fact that he had been receiving from seven to eight thousand dollars a month from the U.S.S.R. Consulate in Harbin. According to these papers, the money was given each month for distribution among various police officials as a reward for non-interference with certain U.S.S.R. nationals and institutions. The police are now investigating whether the sums received had ever been shared by other Russian officials in their employ.

Money for Non-Interference.
The sum paid out each monthly by the U.S.S.R. Consulate accounts for the non-interference, comparatively speaking, "with Soviet unions and gangs, despite the determination announced more than once of the Mukden authorities to eradicate certain phases of their activities. It is no wonder, therefore, that the article recently appearing in the columns of the "North-China Daily News" under the caption, "Oppressive Harbin Police," should have elicited the wrath of the Soviet organ, "Echo."

Without wishing to whitewash the “Reds” in any way, it must be admitted that the “White” Russians in Manchuria have proved themselves to be a highly incapable lot. Accustomed as they have been to easy living conditions in the heyday of Tsarism and anti-Red campaigns, in Siberia, a great number of them are not able to engage in honest labour.

It has leaked out that Marshal Chang Tso-lin's military headquarters are now much concerned over the increasing arrogance of the "White" troops forming part of the so-called "Allied forces" and are seriously considering how to keep them in check. Attempts have been made to withhold all information from the press and public as to the attitude of these "White" Russian troops, but several incidents having become publicly known, the military headquarters of Mukden are no longer able to hide the nasty situation that has arisen.

**ARIZONA HOUSE TO BE RESTORED.**

Washington, May 15.—To preserve the homes of the prehistoric Indians of the Grand Canyon area, Dr. J. Walter Fewkes, chief of the Bureau of ethnology under the Smithsonian Institution, has left Washington for a summer's work in Arizona. "He will concentrate his attention on the Wupaiki National Monument near Flagging, this contains the best preserved ruins and the highest standing walls of any Indian structors in central Arizona.

In the morning of the world the Indian emerged out of the mouths of the earth through the hasm of the Grand Canyon. They spread to their allotted places on the earth and built their homes, last of all came the Hopi, so that their homes were nearest the anyon. So runs the Hopi legend to explain man's origin and account or such ruins as that at Wupatki. Wupatki was probably inhabited when Caesar ruled at Rome. It is composed of a cluster of houses he largest of which contained three stories. The walls of these buildings are a striking monument, the craftsmanship of these ulders of thousands of years ago. They are constructed of a filling bed on both sides with slabs of pressed stone, often more or less artificially worked. But, as in the construction of all prehistoric asony in the southwest, some of the first principles of the craft—or instance, binding the stones at the corners—were neglected.

Dr. Fewkes proposes to restore the largest of these ancient houses so that it will have the same appearance as when its inhabitants deserted it. The stones will be aid in adobe as was the custom in pueblo dwellings, and the interior will be plastered with adobe-decorated with red pigment. The replace will be restored, and large logs will be laid across the top of the walls to support the roof of willow twigs, cedar bark and clay.
As a leading authority on the Hopi Indians, Dr. Fewkes has been asked, in conjunction with his trip, to collaborate in the preservation of some of the songs of this tribe. He will check the authenticity of the songs, of which phonograph records are to be made. From 1890 to 1896, Dr. Fewkes lived with the Hopi-Indians to study their customs, language, and beliefs, and was the first while man to be initiated into the mysteries of the famous snake dance.

## [13] POET AND AUTHOR.

**DEATH OF MR. WILLIAM CANTON.**

Mr. William Canton, poet, author, and journalist, died on May 2 at Handon, in his 81st year. Born in the Isle of Chusan, off the East Coast of China, on October 27, 1845, he spent his boyhood in Jamaica, was educated mainly in France, and, going to England, became a teacher. A long spell of journalism in Glasgow followed, and it was during his Glasgow days that he published his first volume. "A Lost Epic, and Other Poems" (1887), the "other poems" including the songs of childhood which first revealed a "poet who would take his place among the laureates of the nursery.

In 1891 Canton left Glasgow for London to join the publishing house of Isblister, and became manager, also acting for a time as editor of the "Sunday Magazine" and sub-editor of the "Contemporary Review." During these busy years he wrote his most famous books, drawing his inspiration from the daughter born to him at Glasgow in 1890.

When he resigned his connection with Messrs. Isbister, he became the official historian of the British and Foreign Bible Society. Though without the slightest inside knowledge of the society's operations, he brought to his task powers of application which enabled him to complete a history in five volumes which remains a worthy memorial of the centenary celebrated in March, 1904.

In celebration of his 80th birthday a presentation was made to him last October by Sir James Barrie on behalf of his many friends.

Mr. Canton, who was awarded a Civil List pension in the spring of 1913, leaves a widow, and only son, the Brother Greatheart, who will be remembered by all lovers of that joyous little soul.—"W. V." in China, Express and Telegraph.
