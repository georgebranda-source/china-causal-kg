## [13] CHINA ASSOCIATION.

There seems no great reason to enthuse over the Hongkong Branch of the China Association, judging by the facts that after being twelve months in existence it has only 83 members and that out of these only 13 considered it worth while to attend the annual meeting yesterday. This apathy is to be regretted. The Hongkong Branch, after lying dormant for eight years, was revived last year solely as the outcome of the troubles in Canton. It ought to have been expected that in a crisis of this nature, affecting the prosperity of the Colony, there would have rallied round the Hongkong Branch of the China Association every British resident, irrespective of age or sex. There was urgent propaganda work to be done, and it was manifestly unfair for an individual British resident to be content with the role of a mere spectator and permit others to shoulder all the responsibility and all the expense. A membership of only 83 and an absentee list of 70 at the annual meeting combine to make an inglorious record of which every Briton outside the Branch membership ought to be heartily ashamed.

## [15] EAST OF SUEZ?

**HOME LIFE WINS**

What was generally recognised as the most successful debate held under the auspices of the European Y.M.C.A., Kowloon, took place last night. The topic of discussion was the very live one as to whether life East of Suez is preferable to that at Home.

There have been debates held at Y.M.C.A. in which experienced debaters have taken the principal parts and have held the audience by their oratory.

Last night, however, the younger members of the Society, having had little or no experience of public speaking, held the floor, and the Chairman, the Rev. J. Horace Johnson, voiced the feeling of all present when he said they had come out of the ordeal with flying colours. The object of the society was not to limit the subjects to discussion by those experienced in public speaking but to give the members the opportunity for self expression. The speeches from the upholders of the affirmative and negative and from the general body of the audience that night had proved that the society had made wonderful strides. It was the most successful evening from this point of view and from the numbers attending that had yet been held.

Mr. W. L. Walker, in proposing the motion "that life East of Suez is preferable to life at Home," gave a forceful speech in which he dealt largely with the monetary advantages which life in the East affords to many; also the conditions of ease with greater opportunity for leisure afforded by working conditions and shorter hours. The greater education afforded by experience in travelling and in an atmosphere of freedom for broadening one's mind was also the subject of reference.

Mr. E. R. Price, in opening for the negative, asked the audience to consider whether conditions of ease were better in the long run than conditions of keener competition such as one had at Home which tended to keep one mentally alert and to increase one's chances of rising to good positions when the opportunity arose; positions which entailed greater responsibilities and enhanced chances of promotion to higher positions of administration than one could ever attain to in the limited sphere of the East. He dealt with the peculiar difficulties of married people in the East in regard to separation with children sent home to be educated and drew a comparison between the limited opportunities afforded for relaxation and amusement in such places as Hongkong and the unlimited opportunities afforded at Home.

Mr. W. J. Keates, and Mr. H. Buxton followed in seconding the affirmative and negative respectively. Mr. Keates referred to the quick passage of time in the East and the social atmosphere which made for a more detached viewpoint in one's mental outlook, also the greater bonds of friendship which were cemented in the East by reason of separation from the Homeland. Mr. Buxton referred to the handicap of health under which many laboured in the East due to tropical conditions, maintaining that conditions of bodily health were essential if we were to give of our best in our occupations. Conditions of ease were a necessity in the East. They were not a necessity at Home and once the necessity for them was removed they ceased to be advantages.

Interesting contributions to the debate were made by Messrs. P. D. Wilson, Willea, Petrie, H. J. Fountain, G. J. Harman, Ingram, H. G. Swinburne and others.

The "China Mail" was able to throw considerable light on the good work that is going on in leper settlements in South China when, some months ago, we published an interview with Mr. Danner, a leper worker who had concluded a tour in South China, and a report of the year's happenings at the C.M.S. leper and settlement hospital at Pakhoi.

Mr. Danner then "foreshadowed the visit to Hongkong of " officials of the newly formed Chinese Mission to Lepers in which Chinese medical men are taking a keen interest.

News has been received in Hongkong of the coming visit of the Rev. T. C. Wu, General Secretary of the Chinese Mission to Lepers, Shanghai. Mr. Wu is at present in Canton having travelled there overland by way of Foochow, Amoy and Swatow in the interests of his Mission.
Through the courtesy of General Chang Kai-shek at Canton, Wu was enabled to visit Tai Kam, an island leper colony in a notorious pirate infested region. The trip was made by a Chinese gunboat in company with the Rev. John Lake of Tungshan and the Rev. Shu Ding-chong, the Superintendent of the Tai Kam Leper Colony.

Although the party met with many armed pirates on the trip they were unmolested. At the settlement, Mr. Wu preached to a gathering of over one hundred people, mostly "labourers" who combined piracy with their profession.

Mr. Wu is "expected" to arrive in Hongkong on Saturday and arrangements are being made for him to address some local meetings in regard to his humane work of helping and caring for the most afflicted class "of sufferers, the lepers.
