## [12] Central Calm Area.

<sub>section under [11] IF IT CAME.</sub>

The passage of the centre of a typhoon over two or more adjacent stations affords a means of determining the diameter of the calm area, which exists at the centre of a typhoon.

If for instance, at one station (A) the calm lasts for twenty minutes, while at another station (B) 2.4 miles to eastward the calm lasts 50 minutes and the typhoon is travelling north at the rate of 12 miles an hour, then the diameter of the calm area would be 12 miles.

At a third station (C) 9 miles to the east of (A) the calm would "would last 50 minutes also, but the wind would veer, whereas at A and B it would back, C being on the opposite side of the centre to A and B.

The centre calm, or eve of the storm as it is called, differs. in different typhoons. Its diameter varies from about 7 to 25 miles.

Sometimes an almost absolute calm occurs and sometimes light, variable winds are prevalent. Sometimes the skies clear completely sometimes partially and sometimes the heavy nimbus merely gives way to a veil of thinner cloud.

The barometric minimum (which varies from 27 inches to 29 inches) is said to occur sometimes at the beginning of the calm, sometimes shortly before, and sometimes shortly after; but as the time-scale of the ordinary barograph is usually very contracted and the driving clock an imperfect timekeeper, it is frequently impossible to say within 5, 10, or even 15 minutes, when the minimum pressure occurs. Probably it is within a few minutes of the calm. When the diameter of the calm area is large, and the sky clears, the temperature rises as the centre passes, otherwise there is no perceptible change.

Typhoons vary considerably in strength. In some the violence of the winds even 100 miles from the centre beggars description while in others a vessel may pass actually through the centre without sustaining serious damage.
On June 2, 1916, the Observatory in Kowloon was on the edge of the calm centre of a small typhoon, moving north-east and evidently filling up. The wind, which had been strong to fresh, died away completely between 1 p.m. and 2 p.m. increasing again with fresh squalls from northward by 4 p.m. No damage was done. In contrast to this may be mentioned the Mauritius typhoon of April 29, 1892, in which 10,000

people were killed.

In those days little was known by the public about typhoons, and the central calm was mistaken for the end of the storm. People therefore opened hurricanes shutters, and left their houses, some never to return and others to find them in ruins, or blown to another street by the terrific hurricane which followed the central calm.

Even if fully prepared Hongkong would suffer considerably, should the centre of a severe typhoon pass over the Island.
Ancient (and some modern) houses would collapse and probably a certain portion of the ships in shelter would be blown ashore and their decks swept clean. Many trees would be blown down. The greatest danger to pedestrians would be from tiles and corrugated iron hurtling through the air.

## [15] PERJURY.

**DRAFT OF NEW BILL.**

A Bill to amend the Perjury Ordinance it is proposed to introduce into the Legislative Council shortly.

This Bill proposes to effect a minor amendment in the Perjury Ordinance, 1922. Section 10 of that Ordinance makes it possible, when a witness has made two contradictory statements, to charge him with having made the contradictory statements and to obtain a conviction on proof of this contradiction without the necessity of proving the truth or falsehood of either statement. This section originally appeared as a section 6 of Ordinance No. 7 of 1857. Section 12 of the Perjury Ordinance gives a Judge or Magistrate power to direct a prosecution for perjury and to commit the accused for trial or to admit to bail. There is no similar power to order a prosecution for an offence against section 10. Clause 2 of the Bill proposes to give this power.

## [23] COMING VOTE.

### What it Means to Both Sides.

At the conference of miners' delegates which was held to-day Mr. Herbert Smith, President of the Miners' Federation, called upon the delegates to give reports on the position in their districts and the numbers of men who had returned to work. It is stated that it was found that in the aggregate 6,100 men had resumed work in all the coal fields, excluding safety men. The men who had returned were not necessarily coal, getters, but generally embraced every class of mine workers.

There was afteryard a discussion on the general position and on the recommendation of the Executive that the peace proposals framed in conjunction with the Bishops and the Free Church leaders should be accepted.
It is understood that the discussion was at times very animated. The Executive had ruled out of the proposals any arbitration on the question of hours, but delegates from some of the larger districts are stated to have objected also to arbitration on wage reductions.
At the close of the conference, however, it was officially stated that it had been decided by a very large majority to send the proposals to the districts to be voted upon, with a recommendation that they be accepted.

Whatever may be the result of the district vote, there will remain the fact that the primary proposal is that work shall be resumed for a period of four months at the old rate of wages whilst negotiations are proceeding, and that financial assistance shall be given by Government for this purpose. The Government has definitely stated that no such further subsidy or loan is possible. If the vote is favourable, however, it will at least mean that positive proposals have been substituted for the negative declarations of the miners' lenders, which have hitherto stood in the way of negotiations.—British Wireless Service.
