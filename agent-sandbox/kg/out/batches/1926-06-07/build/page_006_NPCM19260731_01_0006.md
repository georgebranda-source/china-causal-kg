## [12] THOSE RENTS.

On every hand the evidence is accumulating in regard to the increase of rents in the Colony. There are two sides to every case, and it is a coincidence that the respective versions are set out in our correspondence "columns today."

The first, from a Kowloon resident, puts his plea for a Fair Rents Board very fairly and moderately. His arguments should strengthen the hands of the Kowloon Residents' Association, about 90 per cent. of whose membership is reported to favour the creation by the Government of a Fair Rents Board.

These increases in rents were bound to follow the withdrawal of the protection to tenants afforded by the Rents Ordinance. In many cases, indeed, during the last fifteen months, landlords, in letting houses to new tenants, have carefully pointed out that such rents would hold good only for the duration of the strike and boycott, and that an increase must be expected afterward. In taking away the Rents Ordinance the authorities have taken away every prop of protection that many tenants had. There is now nothing for them to fall back on, and the only choice before them is to pay one, two, or half a dozen increases in rent—or clear out to other premises where they may possibly fare worse.

It cannot be gainsaid that the landlords have been as badly hit as other people by the conditions prevailing during the past fifteen months. In some cases a certain increase in rent could not be grudged. That 15 per cent. permitted by the "Government—to which one of our correspondents to-day refers—was at least reasonable, even although the very natural and human tendency on the part of people who do not own their own houses is to resist all and every increase in rent. How much further landlords can go beyond that 15 per cent. without being actually oppressive is not a question for us to discuss. Each case can only be taken on its merits. And that, again, can only be decided fairly and equitably by a Fair Rents Board.

He condemns the “principal tenant,” and gives a flagrant case of what is nothing more and nothing less than “squeeze.” In all the arguments submitted during the past eighteen months in favour of the retention of the Rents Ordinance on the Statute-book we have always held that many of the evils of high rents must be laid at the door of “principal tenants.” On one occasion we went so far as to suggest that there ought to be compiled and kept by the authorities, for public information, a list of “principal tenants” with the repts paid by them to the owners and the rents charged by them to sub-tenants. The views of others of our readers will be welcomed on all aspects of the rents question.

## [16] STOLEN TINPLÂTES.

**SEVEN LIGHTER MEN CONVICTED.**

**"SOCONY" PROSECUTION.**

Lengthy evidence was taken before Mr. J. H. B. Nihill at the Kowloon Magistracy yesterday when seven members of the crew of the Standard Oil Company's lighter "Delawar" stood charged with larceny of 16 cases of tin plates.

Mr. F. G. Vaux prosecuted on behalf of the Standard Oil Company, while Mr. W. B. Hind appeared for five of the defendants, the other two being unrepresented.

The case against the men was that they stole the cargo which was part of that discharged into the lighter by the s.s. "Speybank," the theft taking place while the lighter was taking shelter from the typhoon in Tsun Wan Bay.

After hearing evidence, His Worship convicted all the defendants, the first being sentenced to nine months' hard labour, the second and third to six months' hard labour, and the others to three months' hard labour.

## [17] CORRESPONDENCE.

**HIGH RENTS: "EMPTY ARRELI."**

**OWNER REPLIES.**

[To the Editor of the "China Mall"]
Sir,—It is all very well for tenants to rush into print to air their imaginary grievances about high rents. As an owner I have kept some of the impertinent letters I received from some of my tenants in the control days, when I was seeking high and low to find accommodation for my brother who had then retired from his position in Kwangsi.

The principal tenant of one flat, rental $26 per mensem, wanted me to pay him $500 gratuity before he would surrender possession to me, so that my brother could live in my house. Times have now changed and the fortunes of war will fluctuate again.
One argument by tenants is that we bought at the height of the boom and now fleece innocent householders to repair our errors of judgment. This does not enter into the question at all. I was not forced to buy when prices were high. My tenant is not bound to occupy my property. If he thinks the rent prohibitive; led him move out to suburbs and get a flat at $25 a month.

This leads me on to another fruit. Chinese tenants are not ventilating their grouses in print. They have their "face" to preserve. To them, the payment of more rent is better than telling the public that one's landlord has "changed" $184 into $260. I am sure that residents in the more select parts of the Colony would have found "other methods than this" futile, and puerile, attempt to "how up the landlord class." Enclosing my card.

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"] Sir,—I shall be grateful if you will kindly publish the following few home truths in your paper for the benefit of the powers that be. The present high cost of living—largely due to exorbitant rents—is making it almost an impossibility for a Britisher to live in this British Colony.

I may state that I have been a resident of the Colony for the past twenty-five years and have always done my utmost to assist the Government by offering my services during any political or industrial disturbance. I have also served my Country as a soldier and as a volunteer for twenty-two years. I therefore consider that I am justly entitled to some consideration from the Government during this time of stress.

I have just received a notice from my landlord stating that my rent will be increased by sixty Dollars per month as from the 1st August, 1926. When I

The same the tenant of my present house, eighteen months ago, my monthly rental was $125. Since that time the Government, on behalf of the Landlords, allowed an increase of 15 per cent., which I, of course, had to pay. The landlord knowing that he has a free hand, now the Rents Ordinance has been repealed, has increased my rent by $60. Am I to be turned out of my house, which will certainly happen unless the Government gives that assistance in the way of a Fair Rents Board or other methods of stemming such exorbitant demands.

## [27] NARROW ESCAPE.

**HEAVY FALL NEAR TAIKOO DOCK.**

**WORKMEN SCATTER.**

A fall of about 2,000 tons of debris yesterday outside the entrance to Taikoo. Dockyard, near Sai Wan Terrace, was fortunately not attended by loss of life, workmen who were leaving at the time having a narrow escape.

The landslide occurred about mid-day as a large number of the employees were leaving the Dockyard and there would probably have been some casualties if the slide which brought down the bulk of the debris had not been preceded by a smaller one which warned the men and enabled them to scatter in time.

Estate in Hongkong amounting to $16,507 was left by the late Mr. William Stewart Davidson, formerly a well-known resident of Shanghai. Probate of the will in respect of his estate in Hongkong has been granted in the Supreme Court.

Local Chinese theatre-goers will be pleased to hear that Mr. Sit Kok-seen, the famous matinee Idol, is back in Hongkong, after a stay of over a year in Shanghai.
