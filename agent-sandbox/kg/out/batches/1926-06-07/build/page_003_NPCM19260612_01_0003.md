It is pleasant sometimes to take a backward glance and get a picture of Hongkong in days long gone by. There must be much interest in reading some extracts from the life-story of a former Hongkong Governor, Sir G. William Des Vœux, G.C.M.G., who "reigned" here for a short period from 1887.

In "My Colonial Service," Sir William Des Vaux gave an account of his career in British Guiana, St. Lucia, Trinidad, Fiji, Australia, New Foundland and Hongkong, with interludes at home, in course of which he met several of the eminent figures of Victorian era. Sir William was evidently a conscientious and fearless administrator, and a strong defender of native races in Colonies where plantation labour systems tended to place undue power in the hands of the planters. Life in the Colonial service proved full of hardships in Sir William's case, and he remarks in his preface: "As a consequence of several dangerous maladies, including yellow fever and sunstroke, and two serious accidents which cause concussion of the brain and spine, almost the whole of my life as a Governor was a continual struggle against "ill-health." From the tropics Sir William went to New Foundland where he arrived in phenomenally cold weather; thence he sailed some time afterwards to Hongkong. The earlier part of his two volume work deals with his career prior to arrival in Hongkong. It is full of interest and variety, but for the present purpose quotation will be limited to the chapters on Hongkong.

## [2] Hongkong in 1887.

We arrived in Hongkong (writes Sir William) in the early morning of October 6, 1887; a fine day, which showed to the best advantage the very beautiful harbour. Looking at the abundant shipping (including many ocean steamers, some large sailing clippers, and innumerable junkes), the imposing front of the crowded city beyond, and, on the mainland of China, the town of British Kowloon, already growing into importance, I could not help marveling at the contrast between this scene and the one witnessed here: forty-seven years before, when a visiting man-of-war would have found itself alone in the harbour, and when the island was inhabited only by a few fishermen and pirates, and, according to the report of an engineer of the time, contained apparently only one site fit for a European house. What a monument of British enterprise!

[One could wish that that engineer could see the Peak to-day. He would have to admit that he was a creature of limited imagination and contrivance.]

The description of the Official landing might have been written for the present day: these functions are naturally stereotyped in character, and Sir William's account of the landing ceremonies and of his first impressions of Government House are only interesting as representing the "inside viewpoint." In those days the "houses of the more well-to-do inhabitants" rose only "for a third of the distance up the mountain." The Botanic Garden was "inferior to the Trinidad garden in luxuriance of foliage, but had nevertheless a beauty of its own which could hardly be surpassed. Placed on a slope which enabled the greater part of it to be taken in by a single coup d'oeil, its general effect was striking. Considering that its site was originally bare rock, out of which a large portion of it had originally been scooped, and that most of the soil supporting the vegetation had been borne thither, the place was typical of the enterprise everywhere evident in this remarkable little Colony."

Here are some more first impressions:—

"Looking out at night from the

House, before the moon had risen, I witnessed an effect which was quite new to me. The sky, though clear of clouds, was somewhat hazy, so that the small-magnitude stars were not visible, though some of the larger ones were plain enough. Beneath, however, the air was quite clear, and consequently, though the vessels in the harbour were invisible in the darkness, their innumerable lights seemed like another hemisphere of stars even more numerous than the others, and differing only as being redder.

"The more I saw of Hongkong, the more I was struck with the enterprise which had created such a city on such a spot, bearing in mind that the foundations of all the buildings—many of them" of almost palatial size and appearance—and all the roads had been cut out of—of the solid granite, and that the trees everywhere visible over the face of the mountain had all been planted on equally unpromising soil. The roads were admirably constructed and kept, those ascending the Peak being entirely of Portland cement, so as to withstand the torrential rains, which would quickly have swept away any less durable material."

## [8] PROBLEMS OF CHINA.

**MR. KIYOUSE INUI AND PROFESSOR BURGESS.**

Tokyo, May 29.—Interesting aspects of various problems of China's internal conditions, including the questions before the customs conference in Peking, were presented by two speakers yesterday at the Pan Pacific Club luncheon at the Imperial Hotel. The speakers were Mr. Kiyouse Inul, of Japan's delegates at the customs conference, and Professor John Stewart Burgess of Yenching University, Peking.

The tariff troubles of China are as old as the history of her commercial intercourse with modern nations, Mr. Inul said, as a preface to his remarks about the difficult questions before the customs conference. After giving some of the historical facts in connection with the case, Mr. Inul showed how the Chinese delegates themselves precipitated a further tangle by making ad interim propositions to which none of the Powers could agree, though the variance between the suggestions of the Powers might have been smoothed over in time. Continuing, he said:

"Before, we could come to any more" of these questions, the bi-annual coup d'etat took place and China lost her captain and the Conference its host. ## [15] CHINESE ILL-TREATED.

**HEILUNGKIANG MERCHANTS COMPLAIN TO CHAMBER**

Chinese merchants in the Russian portion of Heilungkiang have complained to the Chinese General Chamber of Commerce there of ill-treatment by Russian soldiers who looted their stores and carried off 11 Chinese as prisoners.

A telegram from these merchants states that an armed squad of Russian soldiers made a thorough search of the Chinese stores. Disappointed in their inspection for contraband, they arrested 11 Chinese and looted large quantities of valuables. In spite of the strong protest lodged by the Chinese Consul-General in these districts, they still hold the Chinese. The latter have not been tried and they continue to suffer both bodily and mentally.
The telegram continues: “Since their form of government was changed, the Russians have, on the one hand, declared their opposition to imperialism and on the other hand continue their aggressive policy, which is even worse than that of the imperialists. They have purposely delayed excluding the Sino-Russian Trade Agreement without which we are left unprotected. Again, they have started Bolshevik propaganda. And third, but not least, they have done their utmost to suppress Chinese merchants. According to international practice, the people of two contracting Powers are entitled to enjoy the privileges of trade. They have not only failed to discharge their obligations but have tried on the contrary to suppress us. You are requested to present the case to the Government on our behalf and make known these things to our fellow countrymen.”

## [16] IMMENSE WINE STORE.

**WAITS U.S. DRY LAW PREPAI**

Paris.—Piled up in the million of wine cellars of Rhetms and Rordenaux are ten million bottles of champagne and fine wines waiting for the day when the dry laws of the United States are wiped off the books.

France is convinced that the day is not far distant when America will lift the bars against wines, and she has mapped out an extensive campaign to alleviate the great American thirst. Five minutes after the prohibition law is repealed, France could start loading twenty vessels with the wines she has stored away for America.

It was the winegrowers of the champagne country who sunered most when America went dry and they hope to make up for the eight lim years within a short time, Champagne, while distinctly a French product—so much so that real champagne must be produced within an area 25 miles square—is equally an American drink.

Americas always absorbed the greater part of the French champagne production before the war and what America did not want was shipped to London or to Berlin. "Gout American"—American taste was perfected for the American palate and only Americans like this sweet, bubbling liquid.

The tourists from overseas drink only a small part of the "gout Americain" and the rest is stored away waiting the day when America lets down certain bars and opens up others. America shall benefit by having waited, for the wine has aged perfectly and there are available now some remarkable specimens, such, for example as the 1919 vintage which has no equal in the past half century.

In the Bordeaux region, much of the claret produced before the wif was destined for America. England has been converted to claret drinking—and—a certain quantity finds its way to South America, but Bordenux still has a million bottles stored away for post-prohibition celebrations in America.

The French winegrower was hard hit by the war. The vines in the Champagne country were torn up by their roots when trenches were dug through the vineyards; vines all over France rotted when there were no longer men left to care for them.
