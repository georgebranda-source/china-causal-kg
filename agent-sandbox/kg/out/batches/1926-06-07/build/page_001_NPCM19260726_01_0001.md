That the typhoon signals will be up by Saturday and that Hongkong will have another bad weekend is the prediction made by a local amateur weather prophet with considerable confidence.

This gentleman, as a marine expert, has always to be on the lookout for sudden changes in the weather.

He pointed out to a “China Mail” man this morning that the barometer, at sea-level, was round 29.70-and was “too steady to be good.”

That “deep depression” hanging around also portended typhoon possibilities, he said.

Pointing to the "rather dark clouds over the hills at the eastern entrance to the harbour, he said that he always found guidance there.

Last night and the night before, he explained, there had been, very high tides, and these, in his opinion, were contributory factors.

He thought that the "glass" would begin to show signs of falling shortly, that a typhoon would be observed in a day or two, and that local warnings would be exhibited by Saturday at the latest. As to the week-end prospects he was exceedingly pessimistic.

## [7] KOWLOON ROBBERY

**INCIDENTS NEAR "CHATERS BUNGALOW."**

**PONED AS POLICE.**

—

An alleged robbery at a hut adjoining the house known as "Chater's Bungalow," abutting Nathan and Austin Roads, Kowloon, was the subject of enquiry at the adjourned Criminal Sessions this morning.

It was alleged that two men represented themselves as police officers empowered to search for opium, tied up the gardener who was in charge, and took from him certain articles of property.
Three men were charged with being concerned in the robbery, Lai Sang, Chan Sin and Chan Po, a charge of receiving stolen goods also being preferred against Chan Po.

An interesting feature of the case was the statement by the Crown Solicitor, Mr. H. K. Holmes, that information had been given to the police by a man who admitted that he had a grudge against the gardener who had been the victim of the robbery and that he would like to get him into trouble with the Police.

In opening the case Mr. Holmes said that the gardener, whose name was Wong Chuen, lived in a hut near to Austin Road at the point where Cox's path ran into it. Shortly after 9 p.m., on June 23 Wong Chuen was lying in the hut smoking opium when two men entered stating that they were police officers come to search for opium. They opened a box on which Wong Chuen was lying and one of the men, then said to the other "Don't let him get away." The gardener was then seized and some rope which was kept for drawing water at a well was used to tie him up, the rope being twisted round his neck and his hands tied.

Having taken a belt from their victim the two men ran away followed by the gardener who was able to use his legs. Mr. Braga, who was walking in Austin Road, saw two men going in the direction of Nathan Road and also saw the gardener with whom he spoke in Chinese. In view of what he was told, Mr. Braga pursued the men but was unable to find in what direction they had gone. He returned to the tied up man and released him.

The police received a report and were able to arrest the first prisoner in Shanghai street, Mongkok-tsui, the following evening. At an identification parade the prisoner was identified by the gardener as one of the men who had taken part in the robbery. The second prisoner was arrested some days later at a matshed in Stubbs Road and the third prisoner at Shamshulpo the same day. The latter when taken to the police station produced a pawn ticket which enabled the police to get back the stolen watch.

Mr. Holmes referred to a man who would be called to give evidence as the man who had given certain evidence to the police prior to the arrests. He had told the police that he had a grudge against the gardener and that he had suggested to one of the prisoners that he would like to injure him in some way so as to get even with him. The original plan seemed to have been that information should be laid with the police with regard to possession of opium but that of a robbery seemed to have been substituted later.

When charged at the Magistracy, the first prisoner declined to make a statement; the second said "I did not take anything at all" and the third made an involved statement in which he said that at the time of the robbery he was in Hongkong, at the back of the Mosque Temple, and did not know anything about it. The statement referred to a man named Fan Ki whom the police should ask to "consult his conscience in the matter" (this was the translation from the Chinese).

(Mr. H. G. Sheldon represent one of the prisoners).

One animal case of Rabies was reported to the Medical Officer of Health during Saturday and Sunday.

## [10] PROFESSOR SUMMONED BY A STEWARD.

**$60 FOR A BLOW.**

"Lengthy evidence was called by the complainant, a Chinese steward of the Hongkong Hotel, before Mr. R. E. Lindsell at the Central Magistracy this morning, when Professor Maximilian Langsner, who is to give a demonstration at the Theatre Royal to-morrow, was summoned for assault."

Mr. R. A. Wadeson appeared for the complainant and outlined his case which was that the defendant threw away a chit book produced by complainant to book the table and later assaulted him by striking a blow on the chest which knocked him down.

Complainant in this "evidence said that he "refused an offer made by the defendant in the presence of Mr. C. D. Robertson, assistant manager, of the Hotel, to pay him $5 as compensation. Witness consulted a Chinese doctor who agreed to cure him for $60.

His Worship: One blow on the chest costs $60?

Witness: That was what he demanded.

His Worship: And you agreed to pay it?

In evidence, Mr. Robertson said that defendant agreed to pay the $60, but later changed his mind. He mentioned that complainant had been with the Hotel for 35 years and there was no complaint against him.

Professor Langsner, giving evidence on oath, said that he was a guest at the Hongkong Hotel. On the night of the 20th he went for dinner together with his secretary to his usual table, but was ordered by the complainant to sit at another table. This he considered to be an insult and he insisted on the manager being summoned. The complainant then came with a chit book and refused to go away, "I lost my temper and pushed him away," said the Professor.

## [12] MOTOR THEFT.

**EUROPEAN MOTORIST'S LOSS**

**HAWKER CHARGED.**

A marine hawker was charged before Mr. R. E. Lindsell at the Central Magistracy this morning with the theft of a motor car radiator badge and alternatively with receiving the article knowing "same to have been stolen.

Evidence was given that the motor car, belonging to Mr W. K. Reynolds, of the Hongkong University, was left at Lyttleton Road. On Mr Reynolds returning to the car, the cover of the radiator on which was mounted the Hongkong Automobile Association badge had disappeared.

A report was immediately made and the same day a constable accosted the defendant and another man, who bolted on seeing the constable, leaving behind some baskets which they were carrying. The constable asserted that defendant in the course of his flight produced something from his pocket and threw it into a school yard. This was found to be the missing article.

Mr. W. B. Hind arrived late and informed His Worship that he was appearing for the defence. The evidence was read out to him and the case was adjourned until to-mortow.

A Chinese woman pleaded guilty at the Central Magistracy this morning to unlawful possession of 52 taels of prepared opium and 28 taels of raw opium. ## [18] COAL CRJSIS.

**FAVOURABLE DEVELOPMENT EXPECTED.**

**FRESH TERMS ANNOUNCED.**

*Ruxby, July 24.*

Newspapers anticipate that within the next few days some favourable development in the coal situation will occur and hopes are entertained that debates which will take place in both "Houses of Parliament on Monday may lead to re-opening of negotiations.

Meanwhile it is reported that the drifting back to work by miners continues slowly but the movement is still almost entirely confined to Midlands coalfields.

It is stated in Labour circles that new terms have been prepared by Nottinghamshire and Derbyshire colliery owners upon which pits in those counties will be open next week.

The "Daily Mail" says the proposed new terms, which have been prepared with the approval of the owners' national organisation, will give the miners a wide range of choice. Offers will be framed in such a way that rates of wages which can be paid for seven hours, seven and a half hours and eight hours a day can be studied by men.

## [19] The Churches.

<sub>section under [18] COAL CRJSIS.</sub>

The Bishops and Free Church leaders have issued a statement in which their efforts to effect a settlement of the coal dispute are reviewed and a further appeal is made to the Government to consider proposals already laid before the Prime Minister.

They point out that these terms, which follow closely those of the Coal Commission's reports and the Samuel Memorandum, which the Trade Unions Council approved during the general strike, have been already accepted by the Miners' Federation Executive.

In the mining districts these terms have been criticised on the ground that failing settlement by negotiation they provide for the setting up of a joint arbitration board with an independent chairman.

Reorganisation of the industry is advocated by the church leaders and meets with little opposition. The chief objection by the Government to the proposals is that they are based on a further subsidy for the continuance of pre-strike rates of pay during the four months period allotted to the negotiations.
The Church leaders' statements will be emphasised from church pulpits throughout the country to tomorrow.—British Wireless Service.

To-morrow's debates on the consultation in both Houses of Parliament will probably indicate whether any fresh efforts to settle the dispute by negotiation are in prospect.

Although no favourable development is apparent, restrained optimism (that the end of the conflict will be brought in sight within the next few days) continues to prevail. In some measure this is based on the fact that the number of miners who have returned to work in Warwickshire is steadily increasing but in other parts of the country, and particularly in the North of England, there has been no movement on the part of the men to break away from the instructions of the Miners' Federation.

Mr. A. J. Cook, Secretary of the Federation, addressed meetings in Warwickshire yesterday, and expressed regret that they had taken action without consulting the Federation. The object of his visit was to check the movement for the resumption of work on the new terms offered by the Warwickshire owners. Sixteen hundred are said to have returned in the past two days, and altogether 8,000 will be working to-morrow.

## [20] PRATAS STATION.

**HONGKONG OFFICIALS UNABLE TO GO.**

**"HAIYUNG" DEPARTS.**

The Chinese cruiser "Haiyung has left Hongkong for Prata: about 180 miles to the South of this Colony.

She should have departed some days ago but was held up by the typhoon.

High Chinese officials are on board whose visit is in connection with the inaugural of the station which will send out meteorological reports by wireless.

Of the officials in Hongkong, who were to have gone down, it is known that several had to decline the invitation because of pressure of work, but Mr. N. R. M. Shaw, acting deputy commissioner of Chinese Maritime Customs, has left on the "Haiyung."

The Hongkong rescue tug "Kau Sing" recently took down a wireless operator.

Local experts speak exceedingly well of the work performed by Pratas in locating the first-two typhoons which came this way this season.

## [21] TOWN PLANNING

**MODEL SCHEME FOR EAST KENT.**

**STATE GUARANTEES**

A model town planning scheme, which has been initiated in East Kent in connection with the development of the new coal fields, was the subject of a conference yesterday at which seventeen local authorities interested in preserving the amenities of life were represented.

The scheme aims at encouraging new industry without destroying the natural beauty of the vicinity of the new pits, and already a considerable sum of money has been guaranteed by the State for the enterprise and the work of building new towns has been begun. Eight of these are in contemplation if the pits prove as successful as is hoped.

In such case, it is anticipated that the "discovery of coal and iron stone in East Kent will mean the immigration of 278,000 new residents, or doubling the present population.

The Rt. Hon. Neville Chamberlain, Minister of Health, who attended yesterday's conference, commended those responsible for the foresight that was being displayed, and he regretted that in the earlier days development was allowed to proceed without plan or thought, with the result that immense sums were to-day being spent on repairing the terrible errors of the past. At no time was there a greater need for town planning in Great Britain than now, where housing development on recently constructed roads was proceeding at a rate never before known.—British Wireless Service.

A coolie was charged before Mr. Nihill this morning at the Kowloon Magistracy with robbery. It was alleged that the coolie stole 180 catties of spring onions from the garden of another Chinese at Tai Hang Village.
