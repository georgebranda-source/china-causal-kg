Hongkong mourns the death, at the age of 80 years, of Sir Paul Chater, who in every sense, by long residence and by enterprise, good fortune and generous giving, was the grand old man of the Colony's business, social and political life. It was Paul Chater's Hongkong—under the Empire—which Kipling made to address the Mother Country in the words, "Ha!], Mother! Hold me fast; my Praya sleeps under innumerable keels to-day."
Son of Chater Paul Chater, an Armonian, Paul Catchick Chater was born in Calcutta in 1846. He received his education at La Martiniere College, and at eighteen years of age went to Hongkong as an assistant in the now defunct Bank of Hindustan, China and Japan. As a British Colony, Hongkong was not very old then, and the young bank clerk, drawing a salary of something like 25s. a week, looked into the future with a discerning eye. The opportunities he saw were many, but not behind a bank counter, so he resigned and started business very early in like as an exchange and bill broker. Two years later he was able to take a leading part in local affairs. In 1870 he was joined by the late H. N. Mody, who afterwards, by virtue of his public benefactions, including the gift to the Colony of the University of Hongkong, became Sir Hormusjee Mody. Bt. The partnership, a very successful one, continued until death removed Sir Hormusjee about ten years ago. It

its University to the instigation of Sir Paul Chater; certainly Sir Paul was one of its most generous supporters. But the University was not his only care. Throughout his long career in Hongkong he was foremost in originating and fostering projects for the benefit of the Colony in the faith which he often preached that the future held no limit to its greatness. He had no use for the pessimist, or for those who sat content with the Colony's achievements, and until last year, when the boycott brought commercial disaster to many, Hongkong never failed to justify his faith. The Kowloon Wharf and Godown Company, the Hongkong Land Investment Company, the Star Ferry Company, the Dairy Farm and other very prosperous undertakings were established by him in conjunction with Messrs. Jardine, Matheson and Co. Indeed, a list of the industries and companies which he either originated or assisted in forming and the important blocks of buildings that he and his late partner constructed as owners would be a very long one. Queen's Building, built thirty years ago, which remains to day the most conspicuous feature of the waterfront of Hongkong, was his conception to house the leading shipping and commercial firms, and it gave the start to what is now a striking harbour front, all reclaimed from the sea. With Prince's Building behind, also a most successful Chater enterprise, it flanks the whole of one side of what, is now known as Statue Square. In the centre of this square is the fine Jubilee statue of Queen Victoria, after whom the capital of Hongkong is named, with figures of King Edward, Queen Alexandra, King George, Queen Mary, as well as other notables such as Sir Thomas Jackson and Sir Henry May. Here also the Cenotaph and Hongkong and Shanghai Bank War Memorial are erected. Sir Paul was chairman of and leading contributor to the Jubilee Statue Fund, and it was his purse which provided the other members of the Royal Group. When the Prince of Wales visited Hongkong in 1923 Sir Paul offered to add his statue, but the Prince, with a thought for the men who had fought in the war, preferred that Sir Paul's offer should take the form of support of the ex-Active Servicemen's organisation, and the result was a suitable club for them.

## [13] On The Council.

As a member of the Legislative and Executive Councils of the Colony, Sir Paul held the record for length of service. It was in 1886 that he was elected to a temporary vacancy on the Legislative Council as the representative of the Unofficial Justices of the Peace, though opposed by two other candidates. On the resignation of Mr. F. Sassoon in the following year, Sir Paul (Mr. Chatter, as he was then), although absent from the Colony at the time, was elected for the full term of six years, at the expiration of which period he was re-elected. He was again re-elected in 1890 for a further period of six years. Meanwhile (In 1898) instructions were received from the Secretary of State for two Unofficial Members to be added to the Executive Council, and Sir Paul was selected as the Senior Member. After being honoured in the Jubilee list with a C.M.G. he was knighted in 1902, following his representation.

## [15] His Love of Racing.

Sport of all kinds found a liberal and ready patron in Sir Paul Chater. As a young man he was one of the leading cricketers in the Colony, being a member of the first team for ten years. All his life he bore marks of his intrepidity as a wicketkeeper, for he disdained pads. His chief interest in the direction of sport/lay, however, in the Hongkong Jockey Club, of which he was the oldest official. He пояsted a record of 63 successive attendances at the annual "Derby," and his stable of "Dahlias" hold a proud place in the annuals of racing in the Far East, there being no race, either in Hongkong, or Shanghai, that Sir Paul has not won and several of the classics many times over. He first joined the ranks of owners in 1874, and had long been Senior Steward of the Hongkong Jockey Club. At the meeting of 1884 he achieved the most remarkable success of any one stable on record, winning 15 races with 10 different ponies out of a string of 13 which he had running. He then retired on his laurels and became a judge, but on the death of his partner, Sir H. N. Mody, he resigned this position, which he had occupied for 29 years. and began owning again. ## [16] Relief of Distress.

The amount of Sir Paul's charitable givings will probably never be known. His practical sympathy never failed in flood, famine, earthquake or other disaster. His name invariably headed the list of every public appeal for relief and his cheque was always the most munificent. Privately he has helped many a lame dog over the stile. St. Andrew's Church at Kowloon was regularly attended by him, and was built entirely at his expense. He also liberally helped St. John's Cathedral on many occasions. Shortly after the Great War he endowed those two churches to the tune of £25,000 each. Union Church was favoured with £8,000 shortly afterwards, Sir Paul writing that although belonging to another denomination he had read and benefited from the Pastor's sermons as published in the Press. The "Seamen's Home, one of his partner's gifts to the Colony, and every other welfare and charitable institution in the Colony derived liberal support from Sir Paul. It is safe to say that Hongkong's institutions will sadly miss their chief benefactor. A recent gift was one of eleven lakhs of rupees into his Alma Mater in India, Lu Martinlere College.

## [17] Old Hongkong

It is learned that a very fine collection of old pictures and engravings relating to Hongkong, China and the Treaty Ports, some of which were exhibited in the Hongkong Section at the British Empire Exhibition in 1924, is, thanks to Sir Paul, to have its permanent place in the Colony. Whether this will be at Marble Hall, his magnificent home, overlooking the city, or in a much-heeded new City Hall, comprising library, museum, art gallery, etc., of which Sir Paul prepared plans as a War Memorial remains to be seen. It is to this collection that Dr. Hosea Morse acknowledges indebtedness for the many valuable illustrations to the recently published records of the East India Company's trade with China and the Far East. Sir Paul also possessed one of the finest collections of old porcelain in the world, its value being computed at a quarter of a million sterling. Part of this collection was exhibited some years ago at the Kensington Museum and the whole collection has now been gifted to the Colony.

In addition to his British decorations, Sir Paul was honoured by the French Government with the Legion of Honour, and he also had the Order of the White Elephant from Siam, for which country he was Consul in Hongkong for many years. Sir Paul was a distinguished Freemason, and only recently the Grand Lodge invested him with the 33rd Degree, the first occasion on which this high distinction has been conferred on anyone outside the United Kingdom. One of the most interesting men in the East and one of the wealthiest, he entertained most of the Colony's distinguished visitors amid the splendours of Marble Hall, and they one and all found him a cheerful and delightful host. That his memory will be perpetuated in bronze in the Statue Square he did so much to embellish there is little doubt, but no memorial can compete with that which he reared for himself by foresight, wise judgment, unshakeable faith, sound enterprise, and the capacity for overcoming obstacles and doing big things. In spite of his great age, Sir Paul was in almost daily attendance at his office to the end, and there he extended a kindly greeting to all sorts and conditions of men.

## [20] At THE STAR.

**LIONEL BARRYMORE IN "A MAN OF IRON."**

Showing to-day at the Star Theatre, Kowloon, is "A Man of Iron," starring Lionel Barrymore, who has been absent from local screens for some time.

The story of the play deals with the domestic strife of Philip Durban, the Iron Master, whose loveless marriage has shattered, the cherished happiness that he sought in the union with the beautiful Claire Bowdoin. Claire, the pampered daughter of a scheming social climber, is infatuated with the "worthless" fortune-hunting Prince Novakian, who discards her for a wealthier debutante. Claire, seeing the foolishness of her vanity, turns to her husband, who is cold to her advances. There is much tense drama in development of the story from this point, leading up to an impressive duel. The development is smooth and rapid, and provides a series of tense dramatic episodes that will hold the attention until the very end of the picture.

Lionel Barrymore, though dominating the picture, shares the acting honours with an excellent cust. Mildred Harris, who has been starred in many productions, is lovely as well as convincing in the role of Martha Durban, who plays her part with splendid restraint.
