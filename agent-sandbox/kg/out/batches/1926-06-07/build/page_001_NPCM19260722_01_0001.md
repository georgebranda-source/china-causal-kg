## [6] FACITS & FIGURES

The Peak, Conduit Road and Robinson Road districts are entirely supplied from the Pokfulam Road pumping station with water.

Water is pumped to the 750 ft. tank situated close to Hattom Road and the water gravitates from this tank, which when full holds 388,000 gallons to Conduit Road and May Road as far as the May Road Peak Tram station; and Robinson Road as far as Peak Road (Seth's Corner).

The Peak supply is pumped to the Peak reservoir, which holds when full 395,000 gallons, and is situated just below the Peak flagstaff. Water from the Peak reservoir, gravitates all over the Peak district west and south of Findlay Path and Plantation Road.

Water also gravitates from the Peak reservoir at night to Mount Gough service reservoir, which holds when full 211,000 gallons, and is situated on the summit of Mt. Gough above "Tanderagee." Mt. Gough service reservoir serves all the Peak district east and north of Findlay Path and Plantation Road to Wanchai Gap and Mt. Cameron.

Pokfulam Road pumping station contains three engines. Nos. 1, 2 and 3, the combined capacity of Nos. 1 and 2 being equal to the capacity of No. 3. These three pumps give the only supply to the Peak and high level districts.

On Monday a large boulder weighing between $2,000 and $3,000 tons fell from the hillside and completely demolished the eastern end of the pumping station, carrying away the Eliot approach road and seven pumping and supply mains and completely wrecking No. 3 engine.

No. 2 engine at the time of the accident was under overhaul and therefore dismantled, the dismantled portion being in the eastern half of the pumping station which is now under hundreds of tons of rock.

The only pump therefore left intact is the No. 1, or one quarter of the capacity of the original pumping station, and this pump cannot be operated until one of the pumping mains has been repaired. The whole of the higher levels is therefore completely isolated and is meantime entirely dependent on the water left in the Peak reservoir, Mt. Gough reservoir and 750 ft. tank—a rapidly dwindling quantity.

Work is at present being concentrated on the repair of one high level pumping main and it is hoped to get this through by Sunday or Monday when it will be possible to pump a small supply.

Work in the second place is being concentrated on the repair of No. 2 engine, but as both new forgings and castings are necessary it may be a month before this can be completed.

It is impossible so far to say how much, if any, of the No. 3 engine can be saved...but in any case it will be many months before a full supply can be resumed in the Peak and high level distriets.

This explanation is issued so that consumers may realise the real state of affairs and take the necessary steps to reduce to a minimum the consumption of water from the mains.

Water from nullahs should meantime be collected for washing and flushing purposes till further notice.

Drinking water should be boiled.

## [7] INTENSE GALE MISSES BY 40 MILES.

A very intense typhoon—the second to pass Hongkong this season — caused considerable havoc this morning.

Two sea-going steamers have already been blown aground. At mid-day, a river steamboat was drifting helplessly in another part of the harbour, assistance not being possible in the furious weather.

Although the "blow" was not felt very much in the city, business and the Law Courts and all business centres were at a standstill.

According to one expert, the “glass” has shown a record:

No calamity of importance had been reported by noon, on shore.

Expected yesterday to be making a bee-line for Hongkong, this typhoon showed practically no deviation at all and the centre missed us by 40 miles. It was so intense that—in the opinion of a Royal Naval officer—we would have been blown to bits if it had struck us with full force.
Government "red tape" precludes the "China Mail" from obtaining details as to squall velocity "barometer readings". A private instrument, on sea-level; "Hongkong" side, recorded 29.18 early this morning, before the change in signals: Towards 1 p.m., the reading was 29.19 and steady. Our informant thinks this is a record but is open to correction.

Ships in Distress.
The ships in distress are the s.s. "Pawnee," the s.s. "Confucius," and the steamboat "Ting On."
The "Pawnee" has two funnels and is familiar to Stonecutters bathers having lain up for months, at anchor, outside Yaumati Bay, between Stonecutters and the breakwater. The "Seistan" was another idle vessel in that locality but she went out to a harbour buoy and was safe.

The signals explain how the typhoon was anticipated here.
At 12:10 p.m. yesterday, No. 1 local signal was hoisted, (a red cone pointing upwards) meaning that a typhoon existed which may cause a gale in Hongkong within 24 hours.

At 4.20 p.m., there appeared the black-cone-pointing-upwards-(No.2) which meant: gale expected from the North (N.W. to N.E.)

At 6:30 a.m. to day; No. 6 went up. This is "the black double cone which means: Gale expected to increase. By this time the glass had fallen and it was fairly certain that the typhoon was coming here. As a matter of fact, the first signs of a blow were noticed about 3 a.m.

By 7.45 a.m., No. 4 signal (a black drum) had superseded the others, meaning: gale expected from the East (N.E. to S.E.)—showing that danger was now expected from another direction.
About 10 a.m., a message came through thus: The typhoon is passing to a few miles South of Gap Rock (which is about 35 miles to the south of Hongkong), in a westerly direction; no great increase of wind is anticipated at Hongkong.

## [13] "Empress" Delayed.

The R. M. S. "Empress of Russia" which was scheduled to leave at noon today, for Vancouver via ports, will not sail until the weather clears up. The liner, which was formerly alongside the dock in Kowloon, has now gone to buoy in midstream. Passengers and all concerned will be notified six hours before the liner's departure.

Mails from Home (letters and papers, London, June 24, and parcels) June 17) were due by the P. and O. steamer. "Macedonia," the vessel being scheduled to arrive at 6 a.m. to day. When inquiries were made at noon, it was ascertained that the vessel was outside Lyemeum Pass, waiting for better weather to make port.

The s.s. "President Wilson" arrived last night and anchored in Dowloon Bay. Mails from the U.S.A., Honolulu, Japan and Shanghai are still aboard.

## [16] LOCAL ITEMS.

Although Hongkong may count itself fortunate in that it has escaped the main force of the typhoon, the high wind and rain that has fallen to our portion as its "backwash" has undone much of the work of repair that was in hand in connection with the clearances of roads. The extent of this damage it is impossible to state at the moment owing to the difficulty of survey work.
Also, it may be added, it is too soon to assume that Hongkong is definitely clear of the typhoon but the only chance of our being involved in its train is in the case of a very definite turn.

## [17] Early Ferries.

<sub>section under [16] LOCAL ITEMS.</sub>

Although during the period in which the No. 6 signal was up the red flag (signifying that ferries might stop at any minute) was visible at the "Star" ferry wharf the ferries continued running until shortly after 9 a.m. although considerable difficulty was experienced in effecting a landing. One ferry, in fact, after bringing passengers across the harbour from Kowloon had to turn back after making unavailing efforts to get near enough the wharf to render safe the lowering of the gangway.

The last ferry to discharge passengers on the Hongkong side successfully accomplished the feat at 9.10 but without lowering the gangway. The pier head was used as a fulcrum for bringing the boat alongside and it was then possible for passengers to jump ashore. The very heavy seas running caused the boats to roll a great deal in the course of the passage across and difficulty was experienced in making the wharf. The set of the current took several of the boats in the direction of Queen's pier but they were all able to make up leeway.
The ferries re-commenced running shortly after 1 p.m.

The waves broke over the Prayer occasionally in the central districts but this was more noticeable in the West Point area where accumulations of debris have been flung ashore. A human body afloat was plainly visible near the Pottinger wharf.
