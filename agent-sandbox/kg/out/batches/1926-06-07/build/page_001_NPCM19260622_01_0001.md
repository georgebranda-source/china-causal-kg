## [7] PEACE PROSPECTS.

**IMPORTANT PRELIMINARY CONVERSATIONS.**

**WILL THERE BE TERMS?**

Optimism In Canton And Hongkong.

*Canton, June 21.*

Mr. Eugene Chen, the acting Minister for Foreign Affairs, has again sent a representative to Shameen to discuss with the British Consul-General the question of a settlement of the "strike."—Wah-Tsz Yat-Po. (The word "strike," in Chinese is always used in relation to the boycott and really includes both the strike and boycott).

The Hongkong delegation has not yet proceeded to Canton. In well-informed circles, it is thought that matters of a preliminary nature will be unofficially discussed before the actual conference opens. Such conversations may relate, or may not, to the more important issues, such as the basis of settlement.

Even the. Canton merchants, who are in close touch with the politicians, cannot indicate whether the terms—as announced some months ago—will be stipulated, whether they will be put forward with modifications, or if a new set will be drafted. There is still a hopeful spirit.

"Basis of Understanding."

Obviously, the preliminary negotiations will have considerable bearing on the prospects of a settlement being arrived at. It is thought that if a basis of understanding can be arrived at, the success of the conference is assured, but developments are likely to hinge on the unofficial conversations.

Apparently, this last move by Canton cannot be associated with previous peace efforts. On June 5, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the Kwangtung (Nationalist). Government wrote to the Hongkong Government asking for delegates to be appointed to negotiate. On June 16, Mr. J. F. Brenan (H.M.'s Consul-General at Canton) replied on behalf of the Hongkong Government. The reply was to the effect that the Government of Hongkong considered the "industrial strike" to be a thing of the past, but delegates would be appointed to negotiate for a termination of the boycott against British goods. The Consul-General is also quoted as having expressed pleasure at the proposals (of Carton).

The letter from the Hongkong Government to the Consul-General, dated June 14, is mentioned as asking for information as to when the negotiations will begin. Incidentally, Mr. Brenan was informed that H.M.'s Minister at Peking had nominated him as one of the delegates. Accordingly, the Governor of Hongkong appointed the Hon. Mr. J. H. Kemp and the Hon. Mr. E. R. Halifax as the other two delegates.

There is still a feeling of optimism in the Colony that at least a “temporary settlement”—such including the calling off of the pickets and the automatic resumption of communications and trade—will be brought about, so that a permanent understanding can then be worked for. Those who express such a view point to the reported arrest of members of the Strikers' Tribunal by the Canton Government, and the absence of news of their release or resumption of office. On the other hand, the “outdoor” pickets (i.e., “strike” representatives not in executive capacities) are still functioning with customary vigilance.

## [9] PIRATE'S METHODS WITH

**TERRIBLE VENDETTA**

A terrible revenge is reported to have been taken by a pirate, in the Canton River delta, on strike pickets who incurred his enmity. This pirate chief is alleged to have cut off the left ear of over seventy strikers who fell into his hands, and to have put several to death.

Yuen Kung is the name of the pirate. His den was at Hoi Chow, in the Heungshan district. This outlaw, it is stated, had taken part in the shipping of so-called contraband (foreign goods) through the pickets' cordons. Striet vigilance had been maintained against his boats and bad blood resulted.

Some days ago, Yuen fitted out a decoy in the shape of several large cargo-boats, seemingly sailing in convoy, but really in ballast.
This fleet passed a place in the Heungshan waterways, where the pickets had a station. When the strikers went out to inspect the boats, the pirates pounced on them and carried them off to their stronghold at Hoi Chow. Several pickers were shot. Every one of the others had the left ear out off.

Those who escaped returned to Canton to tell the story. On the application of the strike executive committee, a military punitive expedition was sent against Yuen but he had already deserted his den and had gone into the Shuntak silk district when the troops arrived.

## [12] OUR RENTS.

**K.R.A. REFERENDUM FOR BOARD.**

**PETITION PENDING**

When the Kowloon 'Residents' Association circulated amongst its members, a questionnaire with regard to opinions on the desirability or otherwise of continuing restriction of rents, there was also a question as to whether, in the event of the Government withdrawing restriction (as they now definitely have done), residents favoured the institution of a Fair Rents Board.

In view of the lapsing of the Rents-Restriction Ordinance at the end of this month, a “China Mail” representative made enquiries this morning as to whether the question of the Fair Rents Board would be pressed by the Kowloon Residents’ Association. He was informed that although no meeting had yet been held on the question, on the strength of the referendum the Association would be petitioning the Government for the institution of such a Board.

On the general rents question, it is interesting to note that Sir Shou-son Chow and Dr. R. H. Kotewall, Chinese members of the Legislative Council, have jointly issued a letter to the Chinese landlords advising them to be fair to their tenants when the restriction Ordinances lapses.

## [14] SUN AND SALT REVENUE

Peking, June 15.
The Cabinet announces receipt of a telegram from Marshal Sun Chuan-fang stating that "he is willing to release certain Kiangsu salt revenues.

The position is that by the 1925 agreement the Salt Administration should collect all the Kiangsu salt revenues and pay Marshal Sun 50 per cent., but last April Marshal Sun forbade transfer of the salt revenues to Shanghai. Dr. W. W. Yen recently telegraphed to the Marshal requesting him to release the salt funds and the Marshal informed the Cabinet that he was willing to do so as regards collections to the end of May. He emphasised that his action was largely a matter of personal friendship for Dr. Yen and clearly stated that the question of Peking drawing the salt funds for periods after May must be referred to him.

The transfer of the salt funds from the collectorates to Shanghai will take about ten days and, therefore, no funds have yet reached the group banks at Shanghai. The period involved will be less than two months and the usual 50 per cent. will be returned to Marshal Sun. — Reuter.

## [15] LOCAL RENTALS.

**IS A "FAIR RENTS BOARD" NECESSARY.**

**TENANT'S PLEA.**

(To the Editor of the China Mail.)

Sir,—Not every house owner in this Colony is a bona fide landlord who buys property for investment. There are some who still hold lots of property because they were “caught” when the slump came. It would only be human if they were to exploit the situation, when the Rents Ordinance expires on June 30. Their natural course would be to threaten demolition, or give a month's notice to quit, or increase the rentals as much as possible—and then try to sell out on the artificially inflated value.

Then again there are landlords who are very bitter over what has transpired during the last few years. I admit that in the majority of cases, tenants were to blame for tactless handling. More than one owner have indicated that they will serve notice if only to get their revenge back because silly tenants once flaunted the Ordinance in their faces.

To protect the innocent, it has been submitted that Fair Rents Board should be instituted. With all due respect I predict that the agitation for such a body will become intolerable. A rents tribunal has worked satisfactorily in other colonies and should do so here. There ought to be no hardship for owners, as each case will be dealt with on individual merits. Landlords will still be at liberty to throw occupants out, to demolish and reconstruct, to raise rents, to re-enter into possession themselves and there need be no appeal from them. A tenant who feels that he is being exploited can obtain justice. Values are fixed for assessment of rates and a tribunal can take the Government figures for guidance, instead of wasting time in hearing evidence and arguments. Capital will not be scared away—for fear of alleged restriction on legitimate enterprise—as fair returns will be granted by a Rents Board. The name itself assures both sides of a square deal and not a one-sided ruling.

In conclusion I would, Sir, point out that the sudden abandonment of all restriction will tend to the opposite extreme. I anticipate the landlords' reply to this—that ample notice has been given by the Government. Just as it was claimed when the Ordinance suddenly came into force, that landlords were unduly penalised, so now they will make—not in every instance—unwarranted profits of those who must rent houses. Control came in easy stages—as witness the many amendments to the Rents Ordinance. Decontrol should, therefore, come also in easy stages. The best answer to that, and the greatest good for the greatest number, lies in the immediate establishment of a Rents Board.

Should the Government not lend ear, I advise every tenant to give full publicity to facts, as soon as he receives that nightmare of a letter from the landlord.

Yours, etc.,
FLAT DWELLER.
Hongkong, June 22, 1926.
