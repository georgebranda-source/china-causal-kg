## [7] COAL CRISIS.

Any hope of a truce in the coal crisis at Home appears to have gone with the unanimous decision of the Miners' Executive to resist increased hours and reduced wages. It may be the turn of Mr. Frank Hodges to revive the scheme put forward some time ago for a solution of the troubles. Mr. Hodges, it will be recalled, resigned the Secretaryship of the Miners' Federation on receiving a post in Mr. MacDonald's Ministry, and, unlike other Trade Union leaders, did not regain his position. Mr. A. J. Cook, a Communist, was elected in his stead, as many think, much to the detriment of the miners. Mr. Hodges recognises that the problem of coal is international, and that therefore, international organisation is indispensable, but he adds that internal efficiency must also be obtained in British mines. Elimination of waste must be enforced, unproductive methods at the pit rigorously dealt with, and unification in the production of power for colliery uses secured. He favours the amalgamation into single concerns of all collieries in definite geological areas, a reduction in transport costs and a new method of coal distribution, which would eliminate the onerous charges of middle-men, factors, merchants and retailers. Incidentally, such a method might be applied with advantage to the handling of foodstuffs and other domestic requirements, which increase so alarmingly in price in their passage from the producer to the consumer. But apparently Mr. Hodges's scheme is merely that municipalities should be vested with power to buy coal from the collieries and distribute it retail—a form of State trading which would be opposed by many. In any case this part of the scheme has been definitely turned down meantime so far as Government legislation is concerned. ## [11] “ KINGSCLERE.”

**PART OF BUILDING SOLD YESTERDAY.**

**ALSO YAUMATI HOUSES.**

Part of the site of Kingsclere changed, hands yesterday when that portion of the ground situated at Macdonnell Road was put up for auction at Mr. A. G. da Rocha's sale rooms. It is registered as Section S. of Inland Lot No. 1381, together with the buildings erected on it.

Starting at $8,000, the price rose to $10,100 at which price it was purchased by the representative of the Sum Kwong Hop Co.

Eleven houses at Yaumati were also disposed of yesterday for alightly over $3,000 each. They were put up at the same time with an upset price of $35,000. One advance of $800 was made at which price they became the property of Mr. Fok Pak-yui.

The respective lots consisted of (1) All that piece of ground-situated at Yaumati, Kowloon and registered in the Land Office as Section B of Kowloon Inland Lot No. 760 together with the five buildings erected thereon known as Nos. 12, 14, 16, 18 and 20 Hamilton Street, Yaumati; (2) All that parcel of ground-situated at Yaumati aforesaid and known in the Land Office as The Remaining Portion of Kowloon Inland Lot No. 760 together with the six buildings thereon known as Nos. 74, 76, 78, 80, 82 and 84 Portland Street, Yaumati, the combined areas being 9,091 square feet or thereabouts.

## [12] OPIUM SMOKERS.

**PRIEST AND CONSTABLE IN A DIYAN.**

**POLICEMAN REBUKED.**

In imposing a fine of $2 for smoking opium in a divan and $15 for misconduct as a police officer, the Kowloon Magistrate told the defendant, a Shantung constable, that he was disgracing the police force by keeping company with such men.

Another unusual visitor who was found in the divan was a Chinese priest, but there being no direct evidence against him he was discharged.

Other defendants arrested were fined $2 each, while the two keepers had to pay $30 each.

## [13] COMPANY MEETING.

**EXCAVATION, PILE DRIVING & CONSTRUCTION CO.**

**20 PER CENT: DIVIDEND.**

The 5th annual meeting of the Hongkong Excavation, Pile Driving & Construction Co., Ltd., was held at their registered office, Powell's Building, yesterday afternoon.

Those present were: Mr. B. Montague Edo (chairman), Mr. Charles L. Shank, Mr. Chan Pok-chun, Mr. Edward D. Shank, Mr. Carl B. Shank and Mr. Chau Yueteng (directors).

## [14] Tribute to Late Mr. Ede.

<sub>section under [13] COMPANY MEETING.</sub>

After the convening notice had been read, Mr Carl B. Shank (who is also manager of the company) said:—

"Before commencing the formal business of this meeting, I take this occasion, in view of the relationship existing between our former chairman and our present one, to refer with deep sorrow to the death of the Hon. Mr. C. Montague Ede.

"As you are all aware, Mr. C. Montague Ede was, up to the time of his decease, the chairman of this company and for several years it has been supporter, and a large part of the success the company has enjoyed during the last few years is due to the beneficial services rendered by him. The late Mr. Ede's public activities are so well known to you all that they need not be mentioned here.

"The company is, however, in no small degree compensated for such a great loss by its good fortune in having the late Mr. Ede's son as its present chairman and it is most sincerely hoped that he will remain permanently in the Colony to carry on the work started by his father. The sympathy of this meeting is extended to the bereaved family."

## [17] Kowloon Tong Burden.

<sub>section under [13] COMPANY MEETING.</sub>

"The contract with the Kowloon Tong Co. has proved to be a heavy burden on the company during the year. A great many subscribers, for one reason or another, have found it inconvenient to pay the calls on their houses as they became due and in consequence early in the year the funds of the Kowloon Tong Co. became depleted. When certain materials became exhausted, and the Kowloon Tong

Co. could no longer promptly meet labour payments, our company, as the general labour contractors for the scheme, had one of two alternatives, either to carry on the work in progress or to completely shut down the work. To close down work on a scheme as large as the Kowloon Tong project is quite a serious matter both for the clients and for the contractors and would cause a substantial financial loss to all concerned. After much consideration, at the request of the directors of the Kowloon Tong Co., it was decided that we carry the necessary burden to keep the work in progress and the result has been that up to the present moment our company has a substantial part of its funds invested in the scheme.

## [18] Beacon Hill Reservoir.

<sub>section under [13] COMPANY MEETING.</sub>

"A contract was secured by the company on a competitive basis for building the Beacon Hill reservoir, which is to supply water to the Kowloon Tong estate. This reservoir has long been completed, but no payment in pursuance to this work has yet been made. In addition to these difficulties, the Government who are doing the levelling of the site are long overdue on the agreed time for completing this work, which has caused the general construction work, even before the strike, to be carried on in a piece-meal, and uneconomical manner. This, of course, has absorbed the profit on the general labour contract which would have been available had the original scheme for mass production been carried out. A good many of the subscribers now realise the great assistance this company has been to the whole scheme and many are doing their best to meet their obligations promptly. It is to be hoped that this attitude will continue as your directors feel that without the co-operation of subscribers, and prompt fulfilment of contract on the part of the Kowloon Tong Co., this company is no longer justified in nursing the Kowloon Tong scheme financially as it has done in the past."

I cannot see, I cannot roam,
I cannot smell the air of home.
No more for me the wood and green,
Or walking through the squire's demesne
Where o'er a thousand sights present
Themselves to eyes that don't resent;
Where, under groves and shades as low
And very branches that I know.
My childhood sweet had been unfold.
And boyish mem'ries, since grown old,
Come back to me of schooldays spent
In the one school where aged, bent,
The learned master dignified
Presider with rule that denied
From anyone the slightest say
In all affairs, He's had his day—
And now he sleeps, to a cruel surmise,
A sleep from which he cannot rise.

As if I saw it standing there
In the glorious sunshine, fair.
I see it standing nobly up,
Drinking whate'er is in the cup;
I breathe its peaceful atmosphere,
And doing so, I will not dare
To ask why thus she is content,
For know now I for this is meant.
I see the curling streaks of smoke,
That love more rather than provoke;
I see the herdamen tend their flocks;
I hear the clarion of the cocks;
I see the everyday of all,
And Sunday hear the church-bells call.
There, pious the minister stands,
Proclaiming God to English lands;
I see my parents in the pew,
Deaf to ideas and teachings new,
Most faithful to their God on high,
And flinch not—oh, exalt the sky!
I see my sister in the choir,
Giving praise to th' Eternal Sire.
And then, I see the homestead sweet,
Where, under frost and under heat,
Through many seasons gone was I:
How now I view it with a sigh!
The service ends, my people come,
And following them I hear the hum,
Still, of their thanks, reechoing,
And prayers for their distant offspring
How I love them I cannot tell,
Though they be heaven'n and I be hell;
But they are blood of mine, and blood
Doth link kin in the coming flood.
They pass the gate, the door is open,
Then enter hearts all full of hope.
I try to enter, but cannot—
How helpless hands stretched out, forgo
That miles of land and leagues of sea

And in a paradise to be,

And tells me England loved of yore.
