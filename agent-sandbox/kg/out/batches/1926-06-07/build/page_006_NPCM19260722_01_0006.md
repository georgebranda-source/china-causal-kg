Poincaré, if he succeeds in forming a Cabinet. be able to do any better than M. Herriot or M. Briand? Will his Finance Minister be greater than a de Monzie, Peret. Painleve, Clemente, or Caillaux?

We can rebind it for you in any style you desire at a moderate charge. Making it look like new—but yet preserving the inscripted fly-leaf so dear to book-lovers.

In spite of the serious nature of the crisis the public of Paris booed M. Herriot and cheered the news of his downfall. Salvation or no salvation of the franc, he was simply not wanted by the populace, even the Socialists withholding support that might have tidied over the crisis. Meanwhile France is getting plenty of sympathy as every one in financial sloughs does—but there it rests.

## [13] CAFE INCIDENT.

**MANAGER ACCOUNTS FOR HIS CONDITION.**

**TEMPORARILY INSANE**

Further light was thrown at the Central Magistracy yesterday in regard to the dispute between Mr. L. G. Taylor and Mr. J. L. Durand, manager and director respectively of the Cafe Parisien in Pedder Building, "on July 14, the French national day.
Both were charged with disorderly conduct, while Mr. and Mrs. Taylor brought other charges against Mr. Durand for assault and using abusive language. Mr. Durand was defended by Mr. Hugh Jones and Mr. Taylor conducted his own case.

Sergeant Haywood gave evidence of being present near the Cafe shortly after midnight when he had to speak to a number of Europeans who were making a noise as they were leaving the Cafe. Inside the Cafe, witness said, he saw Mrs. Taylor and Mr. Durand arguing with each other and there appeared to be some pushing between them. Mr. Durand then went upstairs accompanied by another lady.

After he had left, Taylor appeared from the corner of Peddar Street. He approached the witness and spoke to him about two sailors. He was not excited nor intoxicated, but his breath smelt strongly of alcohol. His wife then appeared on the Cafe steps and shouted to him.

Witness asked permission to refer to his note book, where he had written down Mrs. Taylor's exact words. Reading from his book witness said that the words used by Mrs. Taylor to her husband were "all yourself a man, to let him call your wife a-fool?"

Some five or ten minutes later, Durand emerged from the side of the cafe, presumably having come down the stairs. His hand was streaming with blood, and he was closely followed by Taylor. When they got into Pedder Street they started to fight, but witness, who was about 15 yards away, intervened and separated the two men, seizing Durand and pulling him away.

Mrs. Taylor appeared to be pulling her husband away. Witness was leading Durand towards the cafe when he was struck in the stomach. They were both using abusive language, Taylor accusing Durand of having assaulted his wife and Durand accusing Taylor of assaulting him.

" Witness blew his whistle, and an Indian sergeant arrived on the scene. With his assistance Durand was put into a chair, and taken to the Central Police Station. Witness then returned for Taylor, who was lying on the road outside the cafe.

He was being held by some people and appeared as if insane. With the assistance of Mr. C. D. Robertson, witness lifted Taylor and took him to Wyndham Street, where he was also put into a chair and taken to the Police Station.

Replying to a question by Mr. Hugh Jones, witness said that all the parties were excited and appeared to smell strongly of liquor.
Mr. C. D. Robertson gave evidence of being attracted to the scene by hearing police whistles blown. "Witness saw Sergeant Haywood struggling with Taylor and he assisted to put Taylor into a chair.

The police produced a medical certificate showing that when Taylor was examined at 3 a.m. on July 15, his condition was normal. Mr Taylor had then explained that he had lost his reason, and on the advice of Capt. Bloxham he went to the prison hospital and was examined. By that time he had fully regained his senses and was described as being normal.

Giving evidence from the witness box, Mr. Taylor said that from the commencement he had two very excitable people to deal with (Mr. Durand and Madame Flint). The first night was a very big one for the staff and after everyone had gone witness, his wife, and Durand had supper together. The latter got very excited because witness had allowed some of the customers to sign chits.

Witness said that he worked continually for 16 hours a day and sometimes for 20 hours, and on the 14th he had nothing to eat until 3.30 in the afternoon. On that day he had altogether six drinks.

Witness then referred to a visit by two sailors who went away quietly when they were told that they could not come in. Later, after the national anthems had been played and everything was over, witness saw the sailors in the lobby and he showed them out. It was then that Mrs. Taylor rushed up to witness and

complained about being insulted by Mr. Durand.

Witness said that he immediately ran upstairs in search of Mr. Durand and struck him with a chair. He remembered Durand falling through a glass show case. Witness said that from this time he had a poor recollection of what happened. Durand rushed out, and witness followed him. After Durand turned down the stairs witness did not see him again. He could not say what occurred in the street.

The following day he recollected imploring the police to arrest him outside the cafe, and also imploring Capt. Bloxham to put him in jail, but why he did not know.

## [15] RELEASED.

**TROOPS WITHDRAWN.**

The European officers and crew of the Police motor boat which was taken by pickets and the personnel into their custody were relensed yesterday evening.

Mr. W. G. Gerrard, Assistant Superintendent of Police, accompanied by Mr. A. E. Wood, Mr. E. H. Williams and Sub-Inspector Hoare of Sheung Shui, went over the border yesterday afternoon and conferred with picket leaders. The conference lasted three hours and at the end of it an announcement was made that the officers and crew of the launch would be released and that no hindrance would be put in the way of the removal of the grounded boat. The captured men accompanied the members of the delegation back to the border.

A party of 100 men of the East Surrey Regiment despatched to the scene were then withdrawn.
The captured men suffered no discomfort except that they slept on a concrete floor in wet clothes. They were given to understand that plans had been made for their removal to Canton.

## [20] MR. W. BLAKEY.

Owing to the non-arrival of the hearse and to the friends who eventually carried the body on a handbier taking the Stubbs Road route and finding access by the upper level impossible, the funeral of Mr. W. Blakey, which was timed to take place at 5 p.m., did not commence until darkness was falling.
