## [13] UNCLEAN CLOSETS.

**MESSRS. SHEWAN, TOMES & CO. FINEID $10.**

**MAGISTRATE'S DECISION..**

Major C. Willson (at the Central Magistracy) this morning imposed a fine of $10 on Messrs. Shewan, Tomes & Co., at the resumed hearing of the summons by Sanitary Inspector J. Stirton, for the alleged unclean closets in St. George's Building.

At the previous hearing, Mr. R. A. Wadeson (for the defence) had argued that Messrs. Shewan, Tomes & Co. were neither the owners, nor the occupiers.

The prosecution produced publications in which the firm was given as general managers of the Hongkong and Territorial Estates, Ltd. (admitted to be owners).

When His Worship announced that he was against Mr. Wadeson on the point raised and that he was, inter alia, of the opinion that the defendant firm were the most suitable persons to be considered agents, Mr. Wadeson requested His Worship to note two items: the second of which was that the name of the owner had been disclosed, the owner's representative had been in court, and had not been proceeded against Mr. Wadeson's other contention was that under the Ordinance, either the owner or occupier was liable and his clients could not be considered either owner or occupier.

## [14] A MARKET SCENE.

**INSPECTOR ATTACKED BY HAWKERS.**

**SQUELI**

Am attack by Hawkers on Sanuary Inspector John Hughes was described by Mr. R. E. Lindsell's Court this morning when a Chinese looked food stall man was charged with disorderly conduct.

The Inspector stated in evidence that he was set upon by a number of hawkers when he went to clear them from the market place at Jardine's bazaar yesterday afternoon and had seized a basket containing vegetable. He produced a knife which he smatched away from one of the assailants and accused the defendant of having urged on the hawkers to attack witness with the stools at the stall. "Witness said he was forced to beat a retreat and summon police assistance and when he returned the assailants had dispersed with the exception of the defendant."

Defendant stated that he was doing all he could to prevent the hawkers from getting the stools at his stall. He denied that he took part in the attack. The master of the stall gave corroborating evidence and defendant was discharged.

## [22] "WILD STORIES."

**BOLSHEVIST PLANS IN SHANGHAI.**

**PHANTOM OF THE BRAIN.**

(From Our Own Correspondent):

Whilst the recent threats of disturbance in Shanghai, arising out of the shooting of students in Peking, came to nothing, save one or two orderly demonstrations, there is still evidence that Shanghai must be prepared for some trouble in the next few months. Whilst the reverse of Marshal Feng Yu-hsiang in the North, and the coup d'etat of General Chang Kaishek in Canton may be taken to constitute serious reverses for the Bolshevik policy in China, it has yet to be shown that the local representatives of the Soviets are relaxing in their endeavours to create disorder. There has been a number of strikes in the industrial districts, called on the filmsiest of pretexts. Particularly is that the case in connection with the cotton milds, where, fortunately for the owners, surplus stocks would enable them to shut down for a few weeks in the present state of trade depression, did they desire. This undoubtedly proved a strong weapon in their hands, for they would have been able to face a lock out with no little equanimity. Possibly this was recognised by the agitators behind the strikes for it is a significant fact that none of them lasted any appreciable length of time.

It has been suggested that these were more or less in the nature of ballons d'essai to discover the strength of the labour leaders in Shanghai; but this is probably incorrect. If it is not, then the labour leaders have nothing at the present moment of which to be proud.

In the meantime no little anxiety and a great deal of what is nothing about of the man's has been created by the wild stories of what the Bolsheviks intend to do in Shanghai Curing the next three years. That the Communists have some plans for Shanghai goes without saying. They regard Shanghai as the centre of British influence in China, and a place where a very shrewd blow could be struck which, if successful, would go a long way toward damaging British prestige and business. Indeed in a recent Communist speech Shanghai was spoken of as an important place from the Communist point of view. Whilst the authorities doubtless have a very fair idea of what the local Bolsheviks is planning—for the intelligence service in Shanghai is of a high order of efficiency—all manner of alarmist reports gain currency to the disquiet of the ordinary person in the street, until the "North China Daily News" rightly thought it necessary to do something to allay anxiety by a judiciously worded editorial.

The wildest story which gained currency was that on a night to be selected the Bolsheviks would rise and seize the city. They would detail men to the houses of all the Volunteers, Police, and members of the various defence forces, and overpower them during the night; seize the women and children and place them in the buildings along the Bund to prevent the warships firing into the Settlement; and then hand the place over to sack. Quite a pretty little scheme, if it could be worked

But there seem to be many objections to such a plan even from the Bolshevik point of view, which is the most important, for the foreign opinion on the subject can so well be imagined that it needs no description here. The other foreigners strongly disapprove of the idea, and possibly if any such attempt were made would forcibly express their opinions on the matter. When we come to consider the large number of Bolsheviks that would be needed to pick off every individual member of the defence forces, it becomes apparent that they would need a force of some thousands of men, and the maintenance of secrecy amongst such a body would be impossible. It is obvious that if the authorities obtained the slightest inkling of the plan, their precautions would be made accordingly. Then, again, there is little to be got out of it, except loot, and whilst Shanghai would be an attractive place to pursue so engrossing a pastime, it is more than likely that the Chinese merchants themselves, with large interests at stake, would be the first to disapprove. Nor is it possible to

overlook the contention that the Bolsheviks would gain little else from it, for doubtless the despatch of adequate foreign forces would soon rescue the city from their hands, leaving them with no permanent political gain.

Arrayed against the Bolsheviks there are at present the Copstitutional Defence League, and a Chinese anti-Bolshevik League, both organisations capable of doing useful work, with, however, this limitation, that a propaganda of discontent is easier to preach than one to the contrary. Possibly, therefore, they may both find their work more difficult than does the Communist, but provided with sufficient money they may find means, not perhaps of averting trouble, but of minimising it when it does come.

Meanwhile we in Shanghai are watching the way the wind blows as the fated month of May draws near. There may, of course, be trouble, but believing it to be true that a watched pot never boils, your correspondent is not disinlined to believe that the month will pass off much more quietly than many at present anticipate. If it does not, possibly the opportunity of giving the Bolsheviks comrade as shrewd a crack as he has received in the North and South would not come amiss, though the failure of his plans in those localities may cause him to walk a little more circumspectly up here.

## [23] WARSHIP'S CHASE.

**CANTON'S WINDFALL OF**

**MYSTERIOUS ERRANDS.**

Suspected of being on an errand of mystery, two of the long rowing boats peculiar to the "Hoklo (Swatow and district) seafarers, known in the vernacular as the "big eyed chicken" (because of the two glaring eyes painted on the bow), have been captured by a Canton warship, which discovered a very large quantity of arms and ammunition.

The gunboat "Kwong" Kum" was on patrol, under orders from the "Canton Government," in the Ping Hot district (which is in the Waichow prefecture). A signal to these two speedy boats to stop was ignored. A round was fired across their bows and a dinghy was put off.

Before the armed posse could catch up, those on board the row-boats had taken to the sea. In the boats was found a large stock of weapons, including Mausers, and automatics, this being taken back to Canton.

## [24] TEST TRIP.

**BOYCOTT ENDING IN AMOY?**

**B. AND S. CARGO LANDED.**

News from Amoy during the holidays is that the first British steamer to arrive there with cargo, since the boycott pickets began activities, has been discharged.

The matter has been the main topic of conversation for some days and, in the opinion of the Chinese merchants, normal conditions are returning at that point.

It was the China Navigation S.S. "Ichang" which put in with a cargo of beancake. Hitherto cargo in British bottoms had not been discharged nor loaded. Passengers, however, had been land-ed or permitted to embark, the majority being in connection with the coolie transport for southern ports.

On the first day, nothing was done. Then the merchants are believed to have pointed out that the cargo was a Chinese product, from a Chinese port, and for Chinese consumption. Friendly influences prevailed and the cargo was landed.

[Amoy is further North along the coast (from Hongkong)] than Swatow. It is in the province of Fukien and not a part of Kwangtung. The present rulers of Fukien province cannot be described as antagonistic to, nor actually in sympathy with, the Kuomintang of Canton.]
