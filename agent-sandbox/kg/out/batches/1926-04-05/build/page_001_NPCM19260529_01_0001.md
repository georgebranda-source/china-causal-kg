The increase in Bank interest is naturally accounted for by the fact that due to the financial stringency in the third quarter of 1925, our capital issue, in common with that of other concerns, did not meet with the support that under normal conditions would have been forthcoming. Turning to the Balance Sheet you will observe that the items "Land" and "Buildings" have increased by $2,820,000 which is in the main accounted for by the acquisition by the Company on favourable terms of the reversionary interest in the Palace Hotel, Shanghai (to which reference was made in the speech from this Chair last year). The year which has passed since our last General Meeting has been, as you are probably only too well aware, one presenting unprecedented difficulties to practically every business concern of this Colony and it would be superfluous, therefore, for me to labour this point. The unsettled situation created by the disturbances of the month of May resulted in a serious set-back in our turnover in the Northern port but Shanghai recovered quickly, as reflected by our revenue figures which, I am pleased to say, she won an appreciable increase in most departments; and, whilst on this point, it appears to me opportunity to emphasise that the action of your Company in acquiring the Hotels in Shanghai has proved a source of very considerable strength to the Company and is more than justified by the results, for since the amalgamation effected with The Shanghai Hotels, Ltd. at the end of 1923, the Company has been placed in a position where- under it is not nearly so vulnerable as would be the case if its interests were concentrated in one place.
In Hongkong, as the employer of a large staff, the Company was naturally seriously affected by the strike which occurred towards the end of the second quarter of last year, nevertheless, I am pleased to state that we were enabled to carry on the essential services of our establishments; and in fact the Hongkong Hotel, due to its central position, was called upon to supply more meals per day than would have been the case in normal times — although I must add, en passant, that little or no profit accrued to the Company in this respect, our primary object, as a semi-public utility undertaking having been to meet the needs of the community at rates as reasonable as possible in the exceptional circumstances then obtaining. Towards the end of the year the new native staff which had been secured had to some extent been trained in the requirements of our various departments, and generally speaking, the business of our hotels in Hongkong was running smoothly.

## [11] *(no headline: column 4)*

The death is announced of Sir James Cantlie, K.B.E.

Sir James Cantlie, the distinguished surgeon and medical man, had many associations with Hongkong and the Far East.

It was in 1887 that he first came to China in connection with tropical medicine work, and founded here the College of Medicine for Chinese, this being prior to the institution of our University and its faculty of medicine. Of this college of medicine he was, the Dean. Sir James whilst in Hongkong, was associated with what is now the medical firm of Drs. Harston, Black, Balean, etc.

Sir James Cantlie was a believer in Dr. Sun Yat-sen and proved a friend in need to the latter on an occasion when Dr. Sun was in captivity in the Chinese Legation, London. Sir James, through the British Government, obtained Sun's release.

In addition to having held high positions in Britain in connection with medicine and hygiene, Sir James served with a special cholera expedition despatched to Egypt, had acted as plague officer and consulting surgeon to the L.C.C. and had many medical science publications to his name. One of his last remarks which received publicity in the London Press was with reference to broad-brimmed hats.

"I have been advocating the big hat for ladies for forty years," he was reported to have said. "And if we have a hot summer women will find what a friend may have in the old-fashioned straw hat. These narrow brimless hats which allow the sun's rays to fall on the back of the neck are a great peril."

A third official head, is soon to fall, for official announcement will soon be made of the “resignation” of Lam Chu-ham, chairman of the Agriculture Department. This stepping out of office, which will follow the resignations of the heads of the organisation and propaganda departments, announced yesterday in the “China Mail,” will be the third definite result of the famous “eight resolutions” of General Chang Kai-shek which bar Communists from high places in the Government and from commissions higher than lieutenancies in the Nationalist Army.

Lam Chu-ham, it is understood, protests "with some vehemence that he is not really a Communist, but nevertheless he will soon have a successor.

Reports in the Press that Foo Ping-sheung, Commissioner for Foreign Affairs for Kwangtung province, may soon resign, are incorrect. He will not give up his portfolio at this time. Mr. Foo's resignation, had it been submitted, would have had no relationship to the Government's Communist house-cleaning.

## [16] DRUG ADDICT.

Drugs having been given him since he was a "babe" in arms," Joseph de Merrif, an American citizen, told his life story to Mr. R. E. Lindsell at the Magistracy this morning.

De Merritt, of medium stature and about middle age, was not legally represented. He pleaded guilty to possession of heroin and morphine, in excess of the maximum allowed by law, this being twelve official jobs.

When His Worship asked defendant how he came by the dope, the latter replied "Well, I bought it because I had the chance to." Continuing, he frankly admitted that he was addicted to drug taking.

Briefly, his story was that when he was an infant, he had rash and the only person who could keep him quiet was a Chinese amah: When he was about 5 or 6, his family discovered the Chinese nurse's secret—she put opium and poppy juice in his milk. He had had trouble all his life, as a result of taking drugs being always dazed, dizzy and worried.

When he was 8 or 9, Ah Sing—whoever that may have been—taught him how to use the opium pipe, he then taking an occasional puff or two and covered his mouth with a handkerchief. This continued for a couple of years or so, but he broke the habit. When about 22 or 23, he had a terrible accident, the scars of which he bore to this day. His skull was fractured and he was in bed for 28 months. Very properly, he commented, the physicians put him on heroin and morphine. It brought relief from the pain he suffered, but it "resopened his old sores." Since then, the drugs had been regularly prescribed for him. When in Japan he had dysentery and he used drugs.
