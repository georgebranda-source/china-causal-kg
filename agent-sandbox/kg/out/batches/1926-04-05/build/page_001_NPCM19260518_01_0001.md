## [5] CHEAP “REVOLT.”

**COST OF GENERAL STRIKE TO BRITAIN.**

**CHANCELLOR'S DISCLOSURE**

In the House of Commons, in reply to questions. Mr. Winston Churchill, Chancellor of the Exchequer, said that he anticipated that the direct Government net expenditure as a result of the strike will not exceed £750,000 and may be less. The direct Government expenditure would probably not be large, whilst there would be countervailing receipts.

Moreover, the strike had resulted in savings in normal expenditure in some cases—for example, in practice flying and also at Woolwich Arsenal.

As regards revenue there were increased Customs clearances before the strike to set off against reduction during the strike period. The effect upon direct taxation would mainly appear in next year's Estimates.

Any loss in profits would be made up by increased trade activity in the interval, and, assuming that the coal stoppage was not prolonged, he did not anticipate an appreciable disturbance in revenue in the current year. He saw no reason at present to propose additional taxation. (Cheers).

Mr. F. A. Macquisten (Conservative, Angyll): interposed: "Isn't this the cheapest attempt at revolution that has ever been?" (Laughter).

The Miners Federation has appealed to "all those desiring peace in the mining industry to prove their sincerity by helping us to relieve the suffering, and thus create a good spirit among the mining community".

They say that a million miners are locked out—thus five million human beings are suffering.
“We want peace in our industry and will work for a speedy satisfactory settlement to ensure that once again Britain's basic industry will be running harmoniously.”
Truth Will Out.

Plymouth, May 17.
Mr. C. T. Cramp, speaking here, said that the strike would probably cost them near £1,000,000 and the companies £5,500,000. He added that he did not believe that a general industrial strike could ever be carried out effectively because, if it were carried to its logical conclusion, they starved and paralysed themselves as well as everybody else.

The Railway Unions saved their lives by their action regarding the agreement, but possibly some Unions and workers in some industries would find it most difficult to return to pre-strike conditions.

Referring to the report that the Miners' Federation had received a cablegram from the Russian Miners' Congress offering a first instalment of £260,000, collected among the Russian Trade Unions to assist the wives and families of British Miners, Mr. A. J. Cook said that he appealed for funds to relieve distress from anywhere and anybody; and would certainly be pleased to accept money from the Russian mine workers.

## [6] EARLIER CABLES.

It has been agreed between the Bradford Dyers Association and the Dyeing operatives that 4,000 strikers will resume as it becomes practicable. The Association will begin a legal action against the Amalgamated Society of Dyers for breaches of contract.

If that Union consents in Court to a declaration that the 1914 Agreement is binding on the Association and the Union, then the Association will forgo all claims, except £100 damages and costs. The Association adds that it is entitled to claim £1 penalty against individual strikers, but does not intend to enforce this.

Mr. Varley, M.P., leader of the Notts miners, speaking in his constituency last night criticised the T.U.C. for the "dreadful debacle of the general strike." He never dreamed that the calling off of the strike would be assumed as a pos

tion of abject surrender. He had never been regarded as Red and he hoped the miners would now tell him to return to London to make the best terms possible for them.
Demobilisation of the emergency organisations to cope with the general strike is proceeding rapidly, but will take several days, owing to the huge accumulations of stores. For instance in Hyde Park, which has resembled an Army Headquarters, the postal sorting office (where the congestion is officially described as equal to ten Christmas seasons rolled into one) 1,200 tons of parcels were transferred yesterday from Mount Pleasant Office, London, to the railway terminal, where they will lie till the train service is sufficient to clear them. Hundreds of goods trains are in congestion in sidings. All the big towns rolling stocks must be overhauled. One of the greatest burdens the strike has borne is the insurance firms which are inundated with work while other businesses are stagnated. The liabilities assumed by underwriters are enormous, yet the cost of insurance remained most moderate.
LATEST PEACE GOSSIP

Labour demonstrations in London and the provinces yesterday were curiously subdued. Audiences in general were hostile to the speakers who moved resolutions reaffirming confidence in the T.U.C. The railwayman, Mr. Cramp, speaking at Plymouth, said the strike would probably cost the Union Railwaymen £1,000,000 and the companies £5,500,000.

**THEATRE DISPUTE.**

**PORTUGUESE LADIES IN COURT.**

**AN ALLEGED KICK.**

Picture Moves Young Lady To Tears.

Arising out of the exciting picture "Lost in a Big City" shown at the Star Theatre on April 25, several Portuguese ladies and gentlemen were present before Mr. J. H. B. Nihill at the Kowloon Magistracy yesterday to answer charges of assault and riotous behaviour.

It was alleged that the film so excited Miss Maria Sequeira that she kicked Mrs. Gill, wife of the Kowloon postmaster, who was seated in front. Further, that at the eloquent persuasion of the Misses Beatriz and Celeste Gill, who were seated with her, she was moved to tears, and as a reaction from weeping she commenced to laugh, which led Mrs. Gill to assume that she was being mocked. Subsequently it was alleged a "skirmish" took place in the lavatory in which Mr. Gill assaulted Miss Sequeira with an umbrella. A general melee was then stated to have ensued until Mr. A. A. Barros and the two Messrs. Prata arrived on the scene and proceeded to hold the parties back.

Mr. G. S. Hugh Jones appeared for the original complainants, Miss Sequeira and the Misses Gill. Mr. and Mrs. Gill who took out cross-summons were represented by Mr. M. K. Lo, while Mr. D. McCallum appeared for Mr. Barros and the two Pratas who were alleged to have intervened in the dispute.

During the outlining of the complainant's story by Mr. Hugh Jones, he said that the Misses Gill took no part in the fight excepting the time when one of them tried to separate Mr. and Mrs. Gill and Miss Sequeira. He pointed out that the two girls were nieces of Mr. and Mrs. Gill and it was common knowledge that there had been bad feeling between the parties for a long time. Barros and the Pratas were said to have had no part in this bad feeling. Mr. Hugh Jones submitted that the incident arose from the accidental kicking of the chair occupied by Mrs. Gill and that Mr. Gill deliberately went to the lavatory and assaulted the complainant.

Miss Sequeira, in evidence, denied that she kept on continuously kicking the chair or that she laughed at anything which reflected on either Mr. or Mrs. Gill. She further denied that Mrs. Gill had said: "You are not behaving like ladies," or that immediately after she got excited she struck Mrs. Gill. She admitted striking in self-defence.

The case was adjourned.

In responsible business circles, people are saying that, somebody who is able to speak with authority has accepted a bet, at even money, that the Canton boycott will terminate the end of this month, although normal con&tions may not be restored till several weeks later.

Given for what it is worth, the rumour now current is that this "bet" is indicative of the position, as Were Lloyd's rates for underwriting losses during the Home strike. The sum at stake is said to be very small, but those who have heard the story give details.

There will be no negotiation, or stipulated terms, they say, but the pickets will be quietly drawn off. Passengers and shippers will not be notified, those going up merely learning for themselves that the restrictions have been as suddenly removed as they were at first imposed. Notice will not then be taken of outgoing or incoming traffic, thereby allowing matters to revert to the former position.

Because of the threat of invasion by Marshal Wu Pei-fu, they say, the steps predicted are likely to be carried out.

It is evident that Canton will need revenue to wage war, whether strictly defensive, or to send an expedition North as projected, and it will be necessary to retain the goodwill of the merchants and civil population.

With this end in view, the influence of the greater majority of the merchants—that is, with the exception of the very few trades which are profiting by the boycott—will make itself felt. Coupled with the excesses and irregularities of the pickets, and the danger of their falling a prey to disorderly elements, the story may be true.

However, the "Wah Tsz Yat Po" publishes a message to-day that Mr. C. C. Wu is likely to be appointed Foreign Minister—a post he formerly held—with the view of bringing the boycott negotiations to a termination.

## [14] $40 DIVIDEND

A successful year in spite of difficulties was reported at the annual meeting to-day of the Canton Insurance Office, Ltd. The difficulties referred to at the meeting were those arising in the acquisition of business of a re-munerative character and the state of the London market, and consequent undercutting in marine Underwriting. A final and interim dividend totalling $40 is to be paid.

The Hon. Mr. D. G. M. Bernard presided over the meeting supported by Mr. A. S. Gubbay, Sir Robert Ho Tung, Mr. H. P. White, Mr. Henry Humphreys and Mr. F. C. ## [15] Chairman's Speech

The Chairman said: It is with great pleasure that your General Agents and Consulting Committee again present to you a very satisfactory Report, especially as I am unable to announce any amelioration in the position of Marine Insurance generally, although there are signs that Underwriters are now taking steps to try to improve existing conditions.

The most serious problem with which we have to contend is that of maintaining premium income. Business there is, but business from which a reasonable prospect of profit may be looked for is very hard to obtain. From agents in all parts of the world, we receive reports of the additional difficulties that continue daily to arise in the acquisition of business of a remunerative character: The Marine Insurance Market is still too big for the business available, and until there is a shrinkage in the former or a very considerable increase in the latter I can see no hope of any great improvement. It is only necessary to examine the Accounts of the leading British Marine Companies to perceive how great the fall of income has been during the past six years, the figures for 1925 being in many cases considerably less than half those for 1920. We may consider ourselves fortunate in this respect, for, though we have also suffered, it has not been in nearly so marked a degree.

I regret to say that the London Market has been at about its worst during the past year. Bad trade, excessive competition and the unwise departure from many of the established precepts of Marine Underwriting have brought about an unsatisfactory state of affairs which it may prove most difficult to correct. Some Underwriters, but we think there can only be a few, have indulged in a system of reinsurance by treaty, apparently overlooking the fact that if original rates are bare, they must be wholly unprofitable to their Treaty Companies after a substantial percentage of commission has been deducted. This policy is most shortsighted and a rude awakening must follow. To date the result has been merely to reduce rates to a figure that is almost entirely unremunerative. Rates, though easy to cut, are at all times hard to re-establish.

It is unnecessary for me to comment on the local position, and that prevailing in North China, further than to point out that this is to us, as to all other Far Eastern Companies, a source of considerable anxiety. However, this Company has weathered storms in the past and thanks to the conservative-policy that has been, and is being, followed it is now in a sound position and can view with a certain amount of equality the difficulties confonting us.

## [17] TRAFFIC CASES.

**TWO EUROPEAN MOTORISTS SUMMONED**

Among traffic prosecutions at the Central Magistracy this morning before Major C. Willson were two interesting cases against European motorists. The Hon. Mr. E. D. C. Wolfe (Captain Superintendent of Police) appeared for the prosecution.

Mr. W. J. Stanton, an assistant to Mr. L. Dunbar, flour broker of Alexandra Buildings, was summoned for taking his car past moving vehicles other than tram cars on the wrong side of the road.

A plea amounting to guilty being tendered, the C. S. P. said he did not wish to press the charge, but he thought the time had come for something to be done. He mentioned that accused had been fined for disobeying a traffic signal at the "Star" ferry on March 27 and quoted another instance at Kowloon in which the summons was "withdrawn owing to the delay in issuing it for which the police were not responsible. Under these circumstances he thought that perhaps the best plan would be for the accused to see him after the case.
A fine of $20 was imposed.
