## [2] LADY BLAKE

The older residents of Hongkong, (says the "China" Express and Talegraph") will regret to learn that Lady Blake, widow of Sir Henry Arthur Blake, C.C.M.G., died at her residence, Myrtle Grove, Youghal, Co. Cork, on April 18.

The eldest daughter and co-heir of the late Ralph Bernal Osborne, M.P., and sister of the Duchess of St. Albans, Lady Blake married Sir Henry Blake as his second wife in 1874, when he was a resident, magistrate in Ireland. She went with him to Bahamas in 1884, he having been appointed Governor of that Colony. Later she accompanied her husband to Newfoundland, Jamaica and Hongkong. In the last-named Colony he was Governor from 1897 to 1903, and there Lady Blake found much to interest her in the local flora and fauna which she made a special hobby.

Sir Henry went to Ceylon as Governor in 1908, and retiring in 1907, went with Lady Blake to Myrtle Grove, Youghall, Co. Cork, which they made their home and in the grounds of which Sir Henry Blake was interred some years ago. Lady Blake has now been laid beside him.

## [6] Officials' Campaign.

<sub>section under [5] BANGKOK CHOLERA</sub>

The present cholera epidemic in the city has made it advisable to mobilise all available forces for its suppression. Two units of the Sanitary Campaign Division have been detailed to Bangkok. One of these, known as the health boat, arrived several days ago, and has been operating among the river population. Its officials are making a house to house inspection, and, wherever necessary, urging the inmates to correct insanitary conditions and practices.

On Saturday nights the healthboat unit gives a public cinema show which is educational in character. Moreover, anti-cholera inoculations are administered free at the dispensary of the health boat. Unit No. 1, located at Cholburi, has been ordered to Bangkok, and is expected to arrive within the next few days.

"Public instruction concerning the prevention of cholera," said Khun Parivat, Director of the Sanitary Campaign Division, "is of major importance, and the object of the division is to impart such information as not unduly to terrorise citizens, but to warn them so that unnecessary disaster may be avoided by their cooperation with the health officials and local authorities."

## [8] EXPELLED PRIESTS.

**TERROR**

New York.—The first of the priests of the Roman Catholic Church to reach America after being expelled from M×x.com by the Calles, government arrived in New York on the Royal Spanish Mall liner "Leon III." They were penniless, homeless and without plans. All they were certain of was that they would never go back to Mexico.

They told a tale of horror and fright—a story that had about it the flavour of mediocval history. Ten priests, one Carmelite, number and four Marist brothers were in the group that landed here, and back of the flight of each there was an episode of Inquisitional terror. They left Mexico just eight days ago when the government ordered the expulsion with out trial or delay of every foreign priest in the land. Those who came in on the "Leon III" were French and Spanish; some of them had been in Mexico for as long as thirty-five years.

Father Epideforo Martinez, from a Vincentian seminary, at Merida, Yucatan, as spokesman for the group, told how the Mexican police had descended on the homes of the priests to arrest and denort them.

"They did not give us time" to take even our breviaries," he said. "We could get together no clothes. We have no money. They made us come as they found us."

But the teachings of the religious foreigners in Mexico have not been stopped by the action of the police, he said. In dark cellars, that show no light to eyes outside, masses are being offered. The obvious ceremonies have been discontinued, but behind locked doors, where the police least suspect it, priests who have been marked for expulsion are continuing their work.

Father Martinez had no idea of the number of clerics affected by the Mexican order. All he knew was that from all sections of the country came little groups of two and three men and larger groups of women from numberless to be placed upon the first available ship.

QUARTERED WITH PIGS.
"My own experience is typical," he said. "I was eating dinner at my seminary when the police called me a dressed in non-chemical clothes because in Mexico we are not allowed to wear our uniformes on the street. They told me I was under arrest. Their pistols showed they meant what they said. Giving me no time to collect any of my belongings, they took me first to the jail and then on a boat, where I, with several others, was quartered with pigs. That ship was on its way to Havana, and for one day I rode with the swine until the captain heard we were on board and moved us to the first-class quarters."

Some of the men had more difficult times. One young priest was marched along the street with fourteen guns aimed at him: all the while. The crowd of men and women lining the road to the jail booted and booed; but the police beat them with clubs, frightened him with rifles and then turned fire hoses on them.

Others of the men—some of them more than sixty years old—were kept under guard for eight days. The quiet of their monasteries was broken by the monotonous tramp of armed guards outside the doors. Sentinels were there always to see that none escaped.

Only one of the refugees wishes to remain in America. He is Miguel Pons Gilbert, a Spaniard who wishes to continue his work in Santa Fe, N. M.
