## [11] PROSPECTS IN CHINA.

Of greater interest, than usual is the report of the China Association's annual meeting in London. Events in the North and South of China have moved so rapidly since the previous annual meeting, that an altogether new set of circumstances is presented to-day as compared with twelve months ago. The opinions of those on the spot we know full well. What is thought in London cannot fail to appeal to us out here, especially among the members of such bodies as the China Association and the China's committee—the latter a very new organisation brought into being at the instance of Lord Southborough with the paternal blessing of the Foreign Office. It was feared at one time by some publicists that these two Associations might clash or at least overlap each other in their respective activities. The reverse has proved to be the case for the report of the annual meeting discloses the gratifying fact not only that they are working in the closest concord but that Lord Southborough has actually been elected President of the much older organisation. This must make for smoothness in the machinery set up in London to deal with China's problems, besides ensuring that those at Home, official and unofficial alike, are placed from time to time in possession of the most authentic and up to date information as to the course of events out here. There have been, and still are, so many misconceptions in Great Britain as to the position of affairs—in spite of the excellent propaganda work by the respective branches of the China Association in Hongkong and Shanghai—that at times we have almost despaired of the Home public and the Home politicians and statesmen being placed in possession of actual facts as against baseless rumours and senseless misstatements.

## [12] ANOTHER BREAKDOWN

"Hope deferred maketh the heart sick." There must have been thousands at Home yesterday imbued with optimism that the worst would not really come to pass and that there would, after all, be no general strike. The debate in the House of Commons, in its opening stages, did not promise too much The Prime Minister cast all the blame on the Trades' Union Congress for its threat of a general strike, which even that sturdy Liberal, Mr. Lloyd George, condemned as unwise. The Labourites, on the other hand, took up the cudgels for the miners and their Trade Union confreres, holding that there could be no reduction of miners' wages until they actually saw the programme for the reconstruction of the coal industry. Toward the end of the debate a ray of hope was forthcoming. The Chancellor of the Exchequer intimated that the Government was prepared to resume its laborious task of the past few days and resume the negotiations if the challenge of a general strike were first withdrawn. This ought to have meant also the withdrawal by the employers of their notices to the miners, so that both sides could re-enter the sphere of negotiation untramelled by threats or ultimatum.

In any case, the cold fact has to be recorded that negotiations were resumed between the Cabinet on the one side and the Trades Union Congress on the other only to fall at 11.30 p.m. Half an hour later a general strike was proclaimed. From official sources it is learned that the country is quiet—normal is the term used—and that volunteers are pouring in to assist in the maintenance of the necessaries of life. Regrets at this stage are superfluous. Both parties to the dispute, as well as the Government, have made an irrevocable decision fully aware of the calamity to the nation's commercial and financial welfare. All the leeway in industry during the past few years goes for nothing! The duration of the crisis will now vitally depend on the temper of the people, who have a right to be protected from the insidious effects of national industrial strife.

## [17] EXONERATED!

**ARINE COURT VERDICT**

Cuptain J. S. Masson, master of the Indo-China S. N. Col's "Yatshing," was exonorated by a Court of Inquiry which sat at the Marine Court this morning to investigate the collision between his steamer and the "Tacoma Maru" on April 18.

The Court found that the collision occurred (with technical details as to the exact position) in foggy weather, that Captain Masson took all prescautions to avoid collision, and that he was in no way to blame.

Lt.-Com. G. F. Hole, R.N. (Harbour Master) presided, the others on the bench being: — Lt.-Com. C. R. H. Harvey, R.N. (Chart Depot, Royal Naval Yard); Captain J. R. Scott, s.s. "Tyndareus;" Captain A. C. Inglis, s.s. "Selsten;" Captain R. Stephens, s.s. "Kwongtung."

Instructed by the Navigators and General Insurance Co., Lt., Mr. G. K. Hall Brutton appeared for Captain Masson; and Mr. Edgar Davidson watched proceedings on behalf of the owners.

In the witness-box, Captain Misson related the circumstances in detail. The incidents took place while the "Yatshing" was between Breaker Point and Chillang Point, on a voyage from Shanghai to Hongkong. He was with the chief officer (Mr. George Kelr, who gave corroborative evidence) all the time during those hours.

At 5.19 a.m. he heard a ship's fog signal on the starboard bow. The next minute, he stopped the engines and altered course to port. At 5.24 the masthead and bow light of a steamship was observed on the starboard side, and engines were immediately put full speed astern. Two minutes later, the collision took place.

The "Tacoma Maru" struck the "Yatshing" with her port bow, slightly abaft the stem on the starboard bow, then disappeared into the fog, her speed being estimated at 10 knots. The "Yatshing" made a little water in the forepeak and stayed in the same spot. By 5.46, the fog had cleared and witness saw the other ship about two miles on his beam. He sent over to ask if assistance was required. The position was seven miles South of Tong Mei Point, about 130 miles from Hongkong.
