## [8] BLUE SKIES.

*(Reuter's Service.)*

Blue Summer skies on the third morning of the strike found vast crowds going to business in London in a gaymood. "Vehicles were more numerous than ever. But traffic jams were fewer, partly due to the drivers following the back streets hitherto unexplored.

The principal centres of interest in the newspaper world are the offices of the "Daily Mail" and "Daily Herald" outside which crowds still gather, whilst outside the "Morning Post" office, where the "British Gazette" is published, a number of evident strikers congregate. There is no disorder, but the gates are shut by the Police on duty.

## [9] EMERGENCY SERVICES

The emergency services are rapidly improving in all directions. A great increase is reported in passenger trains on the main local lines and trunk lines which are now running 300 to 400 trains daily.

The London tubes are reopening to-day.

Government reports state that, speaking generally, there is no shortage of food. Apparently there has been little effort to raise prices. There is no difficulty in obtaining drivers for transport as anticipated by the Civil Commissioner. In some cases where there is difficulty in moving supplies the Trades Union Congress has permitted volunteer workers to remove the goods.

## [11] Buies Attacked.

<sub>section under [9] EMERGENCY SERVICES</sub>

A mob attacked some buses near the Elephant and Castle, South London. They set fire to one manned by a volunteer driver and conductor, who were forced to dismount, being roughly handled as they endeavoured to get clear. The passengers were told to disembark beforehand. The Police galloped to the scene, and constables and special constables soon controlled the situation.
The driver and conductor of another bus who were waylaid and nearly crashed into the Elephant public house, were also forced to fly to safety.

## [12] ACCIDENTS:

A motor cycle driven by Marine Barnicott with Marine Freeman on the pillow seat met with an accident at Bus Vaux Road West this morning.

In passing a hand truck Freeman knocked his leg on one of the wheels, injuring it below the knee. He was taken direct on board H.M.S. "Taman."

## [13] Lorry Collision

<sub>section under [12] ACCIDENTS:</sub>

Two lorries collided in the New Territories yesterday and one of them ran into a paddy field. The driver of the vehicle escaped serious injury.

In his report, the driver states that he was proceeding between Taipo and Fanling, when another lorry owned by the Luon Hop Transportation Company struck his vehicle in passing and overturned it into the paddy field. He scrambled out to find that the other lorry had disappeared. Damage to the extent of $40 was done to the vehicle.

## [14] Drowned.

<sub>section under [12] ACCIDENTS:</sub>

A Chinese boy, seven years old, living with his father at No. 110, Portland Street, Yaumati, lost his life yesterday afternoon by falling into a pool of water on the reclamation at Argyle Street.

Some people in the vicinity pulled the boy out and artificial respiration was tried by a Chinese doctor without effect. The Kwong Wah Hospital authorities declared life to be extinct when the body was taken there shortly after.

## [15] Another Fatality.

<sub>section under [12] ACCIDENTS:</sub>

A coolie living at the Ho Leong contractors' matshed at Robinson Road, died in hospital yesterday. The man is believed to have died from the effects of poisoning, as it was found on inquiry that he had taken some boiled herbs which he obtained from a medicine dealer at Tung Street.

**SPEECHES TO FOLLOW.**

Final witnesses for the second prisoner were called this morning in the Wing On Bank case and the case was adjourned until Monday morning for the speeches and summing up to follow without a week-end break.

Witnesses were called in support of the second prisoner's statement that Tam Lay, the man who spoke with regard to certain chops, the Crown allege, were forged for the purpose of the alleged conspiracy, was never employed at the engraving and printing shop kept by the second prisoner. They all denied having seen Tam Lay before.

Witnesses were all cross-examined by Mr. Jenkin as to the kind of types used by the shop.
Chan Kau, a young apprentice who had at times entered in the books of the printing shop in Canton, was handed sheets of paper with pieces cut out through which a certain amount of writing underneath could be discerned. He was asked by Mr. Jenkin if he could identify the writing.
