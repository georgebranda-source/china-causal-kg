## [21] A FINE FILM.

**"THE TEN COMMANDMENTS" SECURED."**

**A MODERN ANALOGY.**

Yesterday, a British film from a British novel dealing with typically English scenes was the subject of review in these columns in view of its showing next week at the Queen's Theatre.

A film of an entirely different type calling for lavish expenditure in the production and with scenes from Biblical times and modern days from which an analogy is drawn is the "Ten Commandments" which has also been secured by the Queen's Theatre for showing locally in Hongkong in the near future.
The old story of the captivity of the children of Israel in the land of Egypt, the championship of their cause by Moses, the man who refused the lot of princes to share in the trials of his own people in order that they might eventually be led to their own country, is pictured in a most interesting form. Even those who from childhood have been familiar with the story will find points which in the constant reiteration of the bare narrative may have escaped their attention.
The simple language and the depiction of scenes of captivity in Egypt, the escape, pursuit, crossing of the Red Sea, overwhelming of Pharaoh's hosts, scenes in the desert and of the inspiration of the leader in his draughtsmanship of the ten commandments are presented in such a form that one sees as never before the story which words alone, however beautifully phrased, can not entirely do justice to

Three thousand years separate the prologue and the modern plot which follows the Biblical scene yet the central thought of both is unchanged, without age, and as applicable to day as then. As the sins of Pharaoh and his horde of horsemen are avenged by the down crashing waves to let the children of Israel with their clear faith pass through, so does an emotional Red Sea engulf Dan McTavish who has attempted to raise his puny voice against immutable laws.

As Moses visited the wrath of God upon the thousands of Israelites engaged in false worship of the calf of gold, so do we find punishment inevitably visited upon those who presume to set up substitute idols of wealth, money or power in this modern day.

## [22] CHINESE REPUBLIC

**NOW WITHOUT SEMBLANCE OF A GOVERNMENT**

The Peking correspondent of the "Peking and Tientain Times" on April 21 writes:
Never before in the history of the Chinese Republic has there been so much political uncertainty and confusion as at present. The departure of the Provisional Chief Executive for Tientsin and the consequent collapse of his provisional regime have left Peking without even the semblance of a government. The Chief Executive sought to avert this dilemma by refusing to accept the resignation of the China Teh-yao Cabinet's bloc. The several Mandates issued before his departure accepted the resignations of the Premier who was concurrently Minister for War. Minister of Communications Minister of Finance, vice Minister for Foreign Affairs, and Director-General of the Wine and Tobacco Bureau. Mr. Hu Wei-teh, Minister of Foreign Affairs and the ranking member in the Cabinet, was appointed to serve as Acting Premier ad interim, upon whose shoulder now falls the duties of the Provisional Chief Executive. But as far as can be learned from authoritative sources, Minister Hu has declined to take up the onerous duty, with the result that there is no one in Peking who can be really held responsible for the conduct of the Government.

The Committee for Maintenance has repeatedly announced that it limits its own activities within its well-defined sphere. It seeks to maintain peace and order in the capital, and further than that it will not trend a single step. It will not interfere with the political affairs. Even in the absence of the Provisional Government, therefore, the Committee for Maintenance of Peace does not undertake to do anything which properly falls among the political duties of the Government. It is expected, however, that the Committee will outlive its own announced term of life of five days in view of the absence of a responsible Government.
According to all available information, Marshal Wu Pei-fu is expected to reach Paoting sometime to-day. Marshal Chang Tsolin is also expected to come to Tientsin shortly. The two leaders will have a conference finally to decide upon the form of the next government and procedure of political reorganisation. There has been a good deal of talk about Dr. W. W. Yen coming out to organise a "governing cabinet," and the convocation of the old Parliament to elect a new President in the meantime. There is no more foundation for this assertion than in saying Marshal Tsao Kun may be restored to the Presidency. Until and unless Marshal Wu and Marshal Chang have definitely announced their political programmes, every talk and report about this or that political change

or possibility is little more than mere guesswork.

It is learned that Mr. Hu Weiteh, to whom Marshal Tuan Chijuj entrusted administrative power before his departure to Tiensin, refuses to take upon himself the grave responsibility, saying that he will only look after diplomatic affairs. It is reported that he proceeded to the Peace Committee and placed in its custody the official seals of the Chief Executive, requesting General Wang Shih-chen to look after the administration. General Wang however, is learned to be unwilling to comply with the request, although he would strain his efforts for the preservation of peace and order in Peking. He further undertook to send a circular despatch to Marshal Chang Tso-lin, Marshal Wu Pei-fu, General Sun Chuan-fang and General Yen Hsi-shan to hold a conference without any loss of time, and select a person to take over the government, so that order can be established out of the chaos.

## [23] FIRST WOMAN JOURNALIST

Properly speaking, Madame de Sévigné, born in Paris, in the month of February, just 300 years ago, is the first woman journalist. Most of her letters were indeed immediately addressed to a narrow public, her married daughter, but they had at once a considerable circulation among that daughter's acquaintance, and they were preserved with a care which assured them more permanence than any daily press could have done. Nor are they so intimate that to reproduce them in print, for a public large as the number of those who for three centuries have been able to read French has been a desecration. These letters do not lay bare the deeper, more fundamental passions as do some other published correspondence—for instance the "Letters of a Portuguese Nun." Madame de Sévigné is the most decorous, the politest of epistolary artists. Listen to her disposing of the ultimate things of existence. "My position embarrasses me: without my own consent I was embarked upon life; I have to leave it, and the fact plagues me."
Rapidly, with humorous irritation, she discusses the alternatives presented to her by eternity, concludes that to have died in her nurse's arms would have "saved her much worry" and adds, finally, "Let us talk of something else." Thus she remains witty, superficial, and courageous, conforming to her ideal of breeding.
She is a journalist because her matter is nearly always topical—

She is a journalist because her matter is nearly always topical—the happenings in Paris under the Rol Soleil, the poems and plays which everyone was residing, talk about gardens and wood during the months in which modish folk were rusticating. There is in her writing the touch of actuality which gives its special character

and value to journalism. It is there whether she be speaking of the reactions of the Netherlandish war, or of the death of Turenne, or of the sensitive gentleman who committed suicide when he failed to supply the king with enough roast meat and fresh fish, or of the country dinner party at which the music of the "violins, hautboys, and trumpets" was drowned by the crashing fall of "a pyramid of fruit on twenty porcelain dishes."

In the 17th century Madame de Sévigné had already taken possession of that journalistic sphere which common consent has assigned to women. In spite of her allusions to great events, she is no publicist. Her point of view is invariably personal, and contemporary history interests her only as it affects personal relations, (says the "Glasgow Herald.") The standpoint is one which allows wearisome title, tattle, endless impertinence, nauseous vulgarity. But Madame de Sévigné proves that it is unnecessary to lapse from it to any of these faults. Spontaneously, easily, and charmingly, she treats tragedy, comedy, and pure routine merely as they seemed to a woman of the world who delighted in the play of destiny, found it important in the degree to which it amused, convenienced or incommoded her, and inclined, in her unconfessed thoughts, to doubt whether it had any further significance.
To-day her work lives more truly than that of her contemporaries who commented on passing events with a sense of responsibility. ## [24] BONNIE BABIES.

**SINGAPORE YOUNGSTERS AT MEMORIAL HALLE**

Babies figured rather more prominently in the scheme of things on April 28 than they ordinarily do, proud mothers bringing a large number of bonny youngsters to the Victoria Memorial Hall to compete in the Baby Show which is arranged annually by the Child Welfare Association, an organisation which is doing very valuable work in Singapore. Babies of all nationalities were submitted to the scrutiny of the judges and a "Free Press" reporter was authoritatively informed that the standard reached by the youngsters this year is higher than what it was on a similar occasion a year ago. At all events, one's impressions, if appearances are not deceptive, were that the Singapore climate is not nearly so detrimental to the health, even of youngsters, as it is reputed to be.

The proceedings were divided up into two periods, youngsters between the ages of 1 year and 5½ being required to submit themselves to the ordeal of weighing and measuring and the scrutiny of the experienced eyes of the judges—Mrs. Dr. Ferguson-Davie, Mrs. Dr. Dexter Allen, and Dr. Kelly—before nine o'clock in the morning. These competitors were divided into two classes. Class A for children between 2½ and 5½, and Class B for children between 1 and 2½. In each class there were 21 entries. Points were awarded for general appearance, cleanliness, general nutrition, teeth, ears, height and weight for age, and for feet.—"S. F. P."
