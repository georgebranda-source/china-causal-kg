## [1] UNION INSURANCE.

### Cunton Revival

With regard to what has been said about our long-standing friendly intercourse with the Cantonese, we doubtless all regret the present misunderstandings that have risen between us and look forward to a revival of the friendship and a renewal of business.

In these days it is no use/hiding one's light under a bushel, and the policy of expressing the Society's business in fine substantial buildings such as have been opened at Singapore and Sydney is, I venture to say, one of the best forms of propaganda and advertisement which can be made use of.

I feel confident that we can look forward to a continued increase in the results of the working of the Society, and I heartily endorse everything that has been said by the Chairman with reference to the ability and discrimination of the General Manager and staff in conducting the business.

I have much pleasure in second-ing the Report and Accounts.

## [2] MR. ALVES' SPEECH.

Mr. Chairman,—I have listened to your speech with great interest and generally wish to support the statements made by Mr. Young in the second-in-the-adoption of the report and accounts. I am in agreement with most of his remarks, but I desire to emphasize one particular point which has not been sufficiently stressed upon, and that is the prosperity of the Society is, in my opinion, solely the result of one guiding hand.

In the nature of things, undertakings large or small rise or fall by the efforts of one person, and unorthodox as it may seem to you, it is my firm belief that the larger the undertaking the greater its probability of success, where its destinies and broad questions of policy are confined to the hands of a capable single person; and in this the Union, Insurance Society of Canton, has been fortunate in the past, and if I may say so, in the present; and I hope even to a greater degree in the future, if the accounts now before you are any criterion. As a large single shareholder, and representative of a still larger shareholder closely related to me, I feel that our interests are very ably looked after by the present General Manager in the person of Mr. Paul Lauden.

Whilst commending the policy of distributing only the income from investments which, I notice, as regards the working for the year 1924 was exceeded to the extent of some £20,000, I would urge the view that when the result of the working for 1925 is better known at this time next year, the distribution to shareholders might be enlarged moderately a little further, say to the extent of a further bonus of 5% per share, which would cost about £34,000 extra.

In connection with the dividends, there is a point in the accounts which no doubt is readily explainable. If you will refer to the working account for 1924 you will find the cost of the interim dividend at the rate of 24/- at £161.994; for 1925 the cost is given in the report at £162,000 which is certainly the correct figure. I have not the Balance Sheet at December 31, 1924 before me from which to ascertain the change in the issued capital which, no doubt, took place.

HOUSEHOLDERS' SUPPLIES

## [4] SERIOUS SITUATION

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*London, May 27.*

The seriousness of the coal situation is emphasised by an announcement by the Secretary of State for Mines that householders will only be allowed a half cwt. of coal weekly from to-morrow. They must fetch it themselves, and they must have a written permit from the local authorities. This is a quarter of the amount allowed in the smallest houses in war time. It is one of many restrictions imposed owing to dwindling supplies.

Business premises are cut down to half their usual supplies. No illuminated signs are allowed, and street lighting is reduced to a safety minimum.

A Whitehall communique published under the Emergency Powers Act in this connection adds that heavy penalties are provided for non-compliance with the above orders.

The "Federation" of Master Printers and the Newspaper Society to-day agreed to a national settlement with the Printing and Kindred Trades, Federation providing for a continuance of the agreements existing prior to May 1.
It contains provisions for the prevention of lightning strikes, and stipulates that there shall be no interference with the contents of newspapers and periodicals published by members of the employers' organisations.

## [11] AFTER FIVE YEARS.

**COMMENTS ON DOWNFALL**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

Abd el Krim's surrender does not merely end the career of the world's most picturesque figure, whose five years guerrilla warfare against two European Powers began by a crushing defeat of the Spaniards in 1921, but probably terminates the secular quarrel between the Moors and Spaniards.
Abd el Krim, no ignorant mountaineer, was the guest of the Government of Madrid, for two years before he attacked them. As a result of his special course of study in Madrid, he engaged foreigners to teach the tribesmen methods of modern warfare, and he controlled the Riffan troops by means of an extensive telephone system.

Among the political consequences of the Riffan adventure has been the establishment of a Dictatorship in Spain, and the recognition by France of the necessity of close Franco-Spanish co-operation in Morocco, while the downfall of Abd el Krim will have repercussions in the whole of the Moslem world.

The “Times,” in an editorial, hopes Abd el Krim's correspondence will be published in order to reveal to the world the intrigues of the mining concessionales and financiers who exploited Riff cupidity and fanaticism.

## [12] Surrender Expected

*Tanglers, May 27.*

The surrender of Abd el Krim was not surprising, as it was known he was in flight, if not hiding, fearing assassination by his disappointed followers. The French had captured the greater part of Jebel Hamman, the mountain fastness which was Abd el Krim's last sanctuary.

His own tribe, the Beni Wariaghel, had submitted, and practically the whole Riff territory was in the hands of the French and Spanish.

The only two trilies intact, the Ghomara and Djebela, were apparently unaware of Abd el Krim's collapse.

End Welcomed.

France's great sigh of relief at the surrender of Abd-el-Krim rustles the pages of the newspapers which joyfully welcome the end of the Riff "venture, which, they emphasise, there must be no repetition. The Riff must henceforth be properly governed," in connection with which the continuance of Franco-Spanish co-operation "is underlined in order to exploit the wealth of Morocco.

It is suggested that Abd-el-Krim will not be permitted to reside in Moslem territory and may be compelled to apologise to the Sultan of Morocco.

## [15] WORLD'S COMMERCE.

His Majesty the King granted an audience to the forty delegates of the International Parliamentary Commercial Conference. His Majesty recalling the 1918 Conference, when only eight countries were represented, thought the present encouraging increase indicated a general desire to assist reparation, and the injuries the War inflicted on the world's commerce.

His Majesty picked out from the Conference's agenda the production and distribution of coal, shipping law and Customs which he declared was of vital importance to Britain and doubtless to other countries.

Marshal Wu Pei-fu left for Peking by special train at nine o'clock this morning.
