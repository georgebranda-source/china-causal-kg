## [1] GROPING ITS WAY.

### (Combined from Page 6.)

The Government had introduced Naval ratings and volunteers to assist loyal workers. He referred in this connection to the cessation of the generation of electrical power and light in the day time at five stations, which had affected the London Hospital, a number of bakeries worked by electricity and the cold storage stations in the docks, as a result of which the meat would likely go bad.

The food supply of London was proceeding quite satisfactorily.
Referring to the question of the protection of willing workers, he said that he proposed to ask the community as a whole to enroll as special constables in much larger numbers. There were 7,500 original special constables already on duty and over 3,000 were sworn in during the last two days.

He had directed the Police not to allow the proposed mass meeting in the heart of London to night, which was organised by the Dept-ford Brenneil of the Communist Party and kindred associations.

Sir W. Joynson-Hicks appealed to the people not to notice the alarmist rumours, many of which had already been circulated. The only riot had been a small one at Poplar. The country was steady at the moment, and he enjoined it to stand, just.

## [2] Mr. Henderson Desires a Formula

Mr. Anthony Henderson, the ex-Home Secretary, emphasised that the Trades Union Congress had offered to maintain the essential services. He criticised the bitter attack in a leading article in the "British Gazette" on men whose praises the Government was singing loud a few days ago.

He appealed to the House to strive to get a formula for a settlement of the dispute on which they were working before the final break which, he believed, would not have occurred if the news had not come through that a certain paper had been interfered with.

Mr. Lloyd George condemned the precipitancy in "breaking off the negotiations. He agreed with Mr. Henderson that the nation had a deal of sympathy with the miners, but the general strike had obscured the merits of the minors' ease and alienated sympathy.

The Government had public opinion behind it in the present steps, but he agreed with the condemnation of the "British Gazette" article.

Published in a Hurry. Sir W. Joynson-Hicks, intervening, said that he had not read the article until that moment, but he would convey the Opposition views to the Editor. The paper was published in a great hurry, and the Government did not desire to use it for propaganda purposes.

Mr. Lloyd George accepted this, but he asked for complete impartiality on the views of all Parties. I could not be for the absence of space, because a whole paragraph to day was devoted to monkeys and bearded lizards. Stopping the Press was a most foolish thing, and the Liberals would not oppose the emergency regulations.

## [4] LABOURITE'S FEARS

Resumption of Negotiations $ ^{1} $

Dr. L. Hayden Guest (Labourite, North Southwark) in urging the resumption of the negotiations, said he feared that some rash and ill-considered action by the hot-heads might precipitate a catastrophe.

But Not This Week or Next.

Mr. John Bromley, the engineers' leader, admitted that if all the Government resources were brought to bear they were bound to be defeated, but it would not be this week or next or the week after. He paid a tribute to the peace efforts of the Prime Minister who at the last moment had swayed "sinister hands."

## [5] NEWSPAPER OFFER

Sir E. M. Wilfer (Conservative, Timworth), on behalf of a group of newspapers with a circulation of ten millions weekly, offered to print two columns of matter supplied by the Labour Party provided that the matter conformed with the law. He was confident that other editors would take the same line.

## [6] QUESTION OF DIGNITY.

"Is a question of dignity keeping the parties apart" warmly, queried Mr. T. P. O'Connor (Nationalist, Scotland Division of Liverpool). He added: "Good God, what is the dignity of any man compared with the disaster facing the country?"

## [7] "INVINCIBLE"

Mr. J. R. Clynes, (Labourite, Plathing) emphasise the ultimate invincibility of the Labourites, claimed that the strike call was not merely successful, but that hundreds and thousands of men had come out who were not required to strike.

## [8] NO PERSONALITIES.

### Search for a Solution.

The studied absence of anything in the nature of embittered personal references was strongly evidenced by all Parties earnestly seeking a solution.

There was an eager chorus of demands to know the nature of “the formula” which Mr. Thomas declared that the Labourites were ready to present to the Prime Minister when the negotiations were broken off.

The labourites opposed a number of individual clauses in the regulations, especially that empowering the Postmaster to refuse to accept certain classes of telegrams.
The amendment was defeated on a division, the voting being:—

Against..... 92

A similar fate overtook a motion to delete the clause prohibiting the possession of firearms, explosives, weapons, stones, and dangerous missiles.

Debate Adjourned: The debate was adjourned till to-morrow.

H.R.H. the Prince of Wales and H. R. H. the Duke of York were present in the Gallery during the sitting.

The "British Gazette" appears in two editions, the later of which contains four instead of two pages.
Both issues appeal to loyal citizens to support His Majesty's Government and Parliament to defeat the general strike. "Either the country break the general strike or the general strike will break the country."

## [9] ON THE CONTINENT.

Exporters Losses in Fruit and Potatoes.
Pat's, May 5
The fall of the franc is attributed to sales by British Banks to boater up sterling, and also to the fact that the strike is bound

seriously to affect French exporters and ultimately Continental trade generally.

It is reported from Boulogne that a number of British cargo ships laden with fruit and new potatoes from Spain are held up.
The export of cherries, strawberries, and other early fruit from Provence, has entirely ceased. The growers are likely to be involved in heavy losses.

Two thousand workmen in a motor car factory at Saint Ouen have struck in sympathy with the strikers in Great Britain.

Berlin: May 5.

The conference of the General Federation of Trade Unions, miners, transport workers, and railwaymen adopted a resolution to support to the utmost the British Unions. There will be no bunkering of British vessels, and all measures are being taken to prevent coal reaching Great Britain.

'Difficult to Refuse.'

Copenhagen, May 5.
The Danish General Workers' Union has stated that if a request for support is received from the British it will be difficult to refuse in view of the help which British labour afforded the Danish strikers in the Spring.

Soviet Labour Support.

The Soviet labour organisations are organising financial support for the British strikers.

## [11] IN AMERICA.

Strike Deplored As a Tragedy.

The British strike news monopolises the front pages of all the morning and evening newspapers. Editorials deplore the event as a tragedy, though the talk of a Revolution is received with incredulity as a contradiction of the calmness of the British character.
The "Tribune" says that a Revolution in Great Britain is as absurd as Arctic weather in Cuba or Cairo.

The "New York Times" defends Mr. Baldwin, who, it declares, incurred the reproaches of the more Conservative Tories on account of his efforts in the past two years to promote the interests of the working class, but when the Labourites adopted the attitude that the Trades Union Congress was mightier than Parliament "even the long-suffering. Mr. Baldwin could not be down before that."

## [12] EARLIER NEWS.

*London, May 1*

Good tramway services are operating in Glasgow, Liverpool and Bournemouth.

The publication of newspapers has been resumed in a number of country towns, including Newcastle, Cardiff and Glasgow, photographic plates of typescript being utilised in many cases. London financial papers are published with stock and share movements.

Broadensting was extensively used last night for the distribution of Government information and other news relating to public arrangements. The service was operated every half hour from 9.30 to midnight.

*Paria, May 5.*

All the newspapers comment on the remarkable coolness of the British public in the present crisis.

"Le Petit Parisien" says the man in the street in Britain has set his face resolutely against revolution.

The “Gaulois” says that all civilised peoples should stand together in such a time.

The "Echo de Paris" does not doubt but that Constitutional Government will ultimately triumph.
The "Oeuvre" is of the opinion that the strike is due to the re-adoption of the gold standard.

## [14] SHIPS NOT HINDERED.

*(From Our Own Correspondent.)*

*Amoy, May 2:*

British trade in Amoy has made steady progress during the last two months.

Passengers coming in or going out on British ships now meet with no hindrance. The first batches of cargo on the s.s. "Yingchow" and "Klukiang" were discharged without any trouble. This is the first sign of a return to general business since May 31 of last year.

## [15] Emigrant Traité.

<sub>section under [14] SHIPS NOT HINDERED.</sub>

Emigrants from Amoy to both the Straits and the South Sea Islands are estimated to number about 250,000 since February of this year. The present unrest in the interior, together with better business conditions in the South, is the chief cause of so great a number of people leaving this district.

The submarines of the U.S. Asiatic Fleet, under the command of Captain Marquart, are spending a few days in Amoy, en route to North China from the Philippine Islands. Major Burton Young Read, aide-de-camp to the Governor-General of the Philippines, and Mr. Leroy Webber, American Consul in Amoy (recently transferred to Chefoo), are accompanying the Fleet to Tsingtao and proceeding thence to Peking where Mr. Webber will have medical treatment at the Rockefeller Hospital before undertaking his new work in Chefoo.

## [17] BELGIAN CRISIS.

**SERIOUS FINANCIAL POSITION REPORTED:**

**MINISTERS RESIGNED**

*(Router's Service.)*

A serious financial position has arisen as the result of the depreciation of the franc.

A supplementary Estimate is being submitted to the Chamber for 11,036,000,000. Frances for the 1925 Budget and the 1926 Budget must be revised.

A loan from the Banks, on which the Government was counting is now impossible.

The Minister of the Interior, Baron Rolyn Jaequemyns, has resigned as he disagrees with the fiscal policy, and the Minister for the Colonies, M. Curton, has decided to resign.

Two Chinese, a man and a woman, have been sent to the Government Civil Hospital after being knocked down by motor car No. 1705, owned by the Hongkong Electric Company, in Queen's Road East.

## [21] THE WEATHER.

"East or variable" winds, light; fine" reads the forecast for the 24 hours ending at noon to-morrow.

This morning's Observatory report says that pressure has increased moderately at Shanghai and decreased slightly from S.W. Japan to the Loochoos and Formosa. A depression lies over Western China and a trough of low pressure extends from Formosa to Korea. A wedge of relatively high pressure covers S. China.
