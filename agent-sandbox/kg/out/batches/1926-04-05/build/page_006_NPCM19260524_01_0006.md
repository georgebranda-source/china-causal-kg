In full swing once again is the Chinese civil war. When will it all end is a problem that not even the Chinese themselves dare predict. A semblance of a truce to-day may be shattered tomorrow. China is in the hands of the militarists. Until these have been suppressed, the outlook must remain gloomy.

## [10] CATHOLIC WORK.

**NEW BUILDING FOR YOUNG MEN**

**OPENED YESTERDAY.**

The new building of the Chinese Catholic Young Men's Society in Cainie Road was opened yesterday afternoon by the Rev. Father Bunchio, the inaugurator of the Society a few years ago. The new building was blessed according to Catholic sites and a statue of St. John the Evangelist was also unveiled.

Amongst the large number present was the Bishop-Elect, the Rev. Luther Voltorin.

Mr. Tso Chak-wan, the President of the Society, recalled its origin and attributed its success largely to the efforts of Father Banchio. He also stated that before long they hoped to have a Women's Catholic Society also.

The Rev. Father Valtorta, the Bishop-Elect, said that he had been very often told in Hongkong that the people were lacking in perseverance, and that although they might start some good work they were apt to lose enthusiasm. If that were so, then the Society should be an exception to the rule. They needed prayer, action and sacrifice; they were men of faith, and they believed everything came from God. They must work together and be co-operators with Him. Every priest would give all possible support to the Society. He felt that the Society would increase and expand, and that they would soon find their present building too small.

There was much work to be done, and particularly in the uplifting of their poorer brethren. They believed in the ideal of Christ, and they would surely not fall to come up to expectations. (Applause).

The Rev. Father Downs, congratulated Father Bunchio on the work which he had started. It was a wonderful work, very necessary at such times, for the furtherance of education and the work of the Church.

The Rev. Father Banchio spoke at length in Chinese. Members of the Society presented him with a set of books of Chinese classics.
The following message was called to His Holiness, the Pope: "First gathering of Hongkong Chinese Catholic Youth presents filial homage and asks for Apostolic Blessing."

A concert was held in the Club in the evening.

## [11] CYCLISTS' PROGRESS

**WORLD TOURISTS AND PICKETS.**

The two round-the-world cyclists, Gibelli and Kundinger, recently in Hongkong, arrived in Canton after experiencing difficulty with the strike pickets. They have now left for the North.

They left Hongkong on the 15th instant and spent the night in a bungalow near the frontier. The next day when they crossed the border they were arrested by strike pickets and were only able to proceed on the intervention of the Station Master who provided them with a pass which enabled them to continue safely.

The heavy going, combined with the loads they were carrying, made progress difficult but the cyclists eventually reached Canton on the 17th constant, where they obtained special permits from the Government to travel through Kwangtung northwards to Hankow. The cyclists visited Shameen, where they were warmly received, and left for the North on Thursday.

## [12] OPIUM TRADE

The following article appears in "The Times of India"—of March 22:—

There is a special virtue in doing good by stealth, and to its influence we attribute the almost total absence of debate with which the Indian Legislature last week received the Government of India's plan for immediate steps for the suppression of opium exports from India and in both Houses unanimously adopted it. All the same we wish our legislators had been a little less careful to hide the light of their virtue under the bushel of silence. "Good people from America and elsewhere at Geneva have out of ignorance and misunderstanding poured much unjust obloquy upon India and her Government over the opium business. Easy-going plotists in England and India with even less excuse have done the same. A few speeches from the unofficial benches of the Legislature giving the practical common sense view of the matter would have done something to correct the false impression they have created and would have shown that the execution of the policy now adopted will but put the coping stone on a very fine and nobly inspired series of restrictive measures enacted during a long period of years. This policy does not date from the recent international conventions upon which it is directly based nor spring from the discussions which produced them. It dates back to at least twenty years ago, when, in 1906, the Government of India, animated by none but the highest motives, entered into negotiations with China which resulted in the agreement of January, 1908, whereby India undertook progressively to diminish her exports of opium so as to cooperate with the Chinese Government in their declared policy of reducing and finally stopping the cultivation of opium in their own land. The Chinese have never fulfilled their part of the arrangement, but India faithfully carried out hers, stopping exports to China years ago and never suffering their revival. More India in 1925 adopted the further plan of stopping all exports of opium from her shores to non-Asiatic countries or to any of the latter except to their own Governments or under licences granted by their Governments. She might well have claimed from that point that she had done all that could reasonably be demanded of her. The steps now to be taken aim at doing better by Far Eastern countries and by those in trading relations with them than the Governments of those Far Eastern countries are ready to do, and it cannot too strongly be emphasised that though India is taking them in agreement with an international convention, she is not compelled by her signature of that convention to take them and is acting not merely according to its letter but in the fullest harmony with its spirit. Here in its text.

The State, signatory to the present protocol, recognising that under Chapter I of the Hague Convention the duty rests upon them of establishing such a control over the production, distribution and exportation of raw opium as would prevent the illicit traffic, agree to take such measures as may be required to prevent completely within five years from the present date the smuggling of opium from constituting a serious obstacle to the effective suppression of the use of prepared opium in those countries where such use is temporarily authorised.

India neither exports nor manufactures prepared opium, by which is meant opium in the form required for pipe smoking. The people of India do not use prepared opium because they do not smoke opium. Exceptions here or there merely prove the rule, but everyone in touch with trading centres knows that Far Eastern countries which do not produce raw opium manufacture from their imports of it prepared opium and opium derivatives and export them; Japan, for example, imports quantities of opium that her own people do not consume, and there is little doubt a great deal if not all of it goes abroad again in the form of morphs. That all the opium used in these manufactures is Indian is certainly not the case. Probably only a small proportion is. The steps now initiated by the Government of India and approved by their Legislature will ensure that none of it is. "It is good not.....to do anything whereby they brother stumbleth." We can recall no instance in which a nation has hitherto in accordance with that holy principle acted so finely or with such sublime disregard of the cost as India through her Government and with the approval of her Legislature is doing in this matter.

We doubt whether many people have calculated the financial cost of the sacrifice of funds for nation building services which India's recent restrictive optimum policy has.

## [15] MISSIONARY NEWS

**BISHOP ON FERMENT IN CHINESE LIFE.**

At the last meeting of the General Committee held at Salisbury Square, the Church Missionary Society's policy with regard to its missions in China was discussed.

Bishop Bannister, late of Kwangsi and Hunan, reviewed the elements of ferment in Chinese life, which present great difficulties to the furtherance of all kinds of missionary enterprise. At the same time he emphasised various grounds for optimism, laying stress on the abiding presence in China of the spiritual faculty, together with the fact that repeatedly, and in many different ways, non-Christian Chinese express the conviction that the only hope for the future of China lies in the dynamic behind the Christian religion.

It was mentioned that during the recent military activities, as guns are in action near to mission stations, the mission buildings have been thronged by Chinese seeking protection.

## [16] THE MAURETANIA

**AN EXTRAORDINARY FEAT.**

The extraordinary feat of the Cunard Hner "Mauretanda" in logging 29 knots when rushing to the help of the disabled British steamer "Laleham" is a good reminder to the public that this veteran steamer is still far and away the fastest on the North Atlantic.

When she first came out it was considered marvellous that she could manage 25 knots, but if anybody had said in those days that 20 years later she would be able to add four knots to her speed he would have been disbelieved. In this she is very much like her famous predecessor, the "Etruria" of 1884, which somehow contrived to improve her speed steadily as she got older. In the case of the "Etruria," the performance was perhaps even more remarkable than the "Mauretania," for she was fitted with old-fashioned compound engines and a single crew, with a high power which threw quite enough strain on the shaft to begin with.

It is wonderful what a high speed ship can contrive when the occasion really demands it. The cruiser "Kent" was specially commissioned into the Navy as a 22½-knot ship, as she could not manage her contract speed of 28, yet when it was wanted at the Battle of the Falklands, her speed was worked up to 25.1 knots.

Similarly, there is the story of the steamer during the war which met a German submarine and was chased for miles. When finally she did make port, the marine superintendent was not in the least interested in the captain's account of his narrow escape from destruction, but was fairly dancing with excitement over the fact that she had broken the record by 3½ minutes.

## [17] GERMAN SHIPBUILDING POSITION.

Considerable light is shed on the situation of German shipbuilding and allied industries by the annual report of the directors of the Krupp concern, in which it is stated that, after writing off 16,700,000 marks to cover various trading losses incurred, on the whole undertaking there remains a net total loss of 15,300,000 marks on the year's working. Practically half this deficit was caused by the German's Werft, Krupp's shipbuilding yard at Kiel, which showed a loss last year of seven million marks, and which necessitated writing down the capital of the subsidiary company concerned from 10 to three millions. It should also be noted that the capital of the Krupp Company itself has been reduced from 160 to 100 millions by the cancellation of 60 millions' worth of reserve shares. Herr Krupp, in presenting the report at the annual meeting, referred to the opening up of business with Russia in agricultural and textile machinery, and he also stated that the firm had lost at least one hundred million marks through the destruction of machinery by the Inter-Allied Military Mission, an admission of a highly significant character.
