The Chinese Government has announced its intention of pressing for greater freedom in fixing the tariff rates than was contemplated by Washington, when the work of the Conference was restricted to the increase of the present 5 percent, tariff by a surtax of 2 percent, for necessaries and 5 per cent. For luxuries. The desire of the Chinese to get rid of the conventional tariff and to formulate for themselves a tariff suited to the requirements of their own country, and especially to the protection of their own industries, as natural and legitimate, and there is little doubt that their views will be sympathetically considered by the Conference. The only justification for any form of conventional tariff, or for the participation of other Powers in the formulation of China's tariff, rests on the importance, in the interests of China and of her own trade, of maintaining a maximum of stability with a minimum of interference during the stage of transition through which she is now passing.

The Chinese are naturally de-sirous of increasing the revenues from this source, of obtaining a greater freedom in the disposal of surplus funds after the services of the national debt secured on the Customs has been met, and of introducing a stronger Chinese element into the personnel of the Administration. All of these aims are perfectly natural. The trader, the investor, and the industrialist, whether Chinese or foreign, ask only that the stability of the Customs Administration shall remain un-impaired, and that this particular Service shall be guaranteed against the disturbing influences of partisan interference.

There has never been any opposition in this country to China's desire for a tariff rate higher than 5 per cent, or indeed, for a more scientific and better-distributed scale of duties. If the Central Government will guarantee that each province shall receive from the increased Customs revenue adequate compensation for the protection of trade within its borders, and if identical treatment is guaranteed to all goods, whether Chinese or foreign, the problem can certainly be solved in a practical and effective way. The stoppage of irregular taxes and the protection and security of trade throughout the country would have an immediate effect in restoring confidence among traders and investors all. *Chefq, Nov. 21.*

November 11, Armistice Day, was celebrated here in the usual way. A Service was held in the morning at St. Andrew's Church, where the Two Minutes Silence was observed. The Service was fairly well attended. Poppies were sold for Earl Haig's Fund, and realised the sum of 254 8s. 10d. In the evening the "Sons of Britain Society" held their annual dinner, and later there was a dance in the Chefoo Club, when a cold supper was served, and at which a large number were present.

Recent departures include the Rev. and Mrs. Tully McCrea for the States; they are accompanied by their son and daughter, Mr. Tully, Jnr., and Miss Maribelle. Mr. and Mrs. McCrea have been for many years in this province in connection with the work of the American Baptist. Mission and during the last few years when they have again been residents in this port have won a well-deserved popularity among those who live here.

Miss E. C. Jones, who for the last thirteen years has been Ward-robe Mistress at the C.I.M. Girls' School, has also left, having had to go back to England, due to personal home affairs.

We have had the pleasure "of offering our congratulations to Mr. G. P. Welch, of the C.I.M. Boys' School, and Miss Eva Anderson, of the C.I.M. Preparatory School on their engagement. Also

to Dr. Bryan and Miss Love of the Temple. Hill Hospital Staff; and also to Mr. Brian McCarthy and Miss Lawton, of Hwanghsien, whose engagements were announced at about the same time.
The China Inland Mission Schools are breaking up for their two months' winter holiday during the next few days, and most of the children will be leaving for their respective homes during the coming week.

## [9] CORRESPONDENCE.

**TECHNICAL INSTITUTE**

Sir,—It is simple and clear that the present trouble has been initiated by some of the Hongkong students and for that various methods (including a contemplation of raising the school fees) have been introduced to punish the defaulters.

But isn't it strange that teachers were made to suffer for the students' fault (if it can be called a fault) because the normal class fee which was already considered rather high has been increased by 40 per cent., while owing to the present circumstances many teachers (normal students) got a reduction in their salary and therefore the above increase is absolutely unjustified.

Is it not that the aim of the Technical Institute is to give a liberal Education for bettering the knowledge of those who can not afford to join a higher school or university? If so, why should its aim be lost?
Also certain Educational Institute in Hongkong seems to lose sight of the ends of its founder who devoted all his time to teach the poor children and surely expects his followers to do the same and not to refuse the admission of the poor boys who pay deficient fees under the excuse "no place."
