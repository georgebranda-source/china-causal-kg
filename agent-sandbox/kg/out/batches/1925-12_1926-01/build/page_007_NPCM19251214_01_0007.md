## [2] FRANCE AND RUSSIA.

**FIRST STEPS TO RESUMING RELATIONS**

**IMPORTANT CONSERVATIONS.**

*(Heuter's Service.)*

The Briand and Chicherin conversations mentioned yesterday, will continue during the week, after which M. Chicherin, accompanied by M. Rakovsky, will return to Moscow.

It is anticipated that negotiations for a French and Soviet agreement will enter a decisive phase on M. Rakovsky's return about the middle of January.
“Le Journal” declares that the vital points of the French side are an honest settlement of the Russian debt to France; the ministry of Russia into Europe and ties and the formal Soviet republic of the activities of the First Internationale.

William Turner, who "died on January 17, 1925," according to the headstone on his alleged grave at Mattewan, West Virginia, landed unexpectedly from the United States Line steamer "Resolute" at New York and declared he was very much alive. He was immediately taken into custody on the ground that he had aided and abetted his wife and sister to collect $17,000 insurance on his "death."

Turner, who was a foreman in a coal mine, declares that his father-in-law set off dynamite in order to procure evidence of his legal death, and as a result of the explosion which followed—believed at the time accidental—two men were killed, one of whom was buried as Turner. The body was mangled to such extent that identification seemed impossible, but the insurance companies, while paying the claims, expressed doubt as to the case. Turner says that after the bogus accident he was forced to leave home at the point of a gun by Joe Jacks, jun. his brother-in-law, to meet Jacks, sen. his father-in-law, in New York, and by him forced to go to Hamburg and play dead so that the insurance taken out only a few days before could be collected. For nine months he lived in semi-starvation in Germany, and the failure to receive money from friends at home influenced Turner to write and finally come back to face indictment for murder, to find a grave in a country cemetery, bearing his own name and his relatives living luxuriously upon his insurance, denying he was alive.

"My husband," Mrs. Turner said to the police, "is dead. This new Turner must be a fraud. Ten cases were before Sir Henry Cowper "Gollan, Chief Justice, at the Bankruptcy Court on Saturday, all in respect of Chinese firms.

Three applications for adjudication were granted, three adjournments pending hearing of petitions granted and four refused.

In respect of Hul Cheung Shin, trading as the Cheong Hoo Steamship Company, Mr. L. D. Turner, for the debtor, applied for a receiving order which he understood was to be opposed by the Official Receiver on the grounds of insufficiency of assets. The business of the debtor had been to charter steamers trading between this and other eastern ports. He had begun business on $100,000 capital and had failed owing to the shipping slump last year, the final blow being the recent strike.

The assets, the Official Receiver had down as between $2,000 and $3,000 and the liabilities as nearly $80,000. The largest creditors, the Oriental Steam, Navigation Company had informed him, said Mr. Turner, also, he believed, the Official Receiver, that they were not opposed to the application and were willing to receive a dividend however small. They were convinced that the debtor's losses were genuine and not the result of his own carelessness. Other creditors, convinced of his misfortunes and with confidence in him, were not desirous of making him bankrupt.

The Official Receiver pointed out the heavy liabilities in comparison with the assets and mentioned a debt to the Douglas S.S. Company for $19,000.

His Lordship said he had no option but to dismiss the application for a receiving order.

## [12] THE NEED OF A CLUB.

It is a matter of considerable local interest to notice that at the annual dinner of the Central Union of Chinese Students in London, "at which several well-known persons were present including Mr. Chao-Hsin Chu, Mr. H. G. Wells, Major-General Sir Netil and Lady Malcolm, Sir Denison and Lady Ross, Mr. and Mrs. Bertrand Russell, and many others, the chair was taken by the President of the Central Union, Mr. R. C. Lee, Mr. Lee is a former student of Queen's College and is a son of the well-known Hongkong merchant, Mr. Lee Hysan, who has another son also being educated in England.
In view of the statement made public the other day that Mr. Lee Hysan had under contemplation a scheme by which, with British co-operation and the good wishes of the late Governor and Colonial Secretary of Hongkong, a Cosmo-politan Club would be formed in the old Jardine House on his East Point property, with the object of bringing Chinese and foreigners together for social intercourse, it is of interest to note the sentiments expressed by the President, Mr. R. C. Lee, in proposing the toast of "Our Guests." He stated that it had always been the earnest desire of the Chinese students in England to promote cordial and sincere friendship with British people and also to strengthen the friendship between China and her neighbours. He thought there was a Club for Chinese, British and American business men in Shanghai, which sort of institution would do much to promote that mutual understanding they so much desired. To a certain extent the Chinese students in England had achieved this. He then referred to the privilege they appreciated so much of attending the Universities in England, and thanked the several distinguished guests for attending the dinner.
In view of these sentiments, it is interesting to note Mr. Lee Hysan's interest in the same excellent object, and it is to be hoped that he will not be deflected from his purpose of founding a high class club by any cold water douches such as are the usual lot of pioneers in a new project.
