## [8] HONGKONG LABOUR.

Although the factor of labour plays a secondary part in such a movement as is being directed from Canton with the object of dealing as great a blow as possible at Hongkong as a distributing centre for South China, it may well be that along the lines of a genuine attempt to tackle the problem in Hongkong lies the means of demonstrating our expressed willingness to entertain and forward any policy which will be of real and lasting benefit to the Chinese community and thus lead to the creation of that goodwill which is the first and most important essential for the conduct of business. It does not follow that the desired result would be best obtained by giving way to whatever demands or requests may be made by labour agitators, and it most certainly would not do to give way further in the matter of the guilds; most of which are dependent for their membership on the workings of an inner society which extorts exorbitant subscriptions by means of intimidation and force. Any relaxation of the powers of the Government regarding these guilds or the revoking of any decisions may be considered out of the question for the Government's power at the

ing of the closing of guilds when definitely unlawful acts are proved against them. As, however, in his struggle for emancipation it is essential that the worker of China should share in common with workers of other countries the privilege of organising for the purpose of bettering his lot, the question arises as to what best to offer to take the place of the guilds. The late Mr. Montague Ede some months before he was stricken with his final illness prepared a lengthy document on the subject and if his health had held good it is more than likely that there would have been in operation ere this some of the machinery for the direction of the organisation he favoured. The scheme provided for the erection in Yaumati of a six-storied building as headquarters from which direction would be exercised by the following departments: Labour; Technical Education; Estimation, (of contracts, tenders, etc.) for work undertaken by the Association; Insurance and Banking. The organisation was to interest itself in fixing appropriate wage scales for different trades and a scheme of procedure with reference to arbitration in cases of dispute was drawn up. Details even into such matters as the provision of gymnasium; a newspaper of the Association's own, hospital, restaurant, and sleeping accommodation were gone into and a scheme outlined for the issue of shares. After the late Mr. Ede took to his bed nothing further was heard of the scheme which at the time was considered by many to be impracticable: The promoter was, however, confident that membership of the Association would be valued both by workers and employers as a privilege, so much so that it would induce other workmen and employers to possess similar privileges—he had at the time he brought the scheme forward some 5,000 skilled and unskilled workers willing to join. ## [13] MORE ECONOMY.

The Government “cut its coat” according to its income, or the progress which its income denoted. It built new roads, initiated a new water scheme, built public offices, increased staff departments, had an expensive valuation of “military lands” made on which it commenced negotiations with the War Office, and has been considering harbour improvements. It earned praise for its aliveness in all these matters, even if a few wondered how far the Colony was from a heavier taxation to meet deficiency of income from decreased revenue from sales of opium and sales of land. The Government must naturally “cut its coat” in view of the decreased revenue as a result of the Canton boycott. It cannot be accused of indecent haste in indicating in what direction it is cutting down expenditure to meet income. It has probably deferred extreme measures—distasteful as they must be—until the last moment, hoping for as sudden a return to the normal, as the abnormal was ushered in. Its economies are not likely to find general acceptance. The first known descent of the axe affected a number of locally-employed teachers, and to this has to be added a suspension of building activities in connection with the new Queen's College site and buildings. True educationalists will probably deplore both decisions, and it is possible, in this connection, to suggest once more that the Government's determination to punish striking schoolboys by refusing them admission to Government schools was a trifle harsh. The new site also, by being allowed to lie derelict, is not likely to improve, and it is possible to argue that when the time comes—and many will hope it will be soon—to continue building operations, the cost of such will be added to considerably. It has been decided to effect a saving by ceasing to issue Trade Returns. The banks find no use for them and the Hongkong General Chamber of Commerce is in favour of the abolition of the Statistical Office responsible for them. Where authorities are in unison the voice of criticism is stilled. Trade returns are a feature of Customs activity in China and they afford a valuable insight into the extent of China's trade with the world. We trust it may be possible to continue some less costly form of indicating Hongkong's trade, the commodities and the countries concerned. Economies which may have an effect on the general health of the community should be adopted only after the most careful consideration. Hongkong has been familiar with the sight of its ox-driven dust carts, which are to be retained just as many a Home town is familiar with the horse-driven variety. But in such a place as this where the work of vermin can play such a havoc, the extinction of the old-style method of removal of rubbish is an innovation much to be desired. The Government is entitled to sympathy in the problem it has so suddenly been called upon to face. And the position is made more difficult by reason of the fact that the future holds out no assurance that Chinese labour dementia will subside and conditions remain at a general and commonsense level.

## [18] BIRDS IN NEW YORK.

How birds flying high overhead discover the patches of green deep down among the stones and cement I do not know, but every spring and every fall flocks of migrants find that miserable triangle which is gifted with the name of City Hall Park. The city gardeners have to replace its earth almost every year, but the birds seem not to mind. Juncos always find it, warblers often occasionally a catbird, a woodpecker, or a thrush; once or twice I have caught the gleam of a scarlet tanager's fiery coat on the grass among the English sparrows, as oblivious to the passing throngs as the multitudes were of it. And I shall never forget the thrill, of stepping out upon the roof of one of the Hudson Terminal buildings, twenty-two floors above the city, one crystal spring day, and hearing the whistle of a white-throated sparrow singing the same song which would rejoice the Adirondacks a month later. There they were a whole happy flock of them, flitting about three. windowboxes filed with..... privet. A white-throat by a woodland brook could never pipe so gratefully as there.

I have a little garden, literally within a stone's throw of Brooklyn Bridge. It is a very little garden, and the only garden in the block. But every year, spring and fall, the shy hermit thrushes find it, although I have never seen there one one of the familiar robins of less metropolitan backyards. Kinglets and brown creepers and warblers come too, usually a dozen or fifteen species every year. Once a big flicker landed in our single tree, but he plainly felt uncomfortable; hardly as perplexed, however, as a disillusioned sapsucker which I found flitting from cable to cable on the Brooklyn Bridge, now and again tapping at the stout iron cables as if in hope that one at least of that forest of upright trunks would turn out to be nourishing.
The birds that once sang on Murray Hill and along Minetta Brook are gone, but there are probably as many individual birds on Manhattan Island today as when Peter Stuyvesant was stumbling about. There are of course lower lipids. Migration of warblers to Central Rock are hundred and six by eight species of birds have been noted in

its three square miles. The number of birds that make it their home, however, is declining; it is outside the park that the census registers growth. In 1912 cardinals and goldfinches and wrens and wood thrushes still nested there, and twenty years ago the scarlet tanner found space enough to hide in. To-day these all-depart in June or do not come at all; only the robin, the flicker, and the gruckle, the Baltimore oriole, the yellow warbler, and the red-eyed vireo remain. Some kinds of birds have learned new habits and are most at ease in the city. Some, if-deed, which seem most rural are thoroughly dependent upon man. The barn swallow and the phobe nested somewhere before the white man supplied convenient barns and bridges, but there must have been few of them.

The English sparrow and the pigeon are of course blissfully at home in the midst of roaring traffic.... A few native birds, too, have learned to adapt themselves to urban life. The night-hawk finds the city roof a safer nesting-place than the bare hillsides and rocky pastures where it used to lay its moss-speckled eggs; the chimney swift his forgotten hollow trees and delights in forests of chimneys; and the little screech-owl, too, finds warm shelter among the chimney-pots.

And the gulls! The great bridges which reach skyward out of the tenement regions like city mountains provide ideal observatories from which to watch the gulls. Except for the breeding months, gulls are almost always visible high above any downtown yard. From the Brooklyn Bridge the harbour sometimes seems fairly snowy with them, lazily circling or ploddingly flapping, diving, soaking, eternally graceful; and no Indian ever sighted such flooks as the rich provender cast overboard by the ocean liners brings to New York to day. Each year seems to welcome an increase, and whereas a few years ago it was rare to see shy but hearing gulls from the bridge, one may hope to do not only for the great black-backed gulls and black-masked humping gulls, but with luck, for the little Bonaparte's for the ringbilled, or even the scallop plauous gull—Lewis's Gannett in The Century Magazine.
