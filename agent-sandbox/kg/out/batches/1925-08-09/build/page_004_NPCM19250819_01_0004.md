## [15] PLAIN FACTS.

It is not certain if the proposed meeting of farewell to H.E. the Governor before he leaves Hongkong is to take place. Sir Edward Stubbs's departure cannot be long delayed. He is due to receive certain addresses which have already been prepared and extensively signed. We trust the proposed meetings will not be abandoned but that the Colony will have an opportunity, in true British fashion, of voicing its feelings of appreciation of His Excellency's great services. Painting the lily is not an attracted pastive; therefore we desist from an occupation which has become frequent in regard to departing officials. Our desire for at least one farewell meeting is selfish—if this is the correct word to use. We desire to hear more of the representations which have been made to the Home Government and the steps taken if any, to bring order out of an unnecessary chaos. If we cannot hear all that has been told and

done, or contemplated, we can at least be told of some of the things, and, coming from His Excellency's own lips, this should give the assurance and confidence that the Colony seems to need. For the spirit of restlessness is abroad. Impatience is rife. There is a temptation to judge harshly, to speak unadvisedly, and to suggest extreme measures. The plain man cannot always grasp the ways of diplomacy; or see the sense of a policy of what he describes as drift. The issues are plain. They demand plain measures. If we had less of diplomacy and more of the courage of conviction and the sense of the righteousness of a cause, there would be no loss of time in right-ing wrings. It is along such lines as these that the individual is inclined to think; and the thinking expands with a continuance of silence. It may be that His Excellency has nothing to add to what he presumes is known, and feels no good would be done by reiterating it. This may be so, but a word from the Governor would have enormous effect, even if couched in the language of diplomacy. His "rose-water" methods have so far been successful. They were brought into force to make strikers and intimidators feel that Hongkong was not a safe place for them. It was not expected that in the larger issues they would have any effect except that of helping to stiffen the "stiff-necks" of the present Canton Government. In the larger spheres, the work of conciliation or of the use of force is in other hands. Can His Excellency tell us that they are in good hands, and that wildness of utterance—and action—should be curbed to the limit? His Excellency being a man of like passions will readily understand the plight of the plain man in attempting to understand conditions. He has heard of the plight of his nation as in Switton.

## [19] HOBBS.

The impetus which has been given to golf, bathing and other pastimes in Hongkong since the first strenuous weeks following the strike bespeaks the involuntary call for temporary forgetfulness in times of prolonged depressing circumstances. It was probably with something of the same sort of feeling that most of us turned from matters admittedly of more pressing importance to the news contained in yesterday's cable of the fine accomplishment of Jack Hobbs, the cricketer. By scoring two centuries for Surrey against Somerset he has beaten Dr. W. G. Grace's record of 126 centuries in first-class cricket. It does not necessarily follow, of course that he qualifies by reason of this record for a place of equal eminence in the history of batting to the great "W.G." who is looked upon as the "unapproachable," but apart from the question of relative merit with batsmen of past ages one thing is certain and that is that Hobbs must always rank with the great ones and that he is the greatest batsman of the day. Modest, quiet and typical when in action of the very poetry of batting he has become the idol of present day cricket, and the thousands who congest the tube from the City when there is a chance of seeing him at the wicket, also thousands of others to whom he is only a name, will join in congratulating him on carving an even more secure niche for himself in the ladder of fame. He has not, in the opinion of those best qualified to judge, played at a time to give him the chance of doing full justice to his exceptional powers. When he forced himself into the first place the great bowlers had either ceased to bowl or were yielding reluctantly to the claims of age, but from the manner in which he tackled the googlies he then received and in the light of his demonstrations since, there appears to be no reason for holding any belief other than that if he had played for Surrey in the closing years of the nineteenth century he would have treated the fine bowling he would then have had every whit as cavalierly as he does the more mediocre bowling of the present day. One is reminded in thinking of the way in which he deals almost as he likes with the best bowlers of the day of the story of Shaw and "W.G." Shaw was asked if he was the better bowler or "W.G." the better bat, and in his reply he gave a definition of a great batsman which will last for all time: "I put them where I like, and he puts them where he likes." In that he treats the very best balls delivered in that way and has every stroke at his command it may be said of Hobbs that he will assuredly go down to fame somewhere among such names as the great W.G. "Ranji," Victor Trumpar and Gilbert Jessop.

## [20] SHANGHAI TROUBLES

**DIPLOMATS RESUME DISCUSSION.**

**WHAT JAPAN ADVOCATES.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*Peking, August 19.*

The question of resuming discussions with the Chinese Government in regard to Shanghai, it is understood, has been before the diplomats' meeting this morning.

The Japanese Legation admits that Japan is amongst those advocating that a start be made, with the study and discussion of cognate matters (such as the Mixed Court, and Chinese representation on the Council), even though it may not have been decided in principle that the question fixing the actual responsibility for the events of May 30 be left for the determination of the Judicial Enquiry.

## [23] STINNES FAILURE.

**THE BANKS' LACK OF CONFIDENCE.**

*Berlin, August 18.*

A decided lack of confidence in young Edmund Stinnes is expressed in a statement by a group of banks liquidating the Stinnes concern, refusing to come to the rescue of the Aga Motor Works, while Edmund exercises any influence therein.

As regards general liquidation operations the banks state that one third of the entire indebtedness of the concern can be discharged without the necessity of a recourse to the open market.

## [28] PETAIN'S TASK.

**MOROCCO OFFENSIVE BEGUN.**

Marshal Petain is to arrive in Morocco shortly, to direct military operations in agreement with Marshal Lyautey and General Naulin, says the "Echo de Paris."

The French forces in Morocco at the end of August will number one hundred battalions.

The paper says it will be Petain's task to decide the time of the offensive, and it warns Petain that a reverse "will not be forgiven."

A report from Gibraltar states that General Primo di Rivera has arrived at Algeciras from Morocco, and is reported shortly to meet Marshal Petain at Malvy.

Transports are assembling at Algeciras to convey reinforcements to Africa.

Troops are expected from Madrid and Seville and other centres.

## [29] Socialists Oppose.

<sub>section under [28] PETAIN'S TASK.</sub>

The Socialist Party Congress unanimously decline responsibility for the “Moroccan adventure,” demanding peace at the earliest possible moment and recognition of and independence of the Riff; rectification of Riff frontiers in the latter's favour; but while rejecting credits for “Colonial brigandage” hopes for the evacuation of Morocco.

A Communist “workers’ enquiry delegation” is proceeding to Morocco to-morrow.

## [30] Speed and Efficiency.

<sub>section under [28] PETAIN'S TASK.</sub>

Marshall Petain is proceeding to Morocco after conferring with M. Painleve who made a statement that everything is being arranged with a view to taking the offensive at the utmost speed and efficiency, and the minimum of bloodshed.

Marshall Petain is expected to meet General Primo de Rivera at Algeciras to discuss the co-operation of the French and Spanish armies.

Paris, August 18.
A communique from Rubat says that the French attack on the Thouls front is developing favourably.

The enemy are resisting at certain points but are unable seriously to oppose the advance of four French columns.

Nevertheless the operations are merely a curtain-raiser to the main offensive beginning at the end of the month when General Petain returns.

Meanwhile the French aim to dislodge the Riffs from raiding posts, and safeguard communications all over Algeria, also politically, to influence neighbouring tribes.

## [31] First Leap Forward.

<sub>section under [28] PETAIN'S TASK.</sub>

### Paris, Luter

"As the result of this first leap forward the enemy is falling back northward," pursued by aeroplanes. According to a Fez message, referring to the Dsal operations and south thereof, the Ouledhair tribesmen have unconditionally surrendered.

## [32] Earlier Cables.

<sub>section under [28] PETAIN'S TASK.</sub>

*Paris, August 18.*

A communique from Fez tells of large scale operations begun on the 17th for the purpose of reducing the Dsul country. An army corps and a brigade commanded by General Bonchut are participating and aircraft are intensively engaged in. Riff centres.

Operations on a large scale, intended to reduce the Thouls country, have begun with the participation of an army corps and a mixed brigade under the command of General Bouchut. They opened early this morning by a heavy bombardment and are developing normally. The effect on the disorders is considerable and at several points they show a disposition to give up the struggle.
