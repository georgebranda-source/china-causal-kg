## [9] NO DEADLOCK.

**THE BELGIAN DEBT NEGOTIATIONS.**

**PRESIDENT COOLIDGE'S STATEMENT.**

After two hours conference at the Presidential homestead with Mr. Mellon and Senator Smoot who reported on the Belgian Debt negotiations, President Coolidge made a statement to pressmen that progress was satisfactory and that no deadlock had developed.

The conference is reassembling to-morrow when a final agreement is expected.

Reuter learns that a gigantic financial deal, probably the biggest since the war will presently be negotiated, whereby an Anglo-American syndicate, the names of the members of which are not disclosed, will purchase the entire property of Mannesmann Brothers in the Spanish zone of Morocco and Riff.

The vast property includes the harbour works of Alhucemas, and elsewhere, zinc, sulphur, and other mines and large tracts of agricultural land.

## [31] BOLSHEVIK MENACE.

### (Continued from Page 5.)

A reduction in the population of St. Petersburg from two millions before the war to 400,000 and 500,000 now and its ruined state were also referred to by the lecturer, the matter arising out of a question by the Hon. Mr. C. G. Alabaster.

In reply to another member of the audience who quoted the "China Mail" and asked the lecturer if he had heard the rumour with regard to the opening of the gates of Shameen, Mr. Hellstrom said that he had, but it was only a rumour. When the rumour had got about before the Bolsheviks had caused to be re-erected some kind of barrier outside the Chinese's Customs and had warned the Chinese that they were liable to be killed if they had anything to do with Europeans.

His reference to barricades was challenged by a member of the audience and the lecturer admitted that it was perhaps too strong a term to use in indicating duck and pig baskets near some newly laid cement although they had been strengthened somewhat after the rumour about the gates being opened. He did not think Shameen would be attacked by the Bolshevik Government as they preferred to kill trade rather than risk defeat by war.

The meeting applauded an answer given by the lecturer to Mr. M. Manuk who asked what remedy he would suggest for the present state of affairs. A voice interpolated with "British intervention" but Mr. Hellstrom answered that it was not for him to tell Britain how to put her own house in order.

Conditions in Swatow were referred to by Mr. A. Macgown of that port. He referred to the taxes imposed by whatever military leader happened to have control at the time and said that the taxes were collected by the Chinese Chamber of Commerce whose representatives were accompanied by military. Up to the present house rents (one method of taxation) had been collected three years in advance (ltr.) Mr. Macgown referred to the impossible treatment of the British Consul at Swatow who received no replies to his despatches to the Chinese representatives and was not received when he called personally. Examples were given of the intense anti-British feeling in existence there.

More questions were invited at the end and speaking from the audience, Sir Henry Pollock was received with applause when he remarked that the time for drift and inaction was past. A minority in Canton were defying treaties by which it was stipulated that Canton was an open port to Great Britain for trade not only with Canton but with the interior. He felt he was expressing the feelings of those present when he said that they would be grateful to the Committee of the Chamber of Commerce if they would at an early date call a public meeting with a view to definite action being suggested to the British Government.

Thanks to Mr. Hellstrom, the Chairman and the Hon. Mr. Holyoak for getting into touch with Mr. Hellstrom, concluded the meeting.

## [39] CORRESPONDENCE.

**SPECIAL CONSTABLES:**

(To the Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—There were some men (foreigners), brave and bold (?), who could but would not help in any capacity during the strike. Now these gentry are vexing sarcastic about the Special Constable's work, who, besides doing office work, had to go on picket duty. These men, (Specials) offered their services willingly and smilingly, without pay. I call such remarks as: "The Specials" are brave and useful, aren't they? Not half," and "When a shot is fired you will see the Specials run to the nearest tree for shelter" caddish and disgraceful. My only conclusion is that the speakers speak from experience. I presume that during the 1922 strike these men were enrolled as Specials and when danger threatened them they, brave men, funked!

Although we have not done anything extraordinary we deserve, I think, more than such sarcasm. We thank God that we have done, and are still doing, our "bit," which is more than what our satirists can say.

A SPECIAL CONSTABLE
Hongkong, August 18.
