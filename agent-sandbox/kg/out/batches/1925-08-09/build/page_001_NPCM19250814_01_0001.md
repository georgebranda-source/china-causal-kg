## [8] LONDON DIVORCE.

**WIFE'S LETTERS FROM HONGKONG.**

**HUSBAND'S CHARGES.**

"Concerning a wife eloped to China with her lodger and subsequently wrote in a repentant vein to her husband, a curious story was told in the Divorce Court," says the "News of the World" dated July 10. "Petitioner was Mr. Percy Laight, painter and decorator, of Beaconsfield Road, Stoke, Coventry. He alleged that his wife, Mrs. Eva Mary Laight, has committed misconduct with Mr. C. A. Sutherton Russ, a solicitor."

Mr. Cotes-Preedy, K.C., explained that the parties were married in 1904, and there were three children. When petitioner returned home from military service in 1920 he found that Mr. Sutherton Russ was a lodger in the house. He had then no suspicions that there was anything wrong between his wife and co-respondent.
“In August, 1921, however, Mr. Laight received a letter from a firm of Coventry solicitors, with whom Mr. Russ was then employed, and on going there was informed that his wife had accused him of cruelty, and that he must enter into a deed of separation. In the presence of his wife and Mr. Russ, Mr. Laight was persuaded to enter into a deed of separation.

"Afterwards Mrs. Laight and co-respondent left the house, and then it was that the husband's suspicions were aroused." He heard nothing more of his wife until he received a letter from her from Hongkong, in which she expressed her sorrow for being so wicked and heartless and saying she guessed petitioner had heard with whom she was. Later, Mr. Laight learned that Mr. Russ had obtained the steamship berths for respondent and the youngest son, whom she had taken with her. Mrs. Laight further wrote that she was a very unhappy woman, constantly thinking about the two sons she had left behind, adding, 'I was not mistress of myself,' and asking her husband's forgiveness.
"In sumport of his contention"

“In support of his contention that Mrs. Laight's letters from Hongkong inferred that she and Mr. Russ were living together, Mr. Cotes-Preedy read a letter she had written to a friend, in which she stated:

“In two years’ time we shall be rich, as we have these shares which will finish in 1925. Then we shall get £10,000, and there is Mr. Russ’s business.”

“Counsel added, with regard to the deed of separation, that Mr. Laight entirely repudiated the allegation that he had ever been cruel to his wife.”

“When Mr. Laight was giving evidence as to the deed of separation, Mr. Justice Hill asked: 'Is your wife much bigger than you?'”

"Petitioner: 'No, my lord."
"His Lordship: 'I was wondering why you did it."

“The jury found that respondent and co-respondent had committed misconduct and fixed damages at an agreed sum of $400.
“His Lordship therefore granted a decree nisi to Mr. Laight, with the damages.”

## [12] RENT INCREASE.

**COURT KEPT BUSY WITH DISTRAINTS.**

**HARDSHIP ALLEGED.**

Without expressing an opinion one way or the other, writes a correspondent to the "China Mail," there is certainly some distress in the matter of payment of rent during the last few weeks—a matter to which attention has again been drawn by the report in yesterday's issue of the latest action of the Chinese Tenants Association.

Every day, the writer maintains, the outdoor staff of the Supreme Court is busy attending to applications for distraint for rent. Being engaged in legal work he says that he knows for a fact that in normal times these applications seldom number more than four or five a day, but the number has now risen to about twenty a day.

A shroff, who is connected with the correspondent's client, has been to Court every day this week taking out distraints for rent in different parts of Hengkong.

The correspondent attributes this abnormal state of affairs to the hardship entailed by the poorest classes of Chinese who are hard put to live when food is so high. The number of instances where principal and sub-tenants have absconded wholesale will be surprising. With conditions as they are it is only to be expected that landlords have to take steps to recover back rent. "If, then," continues the writer, "the poor cannot pay back rent, how cfin they pay the increase of 15 per cent."

Yesterday the “China Mail” reported that the Chinese Tenants Association had decided to petition the Government with a view to deferring the enforcement of the new rents law whereby an increase of 15 per cent on the standard rent is permissible. The “Wah Kiu Yat Po” yesterday reported that a number of landlords had been stated as having waived the right to increase for the time being.
To-day it is mentioned that legal advice is being sought in drawing up the petition.

On inquiry the "China Mail" learns that the number of warrants issued is higher than normal. Amounts vary from trivial to fairly large sums. Some warrants have been executed after a few days' overlap and others when two or three months rent is outstanding.
In town this week were notified a good many small-size business establishments where the nation has been right to enter the market.

## [13] COOLIE'S JEERS.

**RICSHA MAN'S "CHNEK."**

That after refusing to take him as a fare, a Kowloon ricsha coolie hid behind a tree and jeered at him, was the allegation made by Mr. J. P. Robinson, the well-known resident and cricketer, before Mr. E. W. Hamilton at the Yaumati Police Court this morning when the coolie was fined $10 or 14 days.

The charge was one of refusing to accept a fare. Mr. Robinson came out of the Palace. Hotel about 1.45 p.m. yesterday and hailed the coolie who, sitting in his ricsha, refused. On Mr. Robinson's going over towards him, the coolie backed away, shouting, and pointing to the Hotel, indicating that he was engaged by a patron who had gone inside and was still waiting. Mr. Robinson then went inside where there was only one visitor who said that he did not have a ricsha waiting in the road. Mr. Robinson then insisted that the coolie should also go into the Hotel when his statement could be disproved but he hid behind the tree and jeered. He was handed over to an Indian police officer.
His Worship found the charge proved and imposed sentence as stated.

Bad Language Used.
Lieut. Col. E. D. Matthews, secretary of the Golf Club, successfully prosecuted in another case, the ricsha coolie being fined $5 or 7 days for using bad language.

Giving evidence, Colonel Matthews said that he engaged defendant's vehicle at the Kowloon "Star" Ferry wharf at 1 p.m. on August 4 to go to Humphrey's Building. In Mody Road defendant went very slowly and wandered from one side to the other of the road. Witness told him to "Faitee" (Hurry Up!) whereupon defendant turned round and used the words complained of.

Defendant denied the charge. His version was that when his fare told him to get a move on, he turned round and answered "Ho" (Good).

His Worship (Mr. E. W. Hamilton) said that defendant was lying as there was all the difference between the monosyllabic "Ho" and the epithets which Colonel Matthews had heard.
His Worship also remarked that it was lucky for defendant that the/ Colonel had taken his number and applied for a summons otherwise he (defendant) might have been worse off.

## [17] TO MEET STORAGE CHARGES.

The Curtis biplane, which Mr. H. W. Abbott brought from Manila in March of this year and has since been lying partially submerged off the Empress wharf at the Kowloon Godowns, was sold by auction this morning on the instructions of the Pacific Main Steamship Company, which is winding up its affairs here. Instructions for sale were given in order to defray cost of storage.

The biplane was the one which broke from its moorings at Manila and was recovered slightly damaged after many hours drifting out to sea. Mr. Abbott, who was in Manila at the time, bought it with the intention of demonstrating the practicability of instituting a passenger and mail service between Hongkong and Shanghai, but since its arrival it has received no attention; and, although the fabric showed little signs of deterioration at the sale this morning, the hull and other parts were not in the best condition. Since one of the recent "blows" the machine has remained partially capsized.

The chief value lay in twin 12 cylinder engines, Liberty motors, which are tremendously powerful, for the 'plane was designed to carry sixteen or eighteen. These motors will naturally be very expensive to run.

The sale of the plane this morning was conducted by Mr. L. E. Lammert, of Lammert Bros. Mr. Rowe and Mr. A. Wing, the latter of whom salvaged the Heung Shan, were the only bidders, and the price advanced from $550 to $650, at which figure the plane was bought by Mr. Rowe. The sale was subject to the restrictions on removal from the Colony in connection with which permission will have to be obtained from the Superintendent of Imports and Exports.
