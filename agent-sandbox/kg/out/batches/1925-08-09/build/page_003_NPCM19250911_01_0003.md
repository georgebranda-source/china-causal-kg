## [1] CHEFOO SCHOOL.

*(From Our Own Correspondent.)*

Since I last wrote the China Inland Mission Schools have held their Exhibitions and Closing Exercises, the summer holidays have come and gone, and now the children are back at work again, the Schools having re-opened yesterday, the 1st Inst.

The Preparatory School held their Exhibition and Prize Distribution on the afternoon of Monday, July 27, when the Prepites gave the crowded audience of parents and friends an excellent entertainment, all, the kiddies being well up to their parts, alike in the songs, recitations, and pianoforte solos. Mr. Webb Peploe was in the Chair, and Mrs. Botham gave away the prizes to the fortunate ones. The conduct watch was won by Grace Harlow; and in the form prizes Ernest Oldfield carried off the 1st in the Upper 1; Grace Henderson-Smith in the Lower 1; Stanley Rist in the Transition, and Beatrice Browne in the Kindergarten.

The C.I.M. Memorial Hall was again crowded on July 28 for the Girls' School's Exhibition, the Chair being taken promptly at 4.30 p.m. by Mr. G. Findlay Andrew, O.B.E. The programme which followed was on the classical side, and being admirably rendered by the girls was thoroughly enjoyed by all present. At the end of the entertainment the Chairman asked Mrs. Andrew to come up and distribute the prizes. All girls securing 75 per cent. for their year's work are entitled to form prizes, and sixteen girls received these. Helen Smith, of Form III, secured the highest per cent. in the School, gaining 90 per cent., and received a special round of applause, when she went to receive her prize. The conduct watch, the most prized award of the year, was awarded to Kathleen Davies.

The Boys' School's Exhibition took place the following afternoon, the 29th, when there was again a large crowd present to see what the Boys could do. No one was disappointed as the Boys put up an extremely good, though rather short, programme, which was much enjoyed by all. Mr. Squire made an amusing and capable Chairman, and at the close of the first part of the entertainment Mrs. Squire very graciously gave away the prizes. Form Prizes in the Boys' School are also awarded to those who during the year have secured an average of 75 per cent. or over on their school work, and thirteen boys secured same, apart from four others who received special awards as they gained their per cent. for two terms, but were away sick during the third. The conduct watch was won by Conyers Hewett, who came forward to receive the much prized trophy amid tremendous applause and enthusiasm on the part of the boys and the audience in general. Those present then adjourned to the School Quadrangle for the Exhibition of Drill, which is always one of the principal features in the Boys' Break-up. The Drill this year was well up to the usual excellent standard, and Mr. G. P. Welch is to be congratulated for the time, trouble and hard work he must have put in to bring it up to such a high pitch.

## [3] ANOTHER SLANDER.

**WNRTHAND RUSSELL'S "PALSEN CHARGES"**

**STRONO SHANGHAI COMMENT**

The weary task of trying to overtake the shanders on Shanghai and its British community published abroad brings us into conflict with an article by Mr. Bertrand Russell in the "Nation and Athenæum" of July 18, says the "North China Daily News." No sensible person in China wastes time, under ordinary conditions, in replying to Mr. Russell, whose views of this country would appear to be formed entirely by what confirmation he could get for his own prejudices out of the least trustworthy elements in the student body. But when the "Nation and Athenæum" publishes an article by him, it cannot go unnoticed. In passing, it is much to be regretted that a paper of such standing should have opened its columns to a contributor so equivocal. This is what we find Mr. Russell saying:—

With regard to the Shanghai shooting, some of the facts are still in debate, others are now generally admitted. The following facts are not in dispute: the crowd outside the police station was unarmed; no notice was given of the intention to fire upon them; the order to fire was given in English, and therefore not understood by most of the crowd; the firing began ten seconds after the order was given; many of those who were hit were shot in the back, showing that they were trying to disperse, "but were not giving time to do so. Other crowds were fired upon during the next six days. Altogether about seventy people were killed, with a proportionate number of wounded. Every non-British person, and almost every British missionary, who has spoken about the affair has pronounced that the British authorities were not justified in their action. Dr. J. W. Cline's formerly head of the missionary college at Soochow, who saw the whole thing, says: "I was not expecting to see the police fire, was shocked when they did fire, and have been sorry about it ever since."

## [4] AN ABOMIX ABLE LIE

Who started the abominable lie that “about seventy people were killed”? We met it in Mr. Sherwood Eddy's slanderous article with which we dealt last week and naturally, as it was in the air, Mr. Bertrand Russell, with his peculiar preclivity for saving everything that he can to the detriment of his own country—one imagines, with some idea of enchancing his own self-importance—has sniffed it out with ghoulish delight. The facts are given in the police report published in the Municipal Gazette of August 6, and, as we said before, the police would be very careful not to give anyone a chance of tripping them up in such a matter. Here they are:

Attacks by the strikers or their sympathizers resulted in the death of two Chinese and one foreigner. The Police in the suppression of the riots killed 22 Chinese and wounded 36. These figures include rioters who lost their lives on May 30, on Nanking Road. Further efforts of the Police to suppress lawlessness led to the arrest of 92 persons for rioting or assault, the apprehension of 22 others for tearing down Municipal Proclamations, and the prosecution of 35 persons for disseminating inflammatory strike and anti-foreign literature. All of these, with a few exceptions, and 34 other persons charged for other offences arising out of the strike, were extremely leniently dealt with, either by being cautioned, fined or imprisoned for short periods.

## [5] 'FALSE.'

As regards the evidence, such as it is, which Mr. Russell's addences, the statement about "almost every British missionary," etc. is as false as that about the deaths. Of Dr. Cline's evidence, it is felt by many persons that his position in the crowd was not at all such as to justify the vehemence of his comment. Of Mr. Harry Westridge's testimony, not one word is said.

## [10] VISITORS AT HOTELS.

Yet Mr. Westridge was unquestionably the most important witness of all. He was nearest to the actual shooting; fifteen years' residence in the interior of China for the China Inland Mission had brought him more than once into contact with Chinese mobs and taught him what Chinese mob psychology is; and with all this to give weight to his words, he said emphatically, in the Mixed Court trial of the students, that he considered the police could have done nothing but what they did and that, in their place, he would have felt compelled to do the like. But testimony of this kind does not suit the purpose of men like Mr. Bertrand Russell and Mr. Sherwood Kiddy.

## [16] COLOURED STATRMENT8

In another part of the article Mr. Russell recommends his renders to obtain a little pamphlet called "China's Case," if they "wish to see how the matter appears to eminent Chinese intellectuals." We have seen it; and we deeply regret that men of the standing of Mr. V. K. Ting and Mr. Lo Wen-kan should have been among those who put their names to it. The internal evidence of the pamphlet shows that the writers must have had before them the full versions of both sides, including the verbatim report of the Mixed Court trial mentioned above and they have carefully suppressed every scrap of evidence which told against the students' version. When "eminent Chinese intellectuals" are capable of such dishonesty, what can be expected of the uneniment? What reliance can be placed on any Chinese testimony?
