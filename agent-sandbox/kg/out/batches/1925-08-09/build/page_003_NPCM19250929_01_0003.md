## [1] BOME PEOT

**JARDINE STEAMER'S ESCAPE.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*Shanghai, Sept. 27.*

When the Indo-China Steam Navigation Company's steamer "Tuckwo," which arrived at Hankow this afternoon from Shanghai, was about two miles above Wuhu on the evening of the 25th inst. a violent explosion, accompanied by smoke, occurred in the Chinese third-class accommodation. Anti-fire measures were taken and no fire resulted, the ship continuing on her voyage.

An enquiry into the cause of the explosion resulted in the finding of a, cunningly-contrived bomb, the cover of which was the outside container of a Thermos flask, which had been placed in an innocent-looking "box. According to the story of the passenger coolie concerned, the box was brought aboard at Chinkiang by a Chinese who left the ship at Nanking and did not return.

Only slight damage was done by the explosion, two Chinese passengers being slightly injured and two bunks wrecked. There had obviously been a determined and well-planned attempt to wreck the ship, which might have had most serious results had the bomb exploded elsewhere than under the wide airshaft, which gave the flame access to the open air and dissipated the force of the explosion. This combined with the excellent behaviour of the officers and crew, tended to allay any tendency to panic on the part of the passengers.

## [2] PEACE EFFORTS.

*(Router's Service.)*

The Government is working hard to preserve peace and to dispel war rumours. Wu Kuanghsin has returned from a visit to Feng Yu-hsiang and is now variously reported to be visiting Yueh Wei-chun and Sun Chuan-fang. Yao Chen, the special delegate to Honan, has wired saying that Yueh Wei-chun has assured him of his peaceful intentions. Teng Han-hsiang, after interviewing Li Ching-lin and obtaining his peaceful assertions has gone on to see Hsiao Yao-nan.
The Government is reported to have instructed Sun Pao-chi, who is at present recuperating at Hangchow, to see Sun Chuan-fang and urge him to keep the peace. Sun Pao-chi is reported to have wired advocating Sun Chuan-fang and Yang Yu-ting to meet to discuss their differences informally at Shanghai.

## [3] FRANCE'S DEBTS.

*(Router's Service.)*

While Washington despatches are optimistic in regard to the prospect of the Franco-American debt settlement, Paris is doubtful and some papers even anticipate the failure of the negotiations. The despatches from French correspondents at Washington, however, speak of the new French proposals as possibly providing for initial annual payments of thirty instead of twenty-five million dollars, increasing in a decade to a maximum of approximately a hundred million, payable in fifty-two years instead of ninety. The Americans are now said to be ready to agree to maximum annuities of 120 million dollars. M. Caillaux is said to have assured Mr. Mellon that no payment would be made to Britain and no Franco-British arrangement will be concluded without identical payment and arrangement with America. The incident of the loan to the Bank of France is reported to be settled.

## [4] CHINA FLOODS

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*Peking, Sept. 28.*

The Secretary of the China International Famine Relief Committee reports a further break on the main south dyke of the Yellow River, 10 miles west of Shihlipu. It resulted in the worst flood of the river since 1887.

The effect of the first break on August 13, which was announced some time ago, was only to flood an area of approximately 400 square miles between the inner and outer dykes. The later disaster resulted in the unrestricted flow of the river southward, where part thereof may reach the Yangtsze through the Grand Canal.

There is a bare possibility of the river entirely changing its course.

The Executive Secretary, Mr. Mallory, who with the chief engineer, Mr. Todd, has just returned from an inspection trip, announces that probably 1,500 square miles are already under water and 2,000,000 people more or less are affected. The Autumn crops are destroyed and probably hundreds drowned. Every hour the flooded area is increasing and the flow of the river is the only limiting factor to the extent of the catastrophe. The C.I.F.R.C. Shantung Committee has opened relief work with Yuncheng as the centre. Steps are being taken to enlarge the scope of these activities as need increases.

## [5] BETTER BUSINESS.

**HONGKONG AT WEMBLEY.**

Scores of letter- and verbal requests are being received daily by the Wembley officials asking whether the Wembley exhibition will go on next year in spite of the definite official statement that the gates will close for good at the end of October, states the "China Express and Telegraph."
Malaya and Hongkong have never looked more animated than during the last few weeks. A Hongkong official declared that much better business had been done lately and that every hope is entertained for a continuance of the same until the pavilion closes. As was the case last year, the Hongkong section closes a month earlier than the general Exhibition, this step having been decided on principally in order that the Chinese employees may leave for home before the cold weather sets in. The Commissioner and Mrs. Flallifax were "at home" at the Pavilion on September 22.

## [6] CHINA SUGAR."

**EFFECT OF TEMPORARY STOP PAGE.**

From a letter appearing in the “Times” from a Robe reader it would appear that the stoppage of the British sugar refineries in China has created a demand for the Japanese product in Manchuria in the boxcott, and in other Eastern countries.

The increased export trade in Japanese goods such as cottons and manufactured foodstuffs is also reported to have reacted favourably on the value of the yen. Japanese grey shootings, again, are reported to have made considerable inroads into the trade with East African ports.

Commenting on the statement with regard to sugar, the "China Express" and Telegraph" thinks that the Taikoo and Jardine concerns in Hongkong are those particularly referred to: The news from Japan certainly indicates activity, of which British interests, seriously affected by the deadlock in China, should take note, it states. Hongkong is not afraid of Japan as far as sugar is concerned. The market lost is not easy to regain. The remark applies equally to shirting and shipping.

## [7] LORRY DEATH

The Chinese driver of a motor lorry belonging to Messrs. A. S. Watson and Co., Ltd. was before the Central Magistrate (Mr. S. B. B. McElderry) yesterday on a charge of manslaughter, and was defended by Mr. M. K. Lo.
The accident occurred at Wellington Street where the lorry moved backward down a slope and ran over a pavement, fatally injuring a fortune teller who was seated there. The driver had left the vehicle at the time.

Sergeant Saunders gave evidence as to the condition in which he found the lorry after the accident. He said that both hand and foot brakes were in working order, but he considered that on an incline such as the scene of the accident the application of the hand brake alone, as was evident from his examination, would not be sufficient.

The case was adjourned.

## [8] BLOOD GIVEN.

**MAN WHO FELT PAIN WHEN PATIENT DIED.**

Nearly three years ago Frederick George Lee, an ex-regimental sergeant-major, now 34, was asked at a labour bureau if he would allow some of his blood to be transfused to a girl of 10 at the Middlesex Hospital, Mortimer Street, W.

“I’ve kiddies of my own,” he replied, promptly agreeing to the request.

The subsequent operation was the first of 25 of a similar nature to which Mr. Lee has submitted since November 1922, there being a chance in every case of saving the patient's life.

He has given 36 pints of his life-blood to others, and at least 17 persons owe their lives to him and to the Middlesex Hospital surgeons in whose care he has placed himself.

In cases where transfusion has not been successful in saving the life of a patient Mr. Lee has had a curious knowledge of the fact. "Should my patient die," he said, "I know at once, for I feel a severe pain in my arm."

Mr. Lee has been ordered a rest, and for two months is not to be allowed to give his blood to patients.

“We want at least 20 volunteers of equal fitness,” a horse surgeon stated.

"They must not have had malaria or serious blood diseases; otherwise, any fit man or woman up to 50 is capable of acting as a donor. Volunteers should write to the secretary of the Middlesex Hospital."

In the course of his speech, Mr. Ponsonby asked the Government to give the House some information in regard to China. The problem was one that might smoulder until it blazed up into something extremely dangerous. His Majesty's Government were showing a sort of spineless inertia, and were taking no action. They seemed to have an incorrigible habit of allowing situations, whether at home or abroad, to come to a head before they could be persuaded to move at all. He thought that the suggestion that the disturbances in China were due to Bolshevik propaganda had been disproved. To say that the movement was due to Communist agitation was far critical in the extreme. It was the culmination of events that had been growing in China ever since the Western exploitation of that country in 1840. The breaking point had been reached in the way the Chinese had been treated as inferior people and exploited by the Western peoples. If the Western Powers wanted to deal honestly by China; it would not be long before a strong Government arose in that country. We were throwing Russia into the arms of Asia; we were entranging the whole of the vast population of China; and were raising up a sort of suspicious feeling against Japan. The unfortunate action of to-day might culminate in great catastrophes years hence.
In reply to Mr. Ponsonby, Mr. R. McNeil said that Russian intrigue was creating great difficulties and making much missionary and unhelpful, however, believe that in the long run it would do any material damage to the British Empire. (Ministerial cheers).

As regarded the attitude of the British Government there was not the slightest foundation for Mr. Ponsonby's charge that the Government were showing supine inertia. From the first moment the troubles developed, the Government had been very active in trying to bring about a wise settlement of the existing troubles, and to devise and encourage and initiate as far as they could a wise policy dealing with the subject of our relations with China and the internal conditions of that country. He agreed that the condition of affairs in China was not due to Bolshevik propaganda, but he did think that Bolshevik propaganda had been fishing in troubled waters. It had been exasperating tempers and making more mischief where there was mischief to work on at the beginning, and he had very little doubt that it was very largely due to Bolshevik propaganda that, anti-foreign hostility had largely concentrated its animosity upon this country rather than upon others. Chinese interests and British interests there were identical. Trade could only flourish in an atmosphere of peace and confidence, and it was obviously to our interest that China should enjoy those blessings. In spite of all the existing difficulties, he did not think we should be pessimistic with regard to the future of China, and as to what we could do there. He hoped that the coming conference would improve the whole of the foreign relations with China. He did not believe that the present Chinese feeling of animosity against this country would continue.
