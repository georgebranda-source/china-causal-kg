Chinese
KONGMOON INCIDENT.

Owners

This morning he was the plaintiff in a case in the Summary Court. He sued his owners, the Cheat Wo Steamship Company, for $1,000 (salary and value of personal property loss).

The facts, as disclosed in evidence, were "that the ship (the Leung Kwong) was ordered from Hongkong to Kongmoon by her owners on July 9. On arrival there everything was apparently quiet, but the crew deserted. The master asked a British ship in port to stand by, but the reply came back regretting inability so to do and the ship in question left for Hongkong.

Mr. Wilson flew his distress signal but, in spite of there being a British gunboat in the harbour, no response was forthcoming. Shortly after the purser came aboard and told the master that his life was in danger and that he had better leave the ship. As there was no food aboard and no crew, the master took his papers and ship's ammunition to the Customs House and returned with his officers to Hongkong.

Cross-examined by Mr. D. McCallum, who represented the owners, Mr. Wilson said that he engaged European officers and received $700 a month with which to pay them.

## [18] LAW AND JUSTICE.

**MR. BIRD DRAWS A DISTINCTION.**

**BUILDING LIABILITIES**

### -

The Supreme Court “building” case in which the award of the Hon. Mr. H. W. Bird as an arbitrator is questioned, drew to a close before Sir Henry Cowper Gollan yesterday, and judgment was reserved.

Mr. Jenkin, for the plaintiff, who asks that the award should be set aside, said yesterday afternoon that his case was that Mr. Bird's award was made with every intention of doing justice as between man and man, but that in so doing he allowed himself to be carried away by the usual practice instead of abiding by the contract.
Mr. Bird: I may be wrong in law but I am quite sure my award was just.

The Hon. Mr. C. G. Alabaster, closing his case for the contractor in whose favour Mr. Bird had found, argued that it was no reason for upsetting an award to state that an arbitrator did not consider a point which was not taken. The arbitrator had made up his mind at the time of the arbitration but he had said that it was a mind open to consider any fresh arguments which might be raised.

In his final address to His Lordship, Mr. Jenkin said that it was impossible to say that there had been no miscarriage of justice when an arbitrator took his judicial seat and then said, "Now look here, this matter is decided and unless you have further light to shed upon it I shall decide against you."
