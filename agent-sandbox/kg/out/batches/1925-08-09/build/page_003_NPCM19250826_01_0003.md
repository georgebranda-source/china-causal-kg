Sir Henry Pollock's Opinion.
Sir Henry Pollock again quoted the Home Foreign Secretary and the London Times" in support of his contention that the British Government had no excuse for stating that they were not cognisant with the gravity of the situation here and instanced the factors leading to the commencement of the second China War, the tearing down and trampling under foot by Chinese officials of the British flag on a boat named the "Arrow."

We ought to be able to rely on our Navy; in conjunction with our military forces, to protect our very important rights of trading with the Chinese at the Treaty ports and in trading through the Treaty ports with the Interior of China. (Applause). He was amazed that the British Government had not yet come to the relief of the Colony.

On the proposition and through the willingness of the Chairman and seconder, it was determined on the proposition of the Rev. J. H. Johnston to amend the resolution to read as follows:—

Accordingly, the residents of this Colony at a great mass indignation meeting, demand that the British Government immediately adopt a drastic policy, as we venture to suggest, in the following ultimatum to the Canton authorities."
Mr. J. Russell urged that they should include in the telegram that power should be given to the local Government to deal with the situation.

After some further discussion the resolution, as amended, was put to the meeting and carried amid loud and prolonged cheers.

Swatow Volce.

The next official speaker from the platform was Mr. Macgown of Swatow who said he had been specially requested by the members of the British Chamber of Commerce in Swatow to represent them and to associate the British community in Swatow with any resolutions sent to the Home Government by Hongkong. He quoted articles of treaties with China which, he said, had been completely and absolutely violated not only in the spirit and letter but in the practice of them.

The illegal Bolshevist Government of Canton had put into force a very effective embargo on all trade to and from Hongkong, and in Swatow all British and Japanese interests are boycotted. "What about it?" asked Mr. Macgown. Are we to accept these conditions without protest? (Cries of "No.") The trouble started in Canton and must be settled with Canton, Canton is quite separate and independent of the Central Government of Peking.

"Swatow has been badly treated and unless the Reds are kicked out there is no possible chance of the resumption of trade as before. The loss of this trade will hit the Colony of Hongkong very hard, harder than many realise. British shipping interests are very large in Swatow, while large quantities of British goods are imported yearly. All trade has now stopped. I have much pleasure in supporting this telegram to the Prime Minister, and I hope prompt action will be taken by the Home authorities to stop this reign of terror in South China. I should like to say that if any attack is made on Canton, a similar attack should be made on Swatow because unfortunately, Swatow is in Kwangtung and just now entirely under Red rule. (Applause).

Shameen And Foochow.
Mr. C. E. Peacock, from Shameen, said that all he could tell the meeting, so far as the situation there was concerned was that "we are still cooking our own food, doing our own washing, cleaning our own house, and doing no business." He also referred to the article with reference to Sir James Jamieson and termed as "grudging" a retraction of some points which had afterwards been published. A letter from a Shameen merchant pointing out the Shameen point of view and stating that they were not looking for a scapegoat had not been published.

The speaker said that the article in question had been referred to by Mr. C. C. Wu, "probably one of the ablest men in the Canton administration." In a situation of this kind he thought the greatest care should be exercised by the Hongkong newspapers in regard to what they published (hear, hear). "Indications of disunion, as these, merely give confidence to the other side and make our battle all the harder."

The Chairman then read a telegram from the Chairman of the Foochow Chamber of Commerce, in which it was stated that the residents of Foochow desired to be associated with the telegram to be sent Home. The telegram also stated the Foochow residents were prepared to share expenses.

At this point a gentleman moved to the centre of the platform from the rear who, although he slurred his words, must have been inaudible except to those in the immediate vicinity, was very much in earnest and was applauded when he retired. He had just come from Shameen, he said, and he could say that it was with difficulty provisions could be obtained. He had been all-round the world and found that Britain was the most hated nation in the world. That was because she was too lenient with the people with whom she had to deal. This was the richest Colony Britain had got, he continued, and why should they not give it protection. We all knew that the British lion was slow to act but why could it not put out its paw and protect Hongkong?

Coast Officers' Voice.
Mr. T. T. Laurenson, of the China Coast Officers' Guild, was the next speaker. On behalf of the mercantile marine of the China Coast, masters and officers, on Thursday afternoon last, a telegram had been sent, he said, to Secretaries at Home, urging them to make every effort with the Imperial Merchant Service, the Marine Engineers' Association; and the Navigators and General Insurance Company to persuade the Imperial Government to take immediate action with regard to Canton and China generally. Further delay, it was pointed out, might be ruinous, and have disastrous effect on the mercantile marine. That telegram was sent by the mercantile marine who he would venture to say, were never behind, and were the first line of defence, after the Royal Navy. (Loud cheers).

Mr. H. Somerset Fitzroy, a local barrister, then took the platform. He rose, he said, to express his disagreement with the latter part of the telegram.

When we get down to what this meeting proposes to send to the Home Government what does it amount to? It demands that the British Government deliver an ultimatum at once to the Canton authorities. (Applause). Have you stopped for one moment to think what that means? A Voice: "No time to stop." (Laughter and applause). You are recognising a belligerent. Can you point out any time in the history of the British nation when it has recognised a two-penny halfpenny belligerent? Take your thoughts back to the civil war when the South was fighting against the North, when the whole of that Continent was in the great throes of war. Did the British Government recognise them? Not a bit of it. You have a small community, whom you say are acting contrary to Peking. You have your treaties with China. Where is the proper place to go? The place where the Foreign Office is going at the present moment. There is one, and only one channel through which we can deal and that is through the diplomatic channel of Peking.
A voice: “Go up the West River and see what Peking can do.” (Loud cheers).

Mr. Fitzroy: The next thing you ask the Home Government to do is to turn out the Bolsheviks. I do not know the exact meaning of that. Perhaps some of you mean the Chinese Bolsheviks, but at any rate what you are asking is that the whole of these people shall be turned out. I think I can hear in that an echo of the ultimatum which was delivered to Serbia in 1914 by Austria. (A Voice: "Nonsense"). It is the same sort of thing that you want to destroy—the sovereignty of a nation and take it by force. Very well, how do you propose to do this when you have sent your ultimatum? (A Voice: "Send a telegram.") (Laughter). Exactly, but that is not what we are discussing at the present moment. We are sending a telegram in which we are usurping the functions of the Home Government, in which we say the Home Government are totally incapable of doing their job despite the fact that we are told they are trusted by everybody. We propose to do it by establishing a blockade by naval forces of Canton. (Hear, hear). Does it ever occur to you that somebody else has something to say besides Great Britain? Europe would be ablaze in 20 minutes after this went through. "Then let us have a blaze." "You are not discussing the matter but asking a series of questions" were the only intelligible of many remarks from the audience at this point.

Mr. Fitzroy, continuing, referred to incidents transpiring in the South American republic of Venezuela in 1902 when measures similar to those advocated, at the meeting were instituted. What was the result? Theodore Roosevelt was the President of America at the time and within three or four days the American fleet was sent to Venezuela. The British ambassador at Washington had to state that the developments there were unauthorised.

In conclusion, the speaker said that he thought they should leave whatever action was considered necessary to the Home Government who had a far greater knowledge of the situation than Hongkong had (Dissent).

The Chairman remarked that the last speaker had entirely overlooked the explanation he had made that while they made some suggestions with regard to the form intervention should take, they, of course, fully realised that the form of such intervention was solely a matter for the decision of His Majesty's Government. (Applausion). That meeting was not presuming to dictate to the Home Government what they should do, but was simply venturing to voice the feeling of the Colony that something must be done and done quickly. (Applause). The diplomacy of Peking had so far absolutely and entirely failed and it was not realised that there was a problem in the south.

Mr. Best, then mounted the platform from the body of the hall and alleged inaccuracies in Mr Fitzroy's statements regarding the Venezuela trouble. Cleveland was President, he said, not Roosevelt and the trouble had been over a gold field and boundary problems.
