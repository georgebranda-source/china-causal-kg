## [9] ACTRESS

**COMING ATTRACTION**

How many foreigners living in the Far East have ever taken the trouble, on have even had the opportunity, to make the acquaintance of the Chinese theatre and Chinese acting?

Amongst those of the more venturesome sort who have penetrated into Chinese localities, the impression has generally been a confused mixture of noise, glare and discomfort.

Now we in Hongkong are to have opportunity to-morrow week of seeing, under comfortable conditions, the one actress in China who, by her personality and wonderful command over her art, can take her place on the same plane as the famous Mei Lan-fang.

Miss Pik Wen-ha is coming to the Theatre Royal in response to numerous requests from those who have paid a visit to the Taiping Theatre and have been impressed by her wonderful acting. Having come under the spell of her charm themselves, they wish others of the foreign community, who do not care to go to West Point and sit through the long hours of a complete Chinese play to have the opportunity of seeing for themselves just so much of the wonderful acting of this charming actress as will give them an insight into a world undreamed of and unexplored by the great majority of foreigners in the Far East.

The usual Chinese play is a matter of hours, and often days. What Miss Pik Wen-ha is going to do is to select scenes from three of her most famous plays, representing tragedy and farce, finishing up with a scene from a comedy which introduces a famous dance composed for the beautiful Yang Kuei-fei, the favourite concubine of the Tang Ming Emperor, of the Tong Dynasty.

These scenes will take about thirty minutes each, and in between, in the 15 minute intervals, the audience will have another surprise. We all have an idea that we know what Chinese music is—a mixture of discordant and ear-splitting noises accompanied by the banging of drums and the beating of cymbals. On this occasion we shall be introduced to a form of Chinese music that, except to those foreigners who are privileged to be admitted to the real Chinese life, is absolutely unknown to us. A really wonderful orchestra of stringed instruments, played by leading Chinese amateurs, will give a selection of old and most interesting Chinese music. Malbourne, Nov. 14.

A record poll is assured at to-day's election owing to the compulsory voting.

Mr. S. M. Bruce's final appeal to the electors, particularly the women, declares:

"The paramount issue is the maintenance of the supremacy of constitutional government."

Though bitter class-feeling has been aroused the campaign has generally been very orderly.

The main points of Mr. Bruce's platform are a promise of national insurance legislation, insistence on secret ballots in trade unions before a strike is decided upon; a proposal to subsidize road construction in order to facilitate the transport of farm produce, and a proposal that Parliament vote twenty millions to build cottage homes for city workers.

The Labour Party devoted its energies to denouncing the Red menace as an election bogey, declaring the constitutional Labour Party is more qualified to deal with extremists than the reactionaries.

Both sides especially appeal to the hitherto unpolled non-Party electors, the Ministerialists asking for a mandate to deal with alleged dangerous elements in the community and the Labourites warning the electors not to be influenced by side issues.

## [14] IT BEGAN IN GLASGOW.

Concert Artist's Strange Experience.

Miss Catherine Leadbetter, a well-known English concert artist, recently related the following personal experience to a reporter who culled at her home, 5 Fernshaw Road, in the Southwest of London.

"A few years after I became a professional musician," stated Miss Lead-better. "I began to suffer from an ailment which alarmed me. I was then fulfilling a four months' engagement at Glasgow, as concert artists, and had to take part in six concerts each week.

"My illness began in a curious manner, my hair coming off in patches. This left my head sore, and I suffered most severely from piercing headache. My nerves were highly strung. I became weaker and weaker until to walk upstairs took my breath away. I had to discontinue my music practice for a considerable while, the strain and nervousness becoming overwhelming. I had cold after cold, followed by an exhausting cough.

“The weakness of my body increased until I felt too tired to go on the stage. My singing voice and my violin playing were for a while affected, and that increased my distress. For four months I continued in that painful and alarming condition. Later on I went to my parents' home in Sheffield, and rested. I was a little better for a while, but last Christmas there came on me a very severe development of the same weakness and suffering. The cough grew worse, and I became more nervous than ever.”
