## [1] MISCHA LEVITZKI.

**WORLD FIGURE AT THEATRE ROYAL.**

**TO-NIGHT'S CONCERT.**

### 답변

To-night at the Theatre Royal Hongkong has the opportunity of hearing a young man who has come to be regarded as a great figure in the pianistic world,—Mischa Levitski. From an obscure Russian boy, then a student of tender years showing exceptional talent, M. Levitzki has risen to the position of a great artist, being claimed as such by thousands everywhere.

At the great festival which was held recently in New York under the auspices of the Beethoven Society, to which all the great artists of the world belong, Mischa Levitzki was one of the two chosen to perform on that evening, the other being the great interpreter, Heifetz, and then as on all occasions his thoughtful and intellectual interpretations completely enthralled his audience.

Writing of Mischa Levitzki in the "Shanghai Times," "Tomasso" has the following comments to make:—

Two nights ago Mischa Levitzki played to a select company at the home of Mr. Strok, under whose management he appears in the East, and the impression he made then will last until he returns to give three concerts in November after a tour of India and Java where his arrival is, eagerly awaited.

I do not want here to dilate on the artistry of this young, debornair yet thoughtful interpreter of the world's greatest musical composers for the piano, but this I will say: Some time ago I took occasion to call the Shanghai lander's love of music to task, and for this act was overwhelmed with an avalanche of wrath which, somehow, affected me very little.

I still retain, the impression I had then, but will willingly deny the stability of it if Shanghai will show to Mischa Levitzki a tenth of the appreciation New York alone has shown to him since his debut there less than a decade ago when, for all his immature years, he showed the strength and vigour of a Hoffman, almost the brilliancy of a Paderewski and all the grace and delicacy of a Percy Grainger, the greatest musician which my own country, Australia, gave to the world.

## [7] MOSQUITO SCOURGE

The invaluable work and pressing needs of the Ross Institute for Tropical Diseases, Putney Heath, moves Home newspapers to eulogise a fresh the research work of Sir Ronald which resulted in the probing of the secret of the malarial parasite and in this connection the following article is of particular interest.

A very dangerous creature is that lady anopheles which, without so much introduction as "Excuse me, sir, your bite next," falls upon you and leaves with you a tiny germ that may lay you up with malaria for many days and possibly end your career. And pretty as dangerous, with the long tapering limbs of a bullerina of the world of diptera, guazy lanceol wings, and brilliant eyes. "The mischief she has done in times past, and is still doing, and will continue to do it not carefully watched, is colossal. She terrified the Malay States by hiding in the still waters, and emerging when a victim opportumely appeared. She rendered little the first attempts to cut the Panama Canal by sedulously infecting the imported labour. She gave the name of "white man's grave" to West Africa. She waylaid and scourged one-third of the entire population of the tropics every year, and accounted for one-third of the inmates in the hospitals. So much mischief and other siren probably ever did.

The has many sisters and brothers, no more estimable in the social sense. One confers yellow fever upon you and another elephantiasis. Yet others bring you plague through the distributing agency of rals, and typhus fever, and sleeping sickness—no end of deadly complaints to render life a torture. An active, sedulous criminal investigation department was badly needed, and by and by it came, but slowly, and only after infinite patient labour was the scourge checked. Then it was possible to compete the Panama Canal, to remove the topographical stigma upon West Africa, to lighten the work of the tropical hospitals.

The real caus of the scornage to be eradicated was a mystery. The midget enemy was unsuspected. First the partial etfieacy of quinine was discovered. But even then, and for very long afterwards, everybody but a few specialists believed that malaria was due to bad air from noxious murses. It was not until 1878 that a French army surgeon discovered the malarial parasite in the human blood, and then Sir Patrick Manson formed the theory that there might be a development of the parasite in the mosquito. But war had to be fought against those wedded to the old false idea—a little war, but prolonged, within the greater war.

## [8] THE DISCOVERY

Then came the amazing discovery of Sir Ronald Ross—again a Britisher, we are grateful to re, member—which revealed anopheles in her renal dangerous character despite her airy graces. Ross was then a young Army doctor in the Indian Medical Service. In the stifling atmosphere of his small laboratory he dissected a thousand mosquitoes, giving something like two hours to each. For fear the air moved by the punkah should waft away the morsels, he had the punkah stopped. The superstitious natives imagined him engaged in witch-craft, and begrudged him the slightest help. They would not have their fingers pricked for testing their blood for the alluring reward of two or three rupees for a single prick. Eventually on an eventful day, August 16, 1897, Ross captured on the wall of his room an anopheles of a type different to any he had been dissecting, and set to work upon it. Four days later he had probed the secret, discovered the malurial parasite in the stomach tissues. Anopheles was conquered.
It was a work for national, may, universal gratitude. The Nobel Prize for Medicine was conferred upon Sir Ronald in 1902, and the knighthood in 1911, but something more practical was needed, and this came by the founding on a voluntary basis of the Ross Institute and Hospital for Tropical Diseases of Putney Heath in 1928, as a memorial to the great discoverer. There the work of research in problems demanding urgent attention is carried on, for there are thousands in this country even now suffering from the effects of tropical diseases. ## [9] JAPAN'S ALARM.

Plans which Chang Tso-lin, the Mukden warlord, has in hand for developing Manchuria and opening up rich land for more direct trade are thought by Japan to be a menace to its hold on Southern Manchuria and, incidentally, by Russia to constitute a menace to its hold on more northern districts.

The Tokyo correspondent of the "Daily Mail" states:—

While China, from Peking to Canton, is racked with anti-foreign rioting, boycotts, and demands for “freedom” from foreign supervision, Manchuria is proceeding calmly in its development, with the aid of foreign capital as well as foreign engineers and the use of foreign material and machinery.

Chang Tso-lin, the Ruler of the Three Eastern Provinces that go to make up the vast dominion of Manchuria, is boldly carrying out a scheme of railway construction that will treble the wealth of his provinces and give him an independence of communication that will double his military strength.

One line is to connect Mukden, Jehol, and Kalgan, and to consolidate under his rule the territory he gained in his recent war with Wu Pei-fu, which made him paramount in China. This line will furnish a new route into Peking. British engineers are now laying out the line, and material for a start on its construction has been purchased.

Take a map of North China and the two main Manchurian railway lines are seen to form a great "T" with the Chinese Eastern, from Manchuli, on the Siberian border, running south-east to Pogranitchafa, on the Ussuri border, forming the upper line of the letter, and the South Manchurian running south-west from Changchun to Dairen, as the vertical stroke.

It can also later be linked up with the Chinese spur lines to the north and give Chang'an independent connection with the Chinese Eastern. Thus his plans not only threaten Russia's hold on North Manchuria but also Japan's hold upon South Manchuria as well.

## [11] SHANGHAI COMMENT

One of our Hongkong contemporaries is waxing righteously — indignant in regard to a practice that is becoming increasingly prevalent in the Crown Colony as a result of the strike which has affected Hongkong so much, notes the "North China Daily News." When the general exodus of Chinese employed by foreigners took place in June, the house servants at Hongkong were among the first to go and, as it is now much easier to get into Canton than out of it, many of those who left Hongkong so light-heartedly three months ago cannot get back if they would. A certain number have, however, returned, but by no means a large proportion, and the shortage of servants—or at least trained ones—is still neutally felt. Now there come complaints that people whose servants have not returned are enticing away other peoples' servants who have either managed to get back or did not go away at all, and it is stated that even Government staffs are being preyed upon. The process adopted is an eminently simple one, and one that is most likely to make appeal to Chinese of the servant class—the offer of higher wages. The practice is strongly being condemned in Hongkong, where in view of the special circumstances of the Colony, it appears to be a lowdown practice and one that must be condemned. With those remarks, no reasonableண்டு can find fault, as also the statement that if things go on in the same manner wages will soar and domestic economies remain seriously on of joint long after the boycott bus finished. Certainly the practice of persons able to pay high wages, enticing the servants of people who are not, perhaps, able to pay as much is a mean one. Apparently, however, it is one of those things that nothing will ever stop, whether conditions be normal or otherwise. Servant-enticing is by no mean an unknown sport in Europe and America, and appears to be one of those phases of the Tenth Commandinent which will be violated so long as there are desirable men-servants and maid-servants for people to covet.
