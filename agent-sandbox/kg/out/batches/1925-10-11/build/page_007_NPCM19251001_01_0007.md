## [1] LABOUR'S SENSE

**ALIVE TO EMPIRE NEEDS.**

*(Reutor's Service.)*

*London, Sept. 30.*

The Labour Party Conference carried the Executive's resolution, moved by Mr. Ramsay MacDonald, declaring that only upon avowedly Socialist principles could the workers' claim be a fair and decent livelihood be met.

An amendment by Mr. Ferguson, of Glasgow, declaring it was necessary to organise the workers for a vigorous struggle to over-throw the capitalist class, was overwhelmingly defeated.

The Conference also passed a resolution declaring that unemployment was only preventable by the scientific co-ordination of nations and economic resources of industrial undertakings for the public good, instead of private gain and demanding the national adoption of this principle of a living wage as a first charge on industry; adequate maintenance of all unemployeds, and their dependants; pensions for aged and incapacitated workers and widows with children; raising the school leaving age to sixteen with maintenance grants; provision for training unemployed, and demanding that the Government take steps to assist in a revival of overseas trade, and closer relations with Russia.
The Labour Conference adopted a resolution moved by Mr. J. R. Clynes urging that steps be taken to ensure closer political economic relationship between Britain, India, the self-governing dominions and other States of the British Commonwealth of Nations, requesting a survey of land resources of the Empire, the training of emigrants, and condemning the capitalistic exploitation of natives in the tropics; demanding Government controlled bulk purchase of food supplies, and raw materials of the Dominions in order to secure stable prices, expounding a scheme to encourage home production of food assistance to British farmers, and recommending periodical conferences representing the Socialist movement in the Commonwealth.

## [2] FRANCE'S DEBTS.

**NEGOTIATIONS MOVING RAPIDLY.**

*(Router's American Service.)*

*Washington, Sept. 30.*

The Franco-American negotiations are moving, rapidly, it is stated. The Americans have reduced the proposed annuities by $20,000,000 and also agreed to insert a clause in a subsequent agreement providing for revision in the event of evidence forthcoming that the French capacity to pay has been over estimated.

## [5] MOROCCO FIGHTING.

**FRENCH ADVANCE CONTINUES**

**SPANISH OFFENSIVE.**

*Fez, Sept. 30.*

The French claim that the advance is continuing, and they expect to secure important starting places for later operations.

The offensive is limited in objectives and consists of preliminary operations with the object of extending French bases, notably on the Kiffane sector.

The French at eight this morning occupied the Karkur heights, north of Kiffane, continuing to advance on the enemy who is strongly entrenched and resisting vigorously.

*Madrid, Sept. 30.*

It is ofteny announced that the Spanish offensive which opened this morning on the Alhucemas sector with the object of capturing the neighbouring mountains is proceeding satisfactorily.

A communiqué states that all the objectives of to-day's offensive which were needed as jumping off ground for a fresh advance to Ajdir, were speedily captured.

Operations started at day-break.

Battleships, aeroplanes and land batteries on Alhucemas island, providing covering and fire, the objectives were reached by 2.15 in the afternoon when the Spanish commander wirelessed success from the battleship "Alfonso Thirteen."

## [6] CABLE NEEDS.

**RATES TOO HIGH.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

The Empire Press Conference passed resolutions advocating the tullest provision of telegraphic communication within the Empire and stressing the urgent importance of an adequate system of cheap and rapid transmission of news over the Empire, also recommending that the Imperial cable system lay an additional Atlantic cable.

The Conference also passed a resolution, moved by Mr. Moore of India, urgently advocating a large reduction in intermediate Press rates to important centres of Imperial communication.

He pointed out that the present cost of "relaying messages" from Australia, Canada, and South Africa resulted in an excessive cost.

## [7] EMPIRE NEWS.

A complaint of insufficient Australian news published in Britain was the burden of a resolution moved by Sir Hugh Denison, New South Wales, advocating co-operation between Australian newspapers with the object of supplying the British Press with a service similar to the present service from Britain to Australia.
Mr. Holtz, Victoria, in second-ing, advocated that other Dominions should be similarly linked up. Mr. Turner, Britain, moved an amendment pledging his conferees to do their utmost to secure the publication of more Empire news and stated that agencies and correspondents already gave British papers more Dominions news that they could use.

Several delegates argued in the same strain.

Mr. Woods, Canada, in supporting the amendment, agreed that this was the most that could be done.
Lord Burnham lengthily explained the difficulties of British newspapers as regards cabled news and showed the difficulty of giving effect to the scheme embodied in the resolution.
Sir, Denison accepted the amendment and as a substantive motion it was finally carried that in view of the growing importance of overseas Dominions, and in order to promote a better inter-Empire knowledge and understanding, members of the Conference pledge themselves to the utmost efforts to secure the publication of more Empire news than hitherto.

moderate advance will be made in the direction of Canton, A great dent of preparation and re-organisation will be necessary before the anti-Bolshevist troops in eastern Kwangtung can hope to challenge the more efficient and well-armed units under the direct control of Borodin and his henchmen. The Peking Government, for various reasons, of which fear of the expansion of the influence of the present regime in Canton is the foremost seems anxious to help the moderate wing of the Kuomintang, and to give Chen Chiang-ming the assistance his army needs. But there are difficulties on both sides. The separatist tendencies of the Kuomintang have not been eliminated. Even though so-called moderates seem to be determined to continue this policy to compel the Central Authorities to give them a large share in the Government. The Citizens' Conference is obviously the faintest means of a settlement of China's political discussions and disunity, but the Kuomintang are not more sincere than the Anfuities in the proposed use of this instrument. Each wants to pack the Conference with arbitrarily chosen nominees who will serve the interests of the respective parties before they will consider the higher concerns of the nation. Peking cannot very well co-operate with the Kuomintang in a war on the Bolsheviks and refuse to co-operate in the Citizens' Conference. On the other hand, many of the Kuomintang leaders, who dispute the authority the Central Committee, have to walk warily. Both extremists and moderates are courting the favours and support of the numerous branches of the party in the provinces, and the Right Wing apparently feel that even a temporary alliance with the present Peking Government would deprive them of much of the sympathy they might otherwise obtain from the provincial committees. The tradition of separatism which has developed since the Republic—partly political and partly psychological—is not an easy thing to break down, in spite of all that the province has suffered since it broke away from Peking.
There are internal differences, for which handicap prompt and effective action by the moderates. We cannot understand the hostility that still exists to Chen Chiang-ming, whose action in refusing to participate in Sun Yat-sen's much-mounted but fantastic Expedition against the North was perfectly right, as same as his chief's course was insane. But the hostility is there, whether it be justified or not. The Party leaders have done their best to make his name anathematic throughout the rank and file, and they cannot very well turn round admit they were wrong, and openly support his campaign. This attitude is based on no punctilious moral grounds. The stigma they have attached to Chen Chiang-ming others have attached to Marsha Feng Yu-hsiang, but that has no prevented the Kuomintang from carrying favour with the latter.

The whole situation is very complicated, and it is probable that the Kuomintang moderates will conduct their own propaganda campaign against the Reds, persuade Peking to keep its own counsel, and, if help must be given the troops now at Swatow and other parts of the province not directly under Canton's authority, to give that help secretly. If there were any evidence of disinterested and patriotic statesmanship, we might look to the Citizens' Conference to provide an amicable solution, but he Tuan Government, which has cunningly used the anti-foreign agitation to perpetuate its enjoyment of office, is not likely—without pressure from abroad—to use the instrument it has created for unselfish purposes. Every approach to unification hitherto has been marred by sordid motives. It will never be achieved that way.

## [8] MR. LLOYD GEORGE AND AMERICA.

Mr. Lloyd George visited the Eisteddfod at Fwlhelli, and received an enthusiastic welcome from a great crowd of people assembled in the Eisteddfod pavilion. He was accompanied by Dame Margaret Lloyd George, Miss Megan Lloyd George, and the Bishop of St. Davids.

Speaking partly in Welsh and partly in English, Mr. Lloyd George, referring to the success achieved by English choirs at the Eisteddfod, said that was for the good of Wales. "We were relying too much upon our established fame, upon our natural facility and genius for song. It was about time our English friends came to stir us up." The political changes of the day, he proceeded, were not nearly so great as the changes in the habits of the people and their attitude of mind towards questions. Whether they were changes for the better or the worse God alone knew, but the real danger of change in a community was that the people in seeking something which was better might lose something equally good. The Prime Minister would have been delighted to have attended the Eisteddfod had it not been for the exigencies of the political situation. The next day they were to have a very distinguished Labour leader (Mr. J. H. Thomas); that day they had a simple countryman whose opinions did not fit quite comfortably into any political mould. (Laughter).

Later Mr. Lloyd George addressed the Overseas Welsh visitors at a reception given by Mr. David Davies, M.P., president of the Welsh League of Nations Union. Speaking particularly to the United States visitors, he said, "I appreciate the particular difficulties which you have in America in regard to the League. I am looking forward to the time when there will be such modifications and adaptations of the original scheme that your difficulties will be removed, and it will be possible for the United States to join in the great confraternity of nations to act together to impose peace on the quarrelsome nations of the world. (Cheers). Until the United States, with its vast and growing influence—an influence which to-day is almost incalculable, and in the coming years will be a dominant influence—can see its way to join some sort of confraternity of nations that will ensure peace and goodwill amongst men, I despair of the possibilities of civilisation in the future. I had a good deal to do with the direction of the war, and was the only Minister throughout the world who went through it from beginning to end. I should not like to see another. It was a horror. It was something that humanity, whatever its faults, imperfections, and sins ought not to be subjected to again.

should never happen again, but what do I see? Nations arming, nations utilising their power and resources with a view to perfecting, increasing, and intensifying the mechanism of human slaughter and misery. Well, unless you in a. Continent which has great advantages over us come to the rescue of civilisation I am afraid of what will happen. I honestly do not believe European civilisation could survive another war. I saw the thing growing in destructiveness year by year, the machinery becoming more shattering, terrible, and devastating. When the Armistice was signed there were inventions ready to be hurled into the mass of humanity which would have been destructive beyond imagination. Since then science has been working; some of the most ingenious brains at the command of the communities have been at the task of working out something still more

terrible. What happened in the last war is nothing to what would happen if there were to be war in the world to-day. America can save us; We have our quarrels in Europe. The weeds of hatred, racial, national, religious—every conceivable form of human passion, anger, wrath, and ferocity are deep down in the soil of Europe. There is not a frontier in Europe which has not been traced in red—fought and re-fought. You are free from that in America. For God's sake keep America free, but do not abandon Europe, and do not ring down the fire curtain so that the flames should not consume you. You cannot say 'Am I my brother's keeper?' No great country can have the brand of Cain on its brow and go through the world. Remember you belong to the same fraternity as poor Europe and the best you have got came from Europe.

"I appeal to Welshmen of every country and every clime," Mr. Lloyd George concluded, "to do their best, each in his own way, to influence the lands they will return to and of which they are citizens, and to which they owe their first loyalty—to influence them to come to assist in the great common task inherited from the founder of our great faith, the redemption of mankind!" (Loud cheers).

## [9] CHINA CABLES.

**TELEGRAMS TO INLAND PLACES**

The Eastern Extension Telegraph Co. notifies that:—

Telegrams to Amoy, Fooahow and Shanghai as well as to all places in North China (Chihli, Shantung, Manchuria, Mongolia, etc.) are accepted and transmitted as usual.

Telegrams to Hankow will be accepted either with abbreviated telegraphic address or with full address and will be forwarded by wire to Peking and thence posted direct to addressees.

Telegrams to other places in Central China than Hankow and to Inland places via Shanghai, Amoy and Foochow will also be accepted and will, if fully addressed be posted direct to addressees from Peking, Shanghai, Amoy or Foochow respectively but if bearing abbreviated address may be retained until normal conditions are re-established.

All telegrams to places affected by the dislocation of the Chinese Telegraph Administration's service are, however, accepted at the senders' risk-only.

Warsaw, September 30.—The visit of Chicherin who is en route to a health resort in Germany was marked by a notable reapproachment between Poland and Russia. Chicherin was received and entertained by the President of the Republic, the Presidents of the two Chambers and the Prime Minister. He had a two hours' conference with the Polish Foreign Minister. The German Minister who was suddenly recalled from leave tried on several occasions to see Chicherin, but calls at the Soviet Legation were fruitless owing to Chicherin's other engagements.—Reuter;

London, September 30. — The Bakers' and Allied Traders' Association has sent a letter to Sir Kingsley Wood asking for an extension of time before the enforcement of the new regulations in regard to the use of boric acid in egg yolks imported from China. The association say that they propose to try out two new methods of preservation by packing in England and sending to China and then re-shipping here in order to see the effect of the various climatic conditions on the contents of the barrels. Sir Kingsley Wood, in reply, regretted that he was unable to hold out any hope of a further extension of time.—Reuter.
