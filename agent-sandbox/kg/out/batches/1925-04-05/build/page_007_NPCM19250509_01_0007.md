## [5] Vines May Curve to the Right or to the Left, But the Twining is Uniform—Trees Have Similar Habits.

<sub>section under [3] [By KATHERINE LOUISE SMITH.]</sub>

**[By NORMAN C, McLOUD.]**

In climbing a pole does a vine twist to the left or to the right?

To the left or to the right?
To Nature Lovers the question is full of interest; and since most of PB find pleasure in the curious workings of natural laws the answer is worth the seeking. Close observation reveals striking individuality in the twining habits of plant growth.

Some vines twist to the right after the fusion of the hands of a clock in circling the dial. Others turn to the left and seem to grow backwards. In most cases the habit is fixed throughout an entire group or family. In others there is variation, even among vines of close kinship.

Climbing Vines are interesting forms of growth, which depend on outside help for their meats of support. In fact, that they may realize their ambition to rise in the world they avail themselves of any upright object available, whether growing or otherwise. The support may be the trunk of a tree, the post of a fence or the wall of a building. Any object, according to the chants to climb is welcome, to the climbers.

More Than One Way To Climb.
In establishing themselves on their supports the vines have notions of their own as to the ways and means to be adopted. Most climbers attach themselves by coiling. With others the contact with the tree or post is effected by means of adhesive disks, as in the case of the widely distributed Virginia Creeper. Another type of vine attaches itself by means of tendrils which are thrown out in all directions to take hold of anything in reach. In this latter class are the gourd and the grape; the grasping tendrils of which are familiar to everyone.

Whether twinning or otherwise fastened to its support, the vine has a definite object in view when the upward journey is undertaken. To the Nature student the climbing habit is essentially utilitarian. Ascending growth is necessary to the fulfillment of the vine's life mission of displaying its flowers to the flitting bees and offer the seeds to the winds and the birds. Enough enabled to escape from the crowded plant life of the surface the vine would be submerged and hidden, and would meet with failure in its efforts to provide the bees with material for the making of honey and the birds with a valuable source of food supply. In similar fashion the plant would be cheated of a chance for reproduction through the scattering of its seeds by birds and breezes.

Legend Of A Great Bridge.
Shortly after attention is riveted on the Washington shore for Castle Rock which Lewis and Clark called Beacon Rock looms up. It covers thirteen and a half acres and can be seen for miles. No wonder Lewis and Clark used it for a landmark. The Indians, too, play their part in the stories of this inspiring river. In one place, on the Washington side is seen the north abutment of the "Bridge of the Gods." The legends say that at one time the great stream was spanned at this point by a natural bridge, but Mt. Hood and Mt. Adams quarrelled and in their rage the great bridge was shattered. Still more romantic is the story that an Indian brave from the Oregon side married an Indian maiden from the Washington

side in spite of the opposition of their tribes. As they started across the bridge to his home they were pursued by her disappointed suitors and kinsmen. Hardly had they reached safety than the bridge fell, carrying with it their advancing enemies. At any rate the bridge fell and formed rapids in the river.

Indians play a prominent part in legends of this river. Castle Rock, 1,140 feet high, whose summit has only recently been sealed by the white man, was an Indian lookout, and Memalooose Island was used by the Indians for many years as a burying ground, the dead being placed in pine huts. On this island is the grave of Vicor Trevitt, a pioneer river man whose wish was to be buried with the Indians. "If there should be such a thing as a resurrection," this sceptic said, "I will take my chance with the Indians."

Fish wheels in operation gather the sufficient salmon in many places and there are the Dalles and the falls of Cadillac—thirty feet high—and the locks which lift the boats to the level of the Middle Columbia. All these are a part of the enjoyment of a trip on the Columbia. Above the Dalles the river becomes unnavigable, caused by balastic formations and this stretch is a favourite spot among the Indians, many of whom live near by. During the fishing season they can be seen far out on the rocks, speaking salmon which are dried in the sun and used as food.
This mighty river with a flow of over 1,800,000 cubic feet of water every second — greater than either the Mississippi or St. Lawrence—fed by overlasting snow fields and glaciers, winds its way through the Pacific North-west and at the mouth the tide reaches in for one hundred and fifty miles. Its crags, cascades, cataracts and falls are worth going many miles to see. If Neolithic man chose this water way to enter this continent he is to be congratulated on his good taste and forenight. At any rate present-day man can see no more picturesque river.

Study of the spirals reveals curious variations. While a particular species will stick to its habits with staunch insistence, other members of the same family will strike off in the opposite direction. The morning glories offer good example of this pecuniary of family tendencies. Most of the plants of this group twine to the right, with no departure from the established rule. In at least one species, however, the custom meets with complete reversal and the vine is always found twisting to the left. Just why this variation should occur has never been determined although the tendency is widely recognized by students of natural Science. The popular flowering wisteris is another vine of variable characteristics. In his report to the American Nature

## [11] *(no headline: column 6)*

Association Dr. Brook cites three species of wisteria growing on the grounds of his own home. In the vines of two species each stem and branch curves to the right. On the third variety the twisting is invariably in the opposite direction.
Science has five no satisfactory explanation of the constancy of vine spirals. That the problem should have received considerable attention at the hands of botanists is altogether natural, because of the distinctiveness and persistence of the twining characteristics of the various species. Several explanations have been brought forward. As one attempt at solution it has been suggested that in some species of vines the tip of the growing plant is attracted by the sun and drawn toward the bright light. Influence of this nature, it is figured, would result in a left-hand twist against the clock. On the other hand it is argued that some species are repelled by the sun and follow the clock-hands from left to right. Whatever the cause, the results themselves are full of interest and well worth noting for ourselves when opportunity is offered.

Vines are not alone in the peculiar characteristics of twining growth. The spiral tendency is definitely established for the trees as well. In many species of forest growth we find trunks that are as strikingly twisted as the vines which may have encompassed their trunks. In tree growth, of course, the spiral has less emphasis, but it is none the less noticeable and impressive.

On some trees the twisting habit manifests itself even in the development of the barb, and examination will show the outside covering of the trunks winding their way toward the top in consistent aspiration. The real manifestation of this ch: aeteristic, however, is found by the man engaged in splitting a trunk for firewood. In work of this kind the labour is often doubled by the difficulty of separating the twisted fibres of the wood—an experience with which every rail-splitter or woodcutter is familiar.

Constancy of direction is not found in the twisting tendency of all trees, but there are several species in which the habit of growth is practically uniform, and in which all specimens will show the same characteristics of twining to either right or left. This is especially true in the case of the chestnut. The wood of this tree is allogetner apt to be marked by twisting fibres, and it has been found that in most cases the direction of the twist is toward the left. Specimens with the right-hand twining of the grain are declared to be comparatively scarce. In the case of the common beech the tendency is the same, and like the chestnut, these trees produce spirals of left-hand twist. This is also true of the staghorn sumac, that widely distributed tree of the brilliant red berries and scarlet foliage widely scattered throughout a large portion of the United States.

When it comes to the growth of tendrils the vines show fine disregard of the law of spirals. There is no sense of direction in a tendrill, as these offshoots of the parent stem adapt themselves to the convenience of the moment in choosing their direction of growth. Reaching forth to find a twig on which to fasten themselves, the tendrill of a grape or hop vine will extend themselves in the direction of being the best foothold, right left up down or otherwise.
In this form of nature study the material is easily reached, even by those of us who live in crowded cities. Rambles in parks, gardens and wood lands will open the way for an interesting field of observation and teach us many of the secrets of nature in the arrangement of her products. The study requires little or not effort and will be found well worth the required attention.
