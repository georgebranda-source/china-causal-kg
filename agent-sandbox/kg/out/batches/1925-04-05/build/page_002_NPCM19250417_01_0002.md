## [12] COMMERCE AND FINANCE

**CEYLON RUBBER**

**1924 "NOT SO PROSPEROUS."**

Shipments of latex showed a considerable increase—59,333 gallons compared to 11,431 for 1923. These shipments took place in November and December, with the result that the Government has brought rubber latex under the restriction ordinance as from January 1. Another factor against the producer has been the exchange, which has ruled on the average about 1d. above the rate ruling for 1923.

From January to the end of July the exportable allowance under the restriction scheme was 50 per cent. This fell to 55 per cent on August 1, and to 50 per cent for the period of three months from November 1. For the period at the time of writing (February) the exportable maximum has been increased to 55 per cent.

The position generally is viewed with optimism, and, "with the price ruling about Is. '5d. in London and 90 cents locally, a good return is shown from rubber investments."

## [15] SPANISH TOBACCO.

The current Bulletin of the Anglo-South American Bank quotes a Spanish Royal Order regarding the steps to be taken for the encouragement of tobacco cultivation in Spain. The Order reviews the progress since the initiation of the scheme of 1917, from which it appears that the matter has not been taken up with any great interest by cultivators, the number of growers in 1921 totalling only 17 and in 1922 36. It is added, however, that since the latter year the number has increased considerably, cultivators totalling 307 in 1923 and 1,050 in 1924, while the area under cultivation has risen from 116 hectares in 1921 to 1,000 hectares in 1924, the latter being the maximum area so far authorised. The present Royal Order sets up a Central Commission to study the question of the cultivation of tobacco in Spain, on which the growers in each of the three districts devoted to the industry will be represented. The Commission will present a report not later than July 1 next showing the results of the experiments hitherto carried out and the modifications, if any, suggested to the regulations formulated on December 30, 1919.

## [17] AUSTRALIAIN SUGAR

The present sugar season will be a record for Australia and for Queensland particularly — the state which grows most of the sugar cane.

The highest production previously was in 1997, when the total yield of sugar in Queensland was 307,000 tons. This year the estimated production will be in the vicinity of 388,000 tons in this State. This, with 22,000 tons in New South Wales and 3500 tons in Victoria (beet sugar) will give a large surplus of production over local consumption.

Approximately 100 American business men were present at the meeting.

## [19] BUSINESS WITH BRAZIL.

**CONGESTED PORTS.**

Advices from Sao Paulo emphasise the fact that special caution should be exercised at the present time in dealing with this part of Brazil. The port of Santos is in "a deplorable state of congestion, and merchandise will be held up" for months. In view of this many local importers have repeated orders for goods to be sent to Rio de Janeiro, with the result that the port is also becoming congested. Due to this duplication of orders, it is probable that much merchandise will be refused.

In the second place, owing to the prolonged drought and the intense heat experienced this summer, water has become scarce and the Light and Power Company is in serious difficulties. Power is being rationed, and factories will be closed a certain number of days per week. One factory alone has dismissed over a thousand workmen, and others are following suit. The outcome will undoubtedly be serious labour trouble in the near future, and there are already rumours of strikes and revolution.

The outlook is far from pleasant. There is political agitation in the north, with the possibility of Federal Government intervention with troops. Agitation is already commencing with regard to the election of the next President.

## [20] FORD'S FREIGHTERS.

### -

The fleet cargc ships which Henry Ford, the motor manufacturer, is building up in the United States is meeting with the usual difficulty of fleets of this sort in finding cargo. Quite apart from the big motor ships that carry the materials to his works on the Great Lakes, he has some ocean-going freighters which carry various automobile parts from New York to the various plants on the Atlantic coast, and then go down south to the Argentine with finished motor-cars. Up to now the majority of the passages northward have been made in ballast, a proceeding which robs the ships of most of the money saved by operating themselves instead of paying freights. Ford's agents are now attempting to enter the coffee trade from Brazil to the United States, an effort that is bringing them into sharp rivalry with the various American, British, and Brazilian firms already established.

## [21] CHINA AND FLOUR

A promising trade in wheat flour is being built up in China by western Canada mills. Last year Chinese importers bought over a million barrels of Canadian flour and present prospects are that the importations for 1925 will exceed those of last year.

## [22] PROGRESS OF PALESTINE

Sir Alfred Mond summarising to newspaper correspondents in Jerusalem his impressions of his three weeks' stay in Palestine, said that he found very noticeable improvement in the general political situation and more understanding by all concerned of the position. He ascribed this to the better relations between Jews and Arabs. The financial position of the Government was much better than four years ago. Some of the industries introduced by Jewish money and effort might be regarded as "large industrial capitalistic enterprises," such as the salt works at Athlit, the oil and soap works. "Shemen," the flour mills, and the Nesher cement works, all at Haifa. He was gratified with the introduction of the new industries, such as silk, tanneries, furniture, and textiles, in all of which the latest machinery was used by people who knew their business. The orange industry, he said, was capable of very great development; and he was confident that Palestine could grow first-class tobacco. He saw a great future for dairying, poultry farming, and vegetable-growing. There was no reason for doubts as to the success of agriculture, in view of the fact that it was possible to raise in Palestine three crops a year on irrigated land.

Sir Alfred also spoke of the mineral resources of the Dead Sea, a matter which, he said, was arousing considerable interest because of phosphate and other deposits.

Sir Alfred laid the foundationstone for a new colony of Rumanian Jewish agricultural works at Haifa.
