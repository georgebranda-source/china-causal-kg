## [16] GOLDEN DREAMS.

Nobody can have read the report of the launching of the Changte at the Kowloon Dockyard yesterday without a feeling of pride. That the order for such a vessel as the Changte should have been placed in Hongkong is in itself a trihute to the Colony's growing importance as a shipbuilding centre, since Australian owners desiring an ocean-going steamer for a highly competitive route must obviously turn to the best market for their requirements. Gratifying as the launching of the Changte is in this respect, it is even more gratifying, however, as evidence that the Colony not only possesses the labour for such work but also possesses that even greater asset, the technical skill. Therefore it is no idle dream to look forward to the day when Hongkong dockyards will turn out mail liners for the great ocean trades—when the Colony will have become the Clydebank of the Far East. Those will be great days, but they are yet far off, and it behaves us, before we are carried away on the wings of hope, to take stock of

our position. There have not been wanting signs during the past few years that the golden future which is almost within our grasp will not be won without the greatest vigilance. Already production costs are ominously high—we were told during a recent strike that moulders' wages were perilously near the Home level—and unless labour takes the same view they may easily become prohibitive. There is no need to dwell upon what that will mean. The very reasons that led to the placing of the order for the Changte in Hongkong will lead to such orders being placed elsewhere. Commerce has only one standard—the strictly practical. Therefore if we are to win the favours of commerce we must keep to the strictly practical. Hongkong to-day possesses the money, the brains and the labour to compete with other parts of the world. The brains and the money will always be ready to play their part. The future therefore rests with the workmen. Labour cannot be made to see that fact too clearly for its own welfare and for the general prosperity of the Colony. That as much as anything is the moral of the launching of the good ship Changte.

## [17] "Ships, Colonies and Commerce."

This old election cry rises to the surface in reading the account of the launch at Kowloon Docks of the "Changte" for the Australian service. "Ships and Commerce" is more applicable to Hongkong; and once more it is proved how efficient is the Colony to prepare one for the other: The seven seas now can show the oil tankers and cargo-boats—the joint production of British and Chinese cooperation at the Kowloon Docks—and henceforth will be added a passenger-carrying steamer in the shape of the "Changte," Shipping undoubtedly brings countries into contact more than any other industry; and it seems appropriate that Hongkong so closely connected with the Australian trade, should have had the privilege of making a contact that all who follow the fortunes of the Colony, and the Dock Co. in particular, will hope will be secure and lead to further connections of a similar happy character. The "Changte" will need to be a good boat to follow in the wake of such old and tried vessels as the "Tai-yuan"—and—"Changsha"—which have braved many a storm: but builders and owners alike have no fear on that score. One thought arises out of yesterday's happy launching function, and it is suggested by Mr. Beith's appeal for preferential transport when a passage to Australia has to be considered; and it is this: Homegoing residents may well consider

the question of a trip Home via Australia. Apart from the health-giving qualities of a somewhat extended sea-trip through romantic and delightful scenes, there is the added attraction of making acquaintance with the great southern continent, and the very independent people who are proud to be known as Australians. Hongkong people should have an added satisfaction in travelling on a Hongkong-built vessel, thus helping on the Colony's most pressing need—ships and commerce.

## [18] The Banque Industrielle.

The “slump time” definitely proved that even banks were not immune from disaster. The word bank had become to be associated with the qualities of strength, solidarity and the incorruptible. Bank scandals there have been, and England has had her fair share; but even these did not undermine the general faith that prevailed, and does to-day to a great extent, in banks as institutions. The closing of the Banque Industrielle de Chine was a sensation of the period. It was a local and a national one; and those who witnessed the collection of bewildered depositors at the door in Chaler Road, will not soon forget the impression of shattered hopes and helplessness then seen. That seems a very long time ago. It seems evident that the representations and discussions which have taken place, have not been in vain. Depositors are to receive due recognition and the bonds that it is proposed to issue to them to liquidate their holdings, will be of a marketable nature. Even if the period of repayment is somewhat extended, the fact must not be ignored that payment will eventually be made in full—much better than a proportionate repayment of so much in the pound sterling or dollar. The Banque may find difficulty in taking, its former place amongst the banking institutions of the world—“once you bite me twice I am shy” as the French lady said—but assuming the correctness of the report to which publicity has been given, and the subsequent carrying out of the conditions mentioned, we trust the fact that it did eventually make good, will not be lost sight of. It is reasonable to assume that the lessons of the past will be taken to heart.

## [19] SUN'S OLD. SHIP.

**RENAMED IN HIS MEMORY**

From Our Own Correspondent.)

In memory of Dr. Sun Yat-sen, it has been decided to rename the cruiser Wing Fung after him.

According to the vernacular papers, a ceremony attended by the leading officials of the Kuomintang was held on board the cruiser when it was given the new name of Chung San—Dr. Sun having been known as Sun Chung san, to the Chinese, in the latter part of his career.

It is believed that the Wing-Fung was one of the Northern Cruiser squadron that broke away from Peking when Dr., Sun came to Canton to organise his Constitutional Government. The late Admiral Ching Pek-kwong led the squadron South and he was later assassinated in Canton. Admiral Ching's statue now stands on Dutch Folly.

When General Chan Kwingming delivered his coup in June, 1922, Dr. Sun fled to the Wing Fung which bombarded Canton and was subsequently bottled up, off Shameen, for some weeks. The other ships in the squadron gradually deserted Dr. Sun but the Wing Fung remained as his flagship to the last.

## [21] CORRESPONDENCE.

**FRENCH BANK AND CHINA INDEMNITY**

[To the Editor of the China Mail.]

Sir,—I read with interest your summary and exposition of the position of depositors in the Banque Industrielle de Chine but owing to official reticence this could not, of course, include the basis of the agreement which has been reached as a result of the negotiations between the Governments of France and China concerning the payment and apportionment of the French portion of the Boxer Indemnity. This reticence on the part of the Government of China surely points to one thing only and that is that it is afraid of public opinion regarding its action.

It will certainly find that there will be (to quote the "Daily Bulletin"). "Agitation against the agreement" if the rumour is correct that to get as much as possible for the Bank (in which, if my memory serves me aright, it is interested to the extent of about ten million tacts) the Chinese Government has accepted an offer of France that all of the French Indemnity money should be devoted to the purposes of putting the Bank on its feet.

The rumour may be without foundation but the reasons for such a move are apparent. The Chinese Government has contended that payments of the French portion of the Boxer India nity should be carried out on, a basis of the present value of the franc and the French Government, and it should be on that of the gold franc. It would be a move which the Governments would consider mutually beneficial if France conceded that payment be made in paper and China waived her claim to that portion of the Indemnity that the French Government had intended for use along educational lines in China.
But would the Chinese Government be doing, right in accepting such a proposition? Even supposing that the rumour I have mentioned is false, is the Chinese Government doing right in devoting even a portion of the French Indemnity for the benefit of the Bank? It may be, in so far as the protection of its own interests and those of a limited number of the people it is supposed to represent is concerned, but what of the legions who have no interest in the Bank, what of the future generation? Why should a body of men have power to waive an offer which would be to the lasting good of the nation in favour of immediate gratification of an instinct which no doubt has a more technical psychological name but which for the sake of expressiveness I will term that of Grab, Grab, Grab.

## [25] PACIFIC MAIL SHIPS!

**HOW THE SALE IS SEEN IN AMERICA.**

**BATTLE OF FINANCE.**

With regard to the appeal by the Pacific Mail S.S. Co. against the U.S. Shipping Board's decision to award their five liners to the Dollar syndicate, the hearing of which has been postponed till April 20, the following excerpts from a San Francisco paper, throw some light on the situation before the bids were opened.

Washington, March 17.
The closing chapters were being written to-day in a battle of giant shipping interests for preferential rights in the awakening, Asiatic market.

Officials of the Dollar and Pacific Mall steamship lines were preparing for the final showdown in the financial poker game which will decide ownership of five "President" type liners now operated by the latter concern out of San Francisco.

Because the Pacific Mail is literally fighting with its back to the wall in the deal, according to many observers, relatively high bids are expected.

For half a century now the Mall liners have been steaming across the Pacific, building up a name which to-day is known as familiarly as the Peninsular and Oriental, the "P. & O." of Rudyard Kipling's Indian tales.

The powerful Dollar Line, which recently shattered precedent and inaugurated a regular “around the world” freight and passenger schedule, with ample financial backing, is admittedly, in the stronger position of the two.

The tremendous strides made by this organization in the past few years, due directly to the canny foresight of Captain Robert Dollar, the 80-year-old Scotch-Americare shipping wizard, have been based largely on Asiatic shipping.

Regardless of the group which acquires control of the vessels, according to Commissioner Meyer Lissner, San Francisco will be the port of call, because of the Shipping Board policy of selling ships on condition that present sea lanes are maintained.
