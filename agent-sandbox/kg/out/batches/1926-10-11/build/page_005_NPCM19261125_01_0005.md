## [8] A YEAR'S SHIPS.

**FEATURES OF LLOYD'S REGISTER.**

The report of Lloyd's Register of Shipping on the Society's operations for the year 1925-26, contains a further gloomy forecast, but also a comment which to some extent, is gratifying. The report predicts that the effects of the coal dispute on shipbuilding will be much more seriously felt next year the amount of new work coming forward showing already a considerable falling off. On the other hand, although the total of the projected tonnage is less than that of last year, the proportion of the amount to be built in Great Britain and Ireland has increased by 13.3 per cent. Plans for 361 new vessels of 1,089,360 tons gross have been passed by Lloyd's Committee, of which total 63.3 per cent. is to be built at home. This may be compared with five 63.7 per cent. of 1913-14 when plans for vessels aggregating 1,688,060 tons were approved.

With regard to classification, 410 rew vessels of 1,330,507 tons gross have been assigned classes. This tonnage is more than that of last year though the number of vessels is markedly less, which indicates a year of larger individual vessels than the preceding one. It may be noted that of this increase Germany has contributed a larger share, the tonnage of vessels built by her and classified under the Society being 140,000 more than her figure of last year. The increasing adoption of the Society's classification is well illustrated by the fact that the figure "for merchant vessels" at the end of June, 1926, is the largest that has ever been recorded, viz., 29,172,698 tons. Of this tonnage Great Britain and Ireland of course take nemier place with 47.4 percent, to which is to be added 4.5 per cent. for the British Domingons to make the Empire's total. These figures show that as other countries hold nearly half the total, Lloyd's Register can claim with no little justification, to be an international institution. In this connection it is interesting to note that in January of this year the Committee subscribed to a convention with the Scandinavian countries, as a result of which these countries are now added to the list of nationalitigs by which the Surveys and Certificates of Lloyd's Register are recognised as exempting vessels from Government inspections of various kinds. The countries comprised in this list are the British Dominions, France, Holland, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Spain, Greece, Belgium, Portugal, and Latvia.

At the end of June there were 297 vessels under construction to the Society's classification, and, on completion of these, Lloyd's Register will cover in the aggregate, 9,854 vessels with a gross tonnage of over 30.5 millions. Of vessels individually over 10,000 tons gross, 8 steamships have been constructed under the Society's special survey, the list being headed by the Conte Biancamano, of 24,416 tons, for the Lloyd Sabudo Societa Anonima per Azioni, with next in order the Carinthia, 20,277 tons, and the Otranto, 20,032 tons, for the Cunard Steam Ship Company, Limited, and the Orient Steam Navigation Company, Limited, respectively. The steam turbine has maintained its position; 14 vessels, of 146,354 tons gross, were fitted during the year with turbines; as against 12 vessels, and 114,009 tons gross in the previous year. One of these vessels, the "Itiyo Maru" of 4,274 gross tons, is fitted with turbo-electric drive, 10 of them have single-reduction gear, the remainder double reduction.

The use of the heavy-oil engine is spreading with remarkable rapidity. The year completed in June establishes a record in the number of vessels of all types fitted with this means of propulsion, and under the Society's classification. This record is 118 vessels aggregating 601,427 gross tons. The previous year's figures were little more than half these, namely, 69 vessels and 302,189 gross tons. The previous year's figures too are excelled in point of size of individual vessels, except in the case of the Aorangi, whose 17,491 gross tons made her the largest vessel of 1924-25. The Gripsholm, of 17,300 gross tons, however, was not far behind, while others were the Amerikaland, 15,355 gross tons, and the Canadolite and Montolite, each of 11,309 tons. The largest and highest-powered oil-engined vessel afloat will be the Augustus, which is now being constructed under the Society's superintendence at Sestri Ponente.

## [9] SHIPPING STRIKE.

**POSITION IN SHANGHAI LAST WEEK**

The following from the "Shanghai Mercury" of November 15, supplements Reuter's cables of that date:

Nine coasting vessels and one liver steamer of the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company are at present tied up at the company's wharf and in the stream as a result of the strike among seagull employees of the company over the question of compensation of the victims of the U.S. "Klang Yung" explosion in which 88 members of the crew lost their lives.

Owing to the week-end intervening, negotiations for a settlement were temporarily halted and it is not quite clear at the moment what are the prospects for a settlement. On Saturday, the anticipated departure of the Ningpo steamer "Hsin Kiangteen" took place and there is also a likelihood of vessels destined for Foochow and ports other than Swatow and Canton getting away in the near future, these being ports unaffected by agitation to the same extent as the two named.

There has been no trouble experienced with the strikers except their refusal to work. There has been no sabotage or violence and the crews are patiently waiting on board their vessels while their delegates settle the matter with the parties responsible and able to bring about a settlement. Cargo is still being loaded and discharged and in most cases vessels due or overdue for departure are filled with cargo ready to sail at short notice.

Monday, according to officials of the company, is not a good day to judge the position of the strike any way. It is what is termed by the company a “neutral” day, when under ordinary circumstances, hardly a vessel of this company leaves port. To-morrow there will be better prospects of telling just how strong the strike really is for several vessels are due to sail.
It is difficult to discover how the negotiations are proceeding for they are being carried on between Chinese officials of the company and the strikers' representatives who are not given to publishing the results of their efforts.
During the week-end one or two more coasting steamers arrived in Shanghai but their captains received no notification from their crews that the latter intended to quit. How sympathetic they are towards the strike will be discovered when the vessels are due to sail again.

The river steamer "Luen Yi" at present running under the British flag, but jointly owned by the three leading shipping companies operating along the China coast, is the only river boat remaining to the China Merchant's Company, the other eight having been commanded by the Northern Commander, Sun Chuan-fang and the ninth, the "Kiang Yung"; having been sunk following the explosion which is the cause of the present strike.

The “Luen Yi” is loaded with a big cargo of sugar which was taken aboard at Anking for Kiukiang but, due to the fighting in the latter port, was over-carried to Shanghai. This cargo is still in the holds of the “Luen Yi” waiting to be carried back to Kiukiang.

Actually, matters are at a standstill, though negotiations are reported to be in progress. The foreign officials of the company are in the dark as to what is going on and it is believed that there is more behind the strike than the mere claim for compensation which the China Merchants' Company is hardly likely to refuse, being but a fraction of the sum being lost daily while the strike lasts.

The Shanghai Seamen's Union entertained six Chinese journalists to dinner in the Yoh Yah Leu Restaurant, in Frenchtown, between 7 p.m. and 8 p.m. yesterday. Cheng Chung-ling, a representative of the Union, who presided, gave a full account of the labour dispute in the China Merchants Steam Navigation Co., and appealed to the press to give their wholehearted support to the strikers, who now number 200.
