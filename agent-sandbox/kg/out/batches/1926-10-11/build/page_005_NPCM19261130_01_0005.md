**OPIUM ON A DOUGLAS STEAMER**

**FIRST TIME IN COURT**

The Master and Agents (Messrs. Douglas, Lapraik & Co.) of the s.s. "Haiching" were the defendants in a case heard by Mr. R. E. Lindsell at the Central Magistracy yesterday afternoon in connection with the seizure, by Chief Preventive Officer Clarke on November 7 of a quantity of raw and prepared opium found concealed in a disused pipe in the ship's stoke-room.

Mr. L. D. Turner appeared on behalf of the defendants.

C. P. O. Clarke, in the witness box, said that, acting upon information received, he boarded the s.s. "Haiching" on November 7. He entered the stoke-room and, after searching and removing a quantity of coal, he found a quantity of opium concealed in a disused pipe. Altogether he seized 360 lbs. of raw opium and 245 lbs. of non-Government opium. Officers of the ship gave him every assistance in his search.
Cross-examined. witness said that there were five or six feet of coal on top of the disused pipe.
Mr. Turner: Why did you issue the warrant?—My instructions from the Superintendent are to issue a warrant in every case where there is reason to believe that someone on board the ship is implicated.

Captain Alan H. Stewart said that he was master of the s.s. "Hutching." He had a been employed by the owners for 23 years, of which for 17 years he had been in charge of one or other of their vessels. This was the first occasion upon which his company had been summoned under the Opium Ordinance. Witness had four Indian guards and three officers on board. The officers and crew were informed that they must be on the look out for smugglers and the ship was searched before leaving every port and that was done on the voyage in question.

His Worship: Once or twice?—After leaving Amoy and again after leaving Swatow.

Continuing, witness said that it was four or five years since his vessel had used oil fuel. He had no reason to suspect any member of his crew as being implicated in any way with opium smuggling. His present crew had been with him only since the strike. His ship was always coaled when in Hong Kong. In witness' opinion, the opium must have been put on board some considerable time ago.

Reviewing the heroic defence of the "Sunning" by its officers against the pirates who had obtained control, the "Peking and Tientsin Times" states:—

The recovery of a pirated steamer seized by forty despernados, by two unarmed officers is a fent of gallantry and endurance seldom if ever equalled in the annals of the British merchant marine. The "Sunning," unlike ships on the Northern run, "was equipped with grilles, in addition to wireless. The former proved no protection. The latter, as we have always anticipated, proved useless at the time of the ship's capture. It was simply the courage of a handful of men that saved the vessel from the usual fate of ships captured in China waters, and ensured the death of capture of nearly all the pirates. It is difficult to find words in which to express one's admiration of the bravery of the officers responsible for recapturing the vessel. It is equally difficult to find strong enough words to condemn those responsible for tolerating a state of affairs under which this kind of crime flourishes.

In addition, to countless piracies of native craft, and a prute raid in view of Hong Kong harbour, itself, the Southern pirates have captured no fewer than six coasting steamers—two of them vessels on the Northern run—during the past twelve months. They seized the C.N.S. "Tungchow," on December 18, last year, the French steamer "Jade" on February 8, the C.M.S.N.Co.'s str. "Kwanglee" on July 13, the same Company's vessel "Hsinfung" on October 1, the French steamer "Hanow" about November 11, and the "Sunning" last Monday. In every case but one—the "Kwanglee," which was run ashore near Macao—the pirates have steered for Bias Bay. In every case but one—the "Sunning"—they have escaped with their booty, and in some instances, with kidnapped passengers. Bias Bay is less than one hundred miles from Canton, the seat of "the most efficient of all the authorities in China" (video Colonel Malone), and less than 25 miles from the important city of Waichow, yet not a finger has been raised against this pirate rest by the local Chinese authorities, although, as the "Tungchow" Court of Inquiry put it, "the only satisfactory way of dealing with the pirate menace is to destroy the pirate bases ashore, without which attacks such as that on the s.s. "Tungchow" would be impossible. And the British Navy, conforming to the policy of patience and conciliation—which apparently recognises even the "sovereign right" to piracy—has taken no action against the Bias Bay pests.

Significance of Struggle.
Is this policy of inaction on the part of the “National Canton Government” and the British Navy to continue? The “Sunning” epic reveals what can be done by a handful of brave men, who, in this case appear to have escaped with few casualties, and comparatively unimportant damage to their steamer. But it is well to emphasise the probable consequences of this midnight struggle. Hitherto the Bias Bay pirates have generally refrained from molesting the foreign passengers and officers after they have once gained control of the vessel. In future such moderation is not to be expected. The heroism of the “Sunning” officers will jeopardise the lives of every foreign officers and passenger who may fall into Chinese pirates' hands. These pirates, as events on the “Sunning” have shown, are desperate ruffians. Their threats to set fire to captured vessels are not mere bluff. And unless strong measures are taken immediately for the destruction of this pirate stronghold, we may at any moment expect news of a much more tragic piracy than that of which the cables have brought us the news during the past two days. Wireless installations, grilles, armed guards, searches of passengers, etc., are only palliative measures, while pirates can continue to land, unmolested, with their loot, within fifty miles of Hong Kong.

The “Shanghai Mercury” concludes a review of the action of the officers in the following terms:—Another epic has enriched the tales told of the turbulent sea... Britishers may well be proud of the fact that the officers on board the steamer, “Sunning,” were faithful to the finest traditions of the mercantile marine and the British Navy. Heroism was shown in a marked degree, as the story unfolded testifies.

## [10] N.Y.K.LINE

The question now arises as to what course of action is to be taken to rid the China Coast of these pests. Word came the other day that the Kwangtung authorities were prepared to cooperate with a view to cleaning out the pirates' nests but the Canton Government did not seem inclined to take rigorous measures. Surely the Powers will not accept this attitude as final, and the strongest representations should be at once made to a bolstered administration which craves recognition and pretends to cherish advanced Republican ideals.
The honours of the fight went to the gallant men on board the "Sunning," rather than the Government. Again tribute has to be paid to the individuals who can rise above difficulties, with brain and brawn, and conquer when the odds are against them. The "Sunning" incident is worthy of a proud place when the history of the "China, Coast" comes to be written.
