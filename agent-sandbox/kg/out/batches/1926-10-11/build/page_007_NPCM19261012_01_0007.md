## [3] BRITISH SHIPPING.

### Feelers Put Out by Local Companies.

Now that the official declaration by Canton of the cessation of boycott activities has been made, the local British shipping companies are seeking to ascertain the position as regards trade by the only practical method—by sending up ships the result of which trips will largely determine future plans as regards sailing dates.

The China Navigation Company (Butterfield and Swire) s.s. "Sinkiang" left Hong Kong for Canton yesterday, this being an extension of the run from Shanghai to Hong Kong. Before the strike, this, together with other ships, was on the Shanghai-Hong Kong-Canton run.

First Since Strike.

The "Sinkiang" is the first of these B. and S. ships, since the boycott trouble originated to make the trip to Canton. It berthed at the B. and S. wharf at Pak-hok-tung and will leave later for Hong Kong and Shanghai. No regular schedule has as yet been arranged, the sailing of the "Sinkiang" being in the nature of a test trip.
To the Indo-China S. N. Co. (Jar'dime's) vessels on the same run (the "Foishing" and "Cheongshing" have already made trips to Canton and left later for Shanghai) has been added the "Hopsang" which leaves for Canton at daybreak tomorrow.

## [5] TRAFFIC CASES

**EUROPEAN INSULTED BY TAXI DRIVER.**

**THE WEEKLY LIST.**

The dangerous practice of carrying protruding beams from motor lorries was referred to in the traffic summonses heard by Major C. Willson at the Central Magistracy this morning.

Finés ranging up to $12 were imposed on the offenders among whom were a number of car drivers caught driving in a dangerous or negligent manner.

The offence of a driver in disregarding a signal to stop and scattering a squad of police near the Queen's Theatre was considered to be more serious than the others and this was met with a fine of $20.

A taxicab driver, charged with using abusive language to his fare, Mr. T. F. O'Sullivan, denied the offence, but admitted that he failed to complete the journey.
Traffic Sergt. Hopkins said that the complainant was absent, as he thought the case would be heard next week.

A suggestion from the defendant that the passenger failed to understand him was turned down by Sub-Inspector Alexander and His Worship adjourned the summons for one week to hear evidence.

## [6] Y.M.C.A. NOTES:

**FIRST LADIES' NIGHT.**

## [7] LOTTERY TICKETS.

**CHINESE WITH A LARGE COLLECTION.**

**SKIPPED HIS BAIL**

A Chinese named Leung Min, alleged to be an extensive dealer in illegal lottery ventures, failed to appear at the Central Magistracy this morning to answer a charge of possession of 683 Macao lottery tickets of a face value of $7,956.

Defendant was arrested with the tickets at Upper Lascar Row yesterday by a Chinese detective and was released on bail of $250.

It was stated that defendant had been arrested along with three others one year ago for a similar offence, but on that occasion also he failed to attend court while out of bail.

The Magistrate ordered the bail money to be estreated.

## [10] WUCHANG LATEST.

How Treachery Sold Besieged
Generals

Wuchang is now in the hands of the southerners' Army. Many disarmed northern troops landed at Hankow today (Monday).—Naval Despatch.

## [17] ASHORE.

**STRANGE DISCOVERY ON AN ISLAND.**

**33 PEOPLE ALIVE.**

A strange tale of the sea has been revealed in a report made at Stanley Police Station "by Chan Tong, master of the Cheung Hok grocery shop at Po To Au Island, which is near Clear Water Bay and on the other side of Junk Bay.

At 9 o'clock on Saturday morning a report was made at the shop by boat people that a number of people had been washed ashore alive at the back of the island.

Shop employees were sent out to investigate and found the story to be true, as they found 30 men and three women on the beach, apparently the survivors of a junk which had foundered in the equally weather during the weekend.

Some of the survivors appeared to be in a bad way with the hardships they had undergone in swimming ashore. The master of the shop took them all in and provided them with food and clothing.

The people spoke the Hoklo dialect, and the police are without any further report from them to account for their being cast on the island.

A Chinese was fined $250, or three months' hard labour, by Mr. E. W. Hamilton at the Central Magistracy this morning for having several tons of coal aboard a junk at Shaukiwan in regard to which he was unable to give a satisfactory explanation.

## [18] STRIKES OBSOLETE.

**REMARKABLE ADDRESS BY LABOURITE**

**EXTREMISTS' OUTBURSTS**

*Rugby, Oct. 11.*

Lively scenes marked the opening of the annual conference of the Labour Party at Margate.
Mr. Robert Williams, in delivering the presidential address, dealt with the mining crisis. He declared that preparations for this industrial struggle had not been confined to the coal owners and those supporting them. Individuals in the Labour Party's own ranks had constantly talked of preparing for it. The Labour Party's own ranks had constantly talked of preparing for it. The Labour Party was the foremost party in condemning militarism in international affairs. If peace was to be sought internationally by methods of conciliation, let industrial peace be sought by the same methods. They must preserve the right of Unions to strike, but conciliation played an important part in their work.

## [19] *(no headline: column 5)*

Men would be heard saying: "Get ready for the next general strike." He would reply that they should read the unfavourable agreements upon which the railwaymen and transport workers resumed work after the general strike last May. He had had over twelve years' experience of strikes, and he knew that it was not easy to secure a return to work on terms as satisfactory as those obtaining before action was taken.

In regard to the miners, Mr. Williams declared:

"Their decision to continue the dispute is heroic. They may be likened to a sightless Samson feeling for the grip of the pillars of the temple, the crashing of which may engulf this thing which we call British civilisation."

The Miners' Conference, he said, asked for an embargo on foreign coal. An embargo had been ineffective during the cbal stoppage of 1921. What hope was there of success in 1926? It was a despairing policy.

He concluded: "The Communist Party and minority movement still believe in a general strike. The Labour Party looks with confidence to the General Election."

## [20] Sentiment Applauded.

Mr. Williams's outspoken remarks were greeted with violent outbursts from the extremists present. He was charged with making insolent attacks on the miners. At times he continued only with difficulty, but his final remark—to the effect that the Labour Party stood for constitutional action—was loudly applauded by the great majority of the delegates present at the conference.—British Wireless Service.
