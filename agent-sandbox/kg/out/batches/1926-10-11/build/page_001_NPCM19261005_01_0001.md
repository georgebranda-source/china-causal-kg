## [8] REPORTED THREAT OF USING. FORCE IF NECESSARY.

Optimism has not increased with regard to the proposed settlement of the Canton boycott and latest developments point to more difficulties for the Nationalist Government in their attempt to arrive at a solution.

Conditions at Lowu railway station, on the New Territory border are now the same as before, practically the full complement of strike pickets being on duty on the frontier.

Passengers on the Kowloon-Canton Railway are now subjected to the same interference as in previous months.

Fresh difficulties—in addition to opposition by a minority of politicians—are confronting the Canton Government.

One point that may further complicate the issue is the reported demand of the strikers for their share of the projected new taxes.

Besides their claim to an interest in the import, luxury and export levies, the strikers are asking for a Government allocation of $10,000 a month as assistance.

Another request is that the Government, the Strike Executive "and all classes" co-operate to form

employment agencies to facilitate the return to work of strikers, the quota to be 3,000 per month, each man on returning to work to be presented with a $100 bonus by the Government as recognition for the "share in the victory."

This scheme is to last for eight months, it is stated, when the movement will be entirely ended. "Meanwhile, the strikers ask that dormitories and free meals continue as hitherto "until a big scheme can be devised to effect a settlement."

## [12] LATEST PIRACY.

**H. K. SEAPLANE IN PURSUIT.**

### Shanghai Steamer on the Way

The China Merchants S. N. Co.'s s.s. "Hsinfung" has been pirated at Bias Bay.

Only mengre details are to hand at the moment but there seems no room for doubt.

As in the case of the "Kwang Lee" (of the same company), the steamer was on the Shanghai-Canton direct run.

In pre-strike times these boats called at Hong Kong en route, but this port is now omitted.

This morning, the China Navigation Co.'s s.s. "Szechuan" left Hong Kong for Amoy and Shanghai.

She wirelessed back a report that the "Hsinfung" had been seen to be pirated in Bias Bay, that hotbed of pirates in the province of Kwangtung.

On receipt of the message, the local Naval authorities despatched a seaplane to scout over the vicinity and report.

The “Szechuan” also indicated that the pirated steamer was on her way to Hong Kong but the local office of the China Merchants. Co. state that she may go round Hong Kong, direct to Canton, unless there are casualties on board which may need urgent medical attention.

## [13] A. Surmise.

The "Hstinfung," of course, is of the usual type of China. Merchants steamer. Before the strike, she occasionally called here, but was mostly on the northern runs.

Few European passengers travel by this route to Canton.

## [17] WAR CONTINUES BUT ARMISTICE QUITE POSSIBLE.

Latest despatches from the Kiangsi-Hupch war zone indicate that the southerners are now standing their ground and fighting desperately against Sun Chuan-fang, whose advance appears to have been stemmed.

The possibility of an armistice has become less remote: While at one place the rival armies are struggling zealously, other detachments on the same front are marking time.

[From Our Special Correspondent.]

The second battle of Kao. An is likely to prove the deciding engagement of the war.

At a vulnerable point in his line on the Kiangsi-Hunan front, General Chang Kai-shek has rushed up his best divisions and is now holding his ground.

The “5 province allies” under Marshal Sun Chuan-fang are still attacking and the fighting is very severe.

A little further north, that is in the Yangtse-Lake Tayeh sector, the southerners are under General Tang Sang-chee (8th army) and General Li Chungjen (7th army).

Bombardment Continues.

About 30,000 troops withdrawn from the pursuit against Wy Peifu, from Hankow and vicinity, and other places, have been moved eastwards to prevent Sun Chuanfang getting any nearer Wu-chang.

The bombardment, by the northern allies, continues at Hwang-chow, which is considered the key to Wuchang-hsien, the capital of Hupeh province which the Nationalists took from Wu Pei-fu.

In the south of Kiangsi, neither side is doing much and the allied commanders there still favour peace as the southerners have not vacated this part of their territory and they prefer to get it back without fighting.

Position Summed Up.

Summed up, the situation is: near Yangtse, the allies are still trying to push on to Wuchang and the 7th Canton army had difficulty in retiring from an advanced position. In the centre Chang Kai-shek has now given up, rearguard actions but is throwing his main strength in to divert attention from Wuchang. In the south, a comparative state of it is felt that the possibility of statemate prevails:

an armistice at this stage is less remote but the deciding battle may be over by this week.

Yesterday's report which gave the number of Canton prisoners taken by the allies as 5,000 is described as exaggerated. The total captures by Sun Chuan-fang are unlikely to exceed 3,000.

## [18] OFFICIAL DESPATCH.

The following is published by courtesy of the Hong Kong Naval authorities:—

*Hankow, Oct. 4. still holding out*

An anti-British demonstration has been planned for tomorrow, Tuesday, but will probably not amount to much.

Southern flags are disappearing in the Chinese city and in the ex-Russian Concession and there is a general feeling that the northerners will be back here shortly.

Heavy fighting is reported between Wang Shih-ko and Hwang-chow; the former is held by the northerners and the latter by the southerners.

It is reported that a big drive towards Nanchang will be made by the southerners to relieve the pressure on Hankow.

General Yang Sen is sending large forces to Ichang and Shash against the southerners.

## [20] DESERVED CONFIDENCE.

"When you next write to Shanghai about this situation," said one of Marshal Sun Chuangfang's staff officers in parting yesterday, "please be cheerful about it. Tell the people that we cannot possibly lose that with Chang Kai-shek being pushed back into Honan we have broken the Red advance, and that the conclusion of this campaign is coming quickly and surely. It would surprise none of us if the Cantonese were in full retreat before a letter could reach Shanghai."

This sanguine statement was the conclusion of an attempted interview with Marshal Sun Chuang-fang on the China Merchants' str. "Kingfishin" at Ki-kiang.

It may sound over-confident, but it does no more than confirm the general impression that one gets coming down the river; after getting well away from pessimistic and poorly informed Hankow.

## [21] In Steel Helmets.

<sub>section under [20] DESERVED CONFIDENCE.</sub>

The first surprise that one gets is the spectacle of the striped flag flying over Huangshihkang, the port for Tayen, and of great numbers of steel helmetted northern soldiers on the foreshore.

From this point down to Wusueh one sees tented encampments of the northerners at every little settlement and then at Wusueh there is General Chen Tiao-yuan's big concentration.

In Kuukiang one finds the community in the grasp of a very well equipped and seemingly serious and business-like soldier who inspire confidence at a glance.

The general plan of campaign is to drive westward from various points in Kiangsi, in several columns, towards the Hukuang railway, and the Siang river and thereby endanger the Red line of communications and force their withdrawal from Hupeh and northern Hunan.

If carried out swiftly enough this withdrawal will of necessity be a rout, since the Cantonese and Humaneese Reds have gone so far afield over routes in which communications are bad. If they have to run to get back before being cut off, it is assumed that there will be no great difficulty about keeping them running well into southern Hunan and over the Kwangtung border.

Apart from the Shantung Brigade at Huangshihkang and the outposts along the river, there is no force above Wusueh and there is, as yet, no thought of a direct advance upon the Wu-Han cities by the river route.

The big fight, however, in the sight of both parties, is that being waged between Nanchang and Pinghsiang, with Chang Kai-shek and his two best Cantonese divisions, including the Whampoa Cadets, on the one hand, and Governor Lu Hsiang-ting, with five brigades of Chekiang troops and several brigades of Kiangsi troops on the other.

If Chang is badly defeated in this quarter, his whole expedition is a wreck and the Hunanese and Cantonese north of Changzh will be helpless refugees in an alien land.
