## [7] Serious Developments.

Rugby, Nov. 29.
Sir Austen Chamberlain, Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs, was questioned in the House of Commons by Mr. Ramsay MacDonald, Leader of the Opposition, regarding the condition of affairs at Hankow. He replied that serious developments in the situation had taken place there during the past week and a grave anti-foreign movement appeared to be at work.

## [8] HANKOW TROUBLE.

### American Warships Move.

The Flotilla Leader Keppel went to Hankow this morning. The American destroyers "Peary" and "John D. Ford left yesterday. —Reuter.

A union of the lower grade employees of the Chinese Maritime Customs was formed on November 21 with the support of the superintendent who was a Chinese official. This union had announced that its aim was to oust the foreign element from the management of the service and bring it under purely Chinese control.
The "Commissioner of Customs hoped it might be "possible" to keep the Customs House open and maintain the light service for foreign nations, but this might be difficult. The Customs House, which was in the Chinese City was picketed.

## [9] "Situation Essier."

<sub>section under [8] HANKOW TROUBLE.</sub>

The latest report was that the situation in some respects appeared to be easier. The union had presented its demands, and these were not expected to be of an impossible nature, though it might be that they were only a preliminary move.

At the same time the general strike had made great progress. Chinese servants of Japanese were already on strike, and Japanese had had to import their food supplies from other centres.

The Communist section was showing great activity, and there was a fear of a general strike being forced. This, if carried out, would throw out of employment thousands of the lower grade workers, and it would be easy to incite these to riot. The situation was being watched by British Government.

Sir Austen Chamberlain, replying to a further question, said the Foreign Office had received a cable from the British community at Hankow stating that the situation was very grave indeed, that they were in daily expectation of an incident which would involve blood-shed, and that the naval forces were not adequate for protection of the community, and asking the Government to give an assurance that these forces would be strengthened. Sir Austen Chamberlain said that this appeal was under the consideration of the Government, including the Admiralty.

In official circles in London it is pointed out that Hankow is easily defensible. Though it is 800 miles from the mouth of the Yangtse, the River at that point is a mile and a half wide, and comparatively large battleships can lie off the waterfront—British Wireless Service.

## [13] Views of "The Times."

<sub>section under [12] Navali Forces.</sub>

*London, Nov. 29.*

The seriousness of the Cantonese advance in China is emphasised in a lender in the "Times" which says that it is a very serious and urgent matter to the foreign interests, especially British which are now very greatly endangered and for the British Government there is "at the present time in the international sphere no more urgent question. Measures, it says must be taken immediately to protect British nationals who are endangered on the Yangtze and it must clearly be longescen that the opportunities of provoking further conflict are innumerable and that the worse enemies of Britain are watching to take advantage of them.

He adds that hesitation is a very dangerous policy for all, and that to trust vaguely in some ultimate Chinese reaction by anti-Bolsheviks is wholly unprofitable, for by the time the reaction comes there may be very little left for us to save and the state of China herself will be appalling.—Reuter.

## [19] *(no headline: column 5)*

Much significance is attached to the arrest, by the highest military authority in Canton, of the chairman of the Executive of the Strike Committee and four other delegates who have all along had an active voice in the management of the boycott of Hong Kong.

On Sunday night, a detachment from the headquarters (in Canton) of the commander-in-chief (General Chang Kni-shek), was sent out to make the arrests.

Mr. Sp Shiu-ching is the Strike chairman, sitting on the Executive of delegates as the representative of the Seamen's Union.

In the 1922 shipping strike at Hong Kong, he was one of the leaders. Subsequently one of his colleagues fell into disgrace following a scandal, and the first general secretary of the Hong Kong Union seceded after a dispute which culminated in a stormy meeting in the Des Voeux Road headquarters, at which the Police were present.

## [20] " Who the Chairman Is:

Mr. So presided at that momentous meeting and since then" has been head of the Seamen, with offices at Canton.

When the Strike began last June, he became chairman of the Strike Executive, the highest position in the movement.

Earlier this year, some of the delegates who sat on the Strike tribunal were detained by the Nationalist Government but what happened to them is not definitely known.

Mr. So was also the spokesman of the strikers when a delegation of Hong Kong Chinese merchants went up last year to explore the possibilities of a settlement.

## [21] Speculation Rife

<sub>section under [20] " Who the Chairman Is:</sub>

Three weeks ago, the Strike headquarters at East Garden, Canton, were destroyed by a fire. A committee of investigation reported that “enemies of the movement” had committed arson.

The present opinion is that, accusations were laid against the Strike Executive by two bodies. Formerly, it had been alleged that several millions of dollars had to be accounted for, and a semi-official announcement followed to the effect that an authoritative statement of accounts would be presented when ready.

Speculation is rife as to the motive behind the arrest of Mr. So. In the circumstances the Canton Government can hardly be expected to make a statement, at least until investigations are complete.

## [24] Merchants' View.

The report, it is interesting to note, says that Mr. So and his four colleagues are under close detention at general headquarters.

Merchants, of course, regard this incident in the light of the Canton authorities making a definite move to curb boycott agitation, it having been announced that the Nationalist policy is to preserve communications.

## [25] SEAMEN'S TROUBLE.

### Canton Government Not to Intervene?

Intimidation — it is called “peaceful picketting”—continues against intercourse with the Steamboat Co.'s vessels and wharves at Canton.

Other than providing armed police to preserve order generally at the wharves, steps have still to be taken to curb the activities of ex-seamen strikers, who are alleged to be sponsored by their Union.

The seamen want the dismissal of certain members of the crews in addition to their own reinstatement and a deadlock has been reached in the negotiations.

## [27] OTHER DIFFICULTIES.

### Problems for Nationalists to Solve.

Several other difficulties are engaging the attention of the Nationalist and the Canton (municipal) Governments.

The four Chambers of Commerce have petitioned the Municipality to take precautions to protect the public food supply, in view of the portents of the present general strike at rice shops.

The waterworks dispute is still outstanding.

In connection with the removal of the seat of Nationalist Government from Canton to Wuchang, a number of obstacles “behind the scenes” remain to be cleared.

One section of the officials are stated "to be not in favour—although not openly opposing—the drastic change. Their argument is that Kwangtung is the home of the Party and is still the most important province.

Military Powers.
Gossip is also busy with the delay in the assumption by General Li Chai-sum of the post of head of the Military Department.

General Li is acting commander-in-chief of the Canton garrison, in the absence of General Chang Kai-shek. He is also the commander of the 4th Nationalist Army, two divisions of which (the 10th and 12th) have won the highest honours in the war.

The disposition of the Canton Navy is blamed for the delay. The present Commander of the Navy is reported to be reluctant to go under the control of General Li and he is said to have communicated with General Chang Kai-shek in the matter.

The creation of the new department of military affairs was approved by the last Kuomintang Conference in Canton.
