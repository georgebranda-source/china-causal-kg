## [9] HONG KONG'S POSITION.

**IMPROVEMENT SINCE THE BOYCOTT.**

**OVERSEAS MONEY FOR PROPERTY: "NOT DEVOID OF HOPE."**

Hong Kong's trade position has improved since the "settlement of the boycott." After extensive inquiries the situation vis-à-vis Canton is "summed up as "not devoid of hope."

In spite of agitation and intimidation, I can state on the best authority that at least $3,000,000 worth of "piece" goods has been sent from Hong Kong to Canton since Oct. 10, when the blockade of this Colony was voluntarily called off by the Strike Committee.

Much has been said about the restrictions to trade through the boycott not having completely ended, but from investigations I have made, I have come to the conclusion that a good deal of the recent impprovement in the Colony has been directly or indirectly attributable to the settlement.

In Nam: Pak Hong the sugar market has had a small boom now that former outlets have been reopened. Not only are the products of the two local refineries exported from Hong Kong but large quantities of raw sugar are transhipped here to the two Kwang provinces and other parts of China.

The property market has shown signs of a new lease of life but the hapse of the Rents Restriction Ordinance and other causes have also contributed.

Metals and other foreign manufactures have found their way up to Canton, Wuchow, &c., as I saw on making a trip this week at the invitation of the “China Mail.” “While some trades have been adversely affected by the slump in the dollar, others have received benefit.

Gold currencies having gone up, Chinese emigrants are remitting more money home, mostly through exchange banks in Hong Kong. Shops known as “California” dealers have done more business now that intercourse has been resumed with the interior.

## [11] Good Sign of the Times.

<sub>section under [9] HONG KONG'S POSITION.</sub>

Good trade in Malaya and other places down South has resulted in more money, earned by overseas Chinese, being sent back to Hong Kong.

This week there were several inquiries by brokers, on behalf of principals returned from Singapore, for Chinese house property situated on the central Praya.

Obviously such premises are required for business purposes—a very desirable sign of the times.

Because landlords are not now encumbered by law and are at liberty to retrieve possession, there is now inducement for merchants to buy property for their own accommodation, in preference to paying higher rentals elsewhere.

## [12] Abundance of Money?

<sub>section under [9] HONG KONG'S POSITION.</sub>

House owners have been able to increase rents, thereby enhancing the relative value. Some of those who secured mortgages during the boom have now found buyers who paid enough to let them wipe off the loans.

It is not strictly correct to say that money is plentiful. The absence of other forms of investment has deflected funds (in the hands of native bankers) to the property market.

Interest on mortgage has lowered slightly but there is no hard and fast rate. Quotations vary somewhat: Short terms and long margins command low rates. Advances are worked out in ratio to the desirability of the security so that fairly high-interest will have to be offered on suburban lots.

## [13] Feeling of Restraint.

<sub>section under [9] HONG KONG'S POSITION.</sub>

Exchange fluctuations and uncertainty as to the future have jointly mitigated against new business in the piece goods market. But when the blockade was lifted, dealers in both Canton and Hong Kong did their best to replenish the very low stocks in Canton.
Mostly in fiscal shipments, lots have been shipped up to meet the autumn and winter requirements in Canton, but a feeling of restraint available.
This uneasiness is not due entirely to the threat that British goods will not be handled after Nov. 8. Having satisfied the first rush so to speak, dealers are waiting for the next development before further committing themselves. Such prudence can hardly be deplored.

## [16] NOT SCOTSMEN

**EGYPTIAN RATHER THAN BE**

**K.O.S.B. "BOMBSHELL."**

Scotsmen prefer to be Egyptians than Englishmen or Americans!

Such was the startling discovery made by a Highlander while riding on a lower level tram in Hong Kong.

There were two young men who hotly denied that they were Sassenachs or Yankees and, ex-asperated, pronounced themselves to be "natives of Egypt. They were two privates of the 2nd. King's Own Scottish Borderers, the new infantry regiment on the station.

The Tramway Co. extends to men in the Services the right of paying half-fare while travelling in uniform. Men of foreign Armies and Navies are debarred. Under Suspicion.

If the two Privates concerned see this, they must excuse the conductor who, although plainly tactless and ignorant of military pageant, was merely doing his duty. Men in uniform, who were neither in the British Army or Navy, had proferred ten cents in the past and unknowingly pocketed five cents change till a European inspector happened to examine the ticket.

This conductor was trying to ascertain if the two Scottish soldiers were in the British forces, evidently viewing with suspicion the tarant trews they wore. He particularly disliked their Glengarry caps which, in his humble opinion, too "closely resembled the "hat of disgrace" mated out to the vixen-foccd, white-bearded villain of the Chinese stage type of mandarin.

The Good Sembritan.
His vocabulary did not include the word British so he asked, when offered ten cents for two first-class fares: — "You Engish?"

"No," returned the Privates with sorrow plainly written on their young faces.
In a moment of inspiration, the conductor rejoined: "You, the American?"

“No, we are Egyptian,” roared one of the couple but further trouble was saved when a gentle man who was on the train and had overheard the conversation went to the rescue.

## [22] ELEMENT OF SURPRISE

### [By Our Special Correspondent.]

Relying almost entirely on the element of surprise, the Southerners sent their division of "Iron troops' miles and miles behind the front to bring off what Marshal Sun Chuan-fang believed the impossible.

It was not the Whampoa Cadets who brought off the coup. The hero is Major-General Chan Ming-shu; who commands a division of

the 4th Army and was formerly stationed at Kongmoon. His men have particularly distinguished themselves in the whole of the present campaign.

While the Northerners clung to the banks of the Yangtse and progressed slowly forwards, this division of mystery men stole round their flank and reached Tean by forced marches. Taking the route over hilly country, they took this railway town without any difficulty and shot northwards, moving nearly 40 miles in one night.

They were about 4,000 strong and soon vanquished the comparatively small garrison left behind by the Northerners.

This division is still in danger of being enveloped but the Northern cause has received such a shock that General Chan is likely to hold on.
