## [12] DESTROYERS HERE.

**ARRIVAL WITNESSED BY CROWDS.**

**IMPRESSIVE SIGHT.**

Watchers on the Praya at 5.45 p.m. yesterday witnessed an impressive sight in the arrival of H.M.S. "Keppel" and the third Destroyer Flotilla.

Their arrival was somewhat in the nature of a surprise in view of arrival having been timed originally for to-day and of later reports indicating that it was not deemed probable that they would arrive before Sunday.

The best point of vantage was the west of the Praya, in the neighbourhood of the Steamboat Company's wharf, as here, the eight vessels comprising the flotilla, were observed entering together from the Sulphur channel.

The "Keppel" led the way, followed by the others in line, four halting at the Yaumati Admiralty anchorage and the "Keppel" and three others proceeding to the central Admiralty anchorage. The progress of the vessels was watched by interested crowds, particularly at the ferry wharf. Two ferries crossing the harbour at the time had to stand by in mild harbour whilst the destroyers passed

## [14] NIGHT-BOATS FOR TRADE!

Nationalist Attitude on the New

Surtaxes.

Efforts to resume pre-strike conditions have now developed to an extent when each new shipping departure is considered a matter of routine. There is not that importance attached to each movement that there was at the beginning of the week; and it will soon be too heavy a task to note each individual trip.

To practically every port in Kwangtung, and to Wuchow in Kwangsi, ships are now leaving from Hong Kong.

But about the best news so far is the fact that the authorities in Canton are providing detachments of police to accord protection and preserve order, when the steamers from Hong Kong go alongside wharves.

## [18] Those Paying Levies.

The Chinese, they say, prefer to trade on night boats as they have practically all day—instead of a few hours—in which to work cargo, on account of the different hours of arrival and departure.

There are no night sailings now because of the existence of martial law at Whampoa.

From inquiries made by the “China Mail” it appears that the Canton Surtax Bureau has not made any determined attempt to collect the “domestic levies” on any thorough scale.

The majority of those who have gone to the Bureau made declarations and paid, appear to be Chinese exporters. As far as is known, staffs from the Bureau have not been sent to out-stations.

An impression is gaining ground that the Nationalist Government is content to let matters remain as they are until the attitude of the Powers becomes more defined, hoping still that it will be possible to work through the Chinese Maritime Customs. Accordingly, it is thought that until the Diplomatic Corps at Peking takes action, Canton will let the temporary arrangements stand for the present.

Peking, to-night, is wondering if tacit recognition has been given to Dr. Koo's government—a recognition carefully withheld from many past cabinets.

What the Ministers Did:
"It happened thus:—This morning Dr. Koo made the round" of the Legations, paying formal calls and announcing to the Ministers of the Powers the fact that he has assumed office.

Late this afternoon, the Japanese, Belgian, Netherlands, Spanish, Norwegian, and Chilean Ministers called upon Dr. Koo in a body, and many other ministers called by proxy or sent cards before 5.30. The British, American, French and Italian Ministers did not call.

For many years in Peking official calls of this kind have been held tantamount to recognition, and the capital-is agog with excitement and speculating over, the turn of affairs.
Hostilities on Banks of Yangtse River.

Meanwhile the influence of Chang Tso-lin in the new government bulks larger and larger. Several cabinet officials are known to have withheld consent to taking the portfolios until they heard from the Mukden warlord by wire, and Dr. Koo himself read to the cabinet at noon to day a congratulatory message from Chang in which the Marshall addresses him as "brother." It is considered noteworthy that no publicity has been given to any messages from Wu Pei-fu—if, indeed, any have been received.

Even if to-day's calls do mean recognition coming to Dr. Koo he will have his hands full. The government is bankrupt; there is no coal in the city, and little prospect of getting any before winter sets in; unemployment is general, and business very poor.

## [21] ON TWO FLANKS.

### Southerners' Drives on Kiangsi.

**ALLIES CLAIM VICTORIES.**

Reinforced by men of the 8th Army brought up from Wuchang, the southerners made another attack on Marshal Sun Chuan-fang along the banks of the Yangtse River.

Simultaneous with this fresh drive on Kiukiang, an offensive was launched on Nanchang.

A decision has not yet been reached on either sector but the northern_Allies claim to have repulsed the Cantonese and driven them back.

The 8th Army, it will be recalled, was engaged in laying siege to Wuchang. When that city fell, several thousands of men were immediately transferred to the Kiangsi-Hupeh sector. ## [23] IN THE KIANGSI ZONE.

### Southerners' Drive on Nanchang Sector.

A batch of war reports received last night by mail confirm, the cabled messages published during the last few days. The gist is that the southerners came perilously near to taking Kiukiang, but have since been pushed back, as reported in the "China Mail."

The southern troops have once again begun to take the offensive in the Nanchang sector and are now engaged in fierce fighting with the Allied forces. It is reported that the railway in the neighbourhood of Lohwa has been destroyed again by the southern troops.

General Chen Tiao-yuan's (northern) troops have once more been transported in the direction of Wusueh apparently due to the advance of the southerners towards Chichun. Judging from all accounts available, the war situation, it appears, is reversing against the Allied forces.—“Toho.”
