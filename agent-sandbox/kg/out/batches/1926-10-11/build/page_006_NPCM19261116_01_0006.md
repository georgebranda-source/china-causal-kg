## [12] HELPING THE POLICE.

In the records of the local Police Force there is no more tragic phrase than "Might have been." Prosecution after prosecution might have been successful—only that the very material point of Chinese assistance was lacking. Criminal after criminal might have been tracked to earth—but for the lamentable fact that Chinese who could have assisted the Police were seized with a timidity that overwhelmed all considerations, including adequate protection for the Chinese themselves. Many a Chinese ruffian might have been caught red-handed—but for the sheer obstinacy of the victimised Chinese in preserving a stolid silence for fear of some imaginary consequences or from dread of being brought into active touch with the Police. There are hundreds,—if not indeed thousands—of Chinese who regard the Police not as guardians of the law and deterrents to evil-doers, but as some strange terror to be avoided as the plague. Such is human nature—of the average Chinese of the lower classes.
Alongside “Might have been” the Chief Justice at yesterday’s Criminal Sessions placed the equally significant word “If.” Commenting on the “hold up” of a Chinese couple at Glenealy His Lordship remarked that “if more people would act as these gentlemen did we would not have so many of these cases.” The story that led to this observation is simply told. The Chinese coupled in question were accosted by a Chinese at the point of a revolver (subsequently discovered to be a toy one), and whilst the girl was handing over her valuables her male companion seized the ruffian. The girl’s screams attracted two Europeans and they rushed to the scene and helped to overpower the desperation, who well merited his sentence of three years' imprisonment.
This, of course, is a departure from the average Chinese psychology. In ninety-nine cases out of a hundred, the Chinese victims

would have handed over all their valuables and permitted their assailant to depart. If subsequently questioned by the Police they would have suddenly developed an attack of forgetfulness as to the personal appearance of the criminal. "All the king's horses and all the king's men could not extract from them one word to assist the Police in their work of endeavouring to trace the desperado. Thus is the task of the Police magnified unnecessarily. Thus do criminals get off scot free. Thus is justice frustrated—all for the lack of a little cooperation on the part of the Chinese victims with the Police. It is a curious trait indeed, but, so long as Chinese human nature is what it is, it is hopeless to expect anything better. A time may come when the average Chinese will wake up to the fact that he owes a duty to himself and to the community to help and not to hinder the Police. Meanwhile we can only welcome an exception to the rule when it is disclosed in Court and re-echo the sentiments of the Chief Justice as so ably expressed yesterday.

## [14] Shipping Boycott.

The news that the shipping boycott at Canton has been called off is of the first importance, alike for the immediate impetus it will give to trade between Hong Kong and Canton, and for the hope it holds out that all obstacles to trade between the two ports are about to disappear. There has been no doubt for some time now that the Canton Government has been sincerely trying to end the boycott. That it has not succeeded before this has been due in large measure to the strength of the pickets. The Canton Government has felt it wiser to play the waiting game rather than to take any drastic steps which might be interpreted as an affront to Labour. The waiting game has paid up to the point that the shipping boycott has now been called off. Let us hope it will prove as affective with the remainder of the pickets. Once these gentry have been removed, there will remain no further obstacles to unrestricted trade now that ships of all flags are at liberty to trade to Canton once more. That unrestricted trade is what both ports require needs no saying—the greater the prosperity of Hong Kong, the greater the prosperity of Canton, and vice versa; is a local axiom that becomes clearer every month. The intelligent classes in both ports realize that fully, and it remains only to impress it upon the minds of such pickets as are still active at Canton, or if that cannot be done to remove those pickets without further argument.

## [15] [From Our Own Correspondent.] Peking Nov. 8.

China's abrogation of the Sino-Belgian treaty is almost the sole topic of conversation in Peking to-day, though the last half-week has not been without other incidents of interest and importance.

The Koo Cabinet was undoubtedly in disfavour with most of the Press and public before the treaty was abrogated. The radicals felt that Dr. Wellington Koo had not been vehement enough about Wanhsien, and that the dragging on of Belgian negotiations would lead no place.

Then, without warning, came the abrogation of the treaty with Belgium, and at a stroke of the pen all Belgians and Belgian interests in China lost extraterritorial rights. The resultant chorus of approval for Dr. Wellington Koo, Foreign Minister and Acting Premier, and for the Regency Cabinet, has been tremendous. Young China feels that a beginning has been made toward ridding the nation of the "unequal treaties."

But there is another side to the picture. "The more sober-minded Chinese, and all of the Legation Quarter, feel that the Chinese Government (so-called by courtesy) has made a grave mistake. The trouble is that China is a signatory to treaties which put her under obligation to submit to the Hague Tribunal precisely such disputes as that which resulted in the abrogation of the Belgian pact. Naturally the Legation Quarter, and of course the Home Governments of all countries having Ministers here will interpret this step to mean that China has decided upon a course of treaty-breaking, and that, since the majority of the people approve of it, no agreement made with China will be held binding."

Another view widely held is that Belgium made a grave mistake in ever negotiating with the present government. The Tariff Conference will not be resumed until China has a responsible Government, and it is felt that Belgium made a mistake in negotiating with a Cabinet that has no real power outside the walls of Peking.

The Peking, Government to-day, in the eyes of most of the Powers, is nothing but the archives of China in which to deposit the proper series of notes and protests. The present Cabinet, in international standing, is of no more importance than a post box in which to drop letters for a certain distant destination—only in this case the destination seems distant in time instead of in miles.

## [16] CORRESPONDENCE. VOLUNTEERING.

[To the Editor of the “China Mail,”] Sir,—Your correspondent “Civis Brittanicus Sum” has raised a question which has been in the minds of many local residents for a long time, and I hope that the cap will fit many heads.

Whilst there are, undoubtedly, a number of "taipans" who will not go out of their way to make arrangements for their employees to attend camp, there are a number of cases where the men concerned do not take the trouble to ask for leave. To the latter I think it may be said that it is "up to them" to do their share and this can be done by making provisional arrangements, subject, of course, to the "taipan's" approval. For instance, when a man knows that it will be necessary for him to get a substitute during his absence, he will find matters much easier if he discusses the matter with his colleagues before hand, and then goes to his "taipan" not only with a request for leave, but also in a position to say that so and so will be glad to help in his absence.

## [20] CHINESE MUSIC.

**WESTERN AND EASTERN TEMPERAMENTS.**

Chinese music is certainly a fascinating subject at the present moment; and the human voice, for the love of which every artist helps to intensify, will always form a matter of human interest.

That Chinese music of the best quality, vocal or instrumental, is becoming popular among Chinese, may be seen by a visit to local ten-houses, which the majority of patrons day and night, frequent more for the music than for the refreshments offered.

In many homes gramophones are replacing regular instruments:

Chinese music is welcomed by merchants, students, workmen, shopkeepers, at marriage core-monies) entertainment and social gatherings.

At the "West Point restaurants, where European and American visitors are welcomed," Chinese music is also introduced, though some of it cannot be said to be creditable reproductions of the genuine thing.

According to the really accomplished Chinese musicians, the ordinary professional's do not represent the best type of musician.
Usually the ordinary Chinese will have no more respect for music than the ability of the musicians to render it. Skilled Chinese musicians, however, crave the art of employing sounds as a medium for artistic expression.

They aspire to impart through the medium of melodious sounds, all their inner feelings and emotions as is the aim of other arts like painting and architecture.

Many instrumental musicists have now the habit of playing to the accompaniment of singers of their own selection; usually these singers are blind girls from Canton.

These blind girls, well trained to love the art rather than simply to fall an engagement, differ from the restaurant singers in that their songs are soft and sweet and better still, very harmonious when heard to the accompaniment of the strains of true musicians.

An Important Question.

The careless players of so-called music so often appearing in public are really speaking, not musicians. Those players on Chinese stages and popular halls also fail to interpret the Chinese music of the highest order.

It is also obvious that Western and Eastern musicians differ in temperament and it is a little backward on the part of the Eastern musicians for that they have been sadly lacking in standard composition for the whole Chinese people.

Music in China is not universal; playing of one class of musicians does not coincide with that of another, though the clapping of hands and the stamping of feet to mark the rhyme and time are similar. Why should not standard music be composed and taught like other subjects in all Chinese schools is an important question.

This question is being taken up by some musicians in Hong Kong; and the matter now lies in the hands of the executive committee of the Chinese Amateur Musical Association. "In order to raise funds for this noble purpose this Association is going to give its third Grand Concert before the end of this year.

It might be recalled that this Association gave a Concert some years ago for the benefit of the Kwangtung Flood Fund. Some of these musicians have had many years' experience in the study of music as amateurs in Hong Kong, Canton, Swatow, Shanghai, and even in London and Chicago. It is certainly well worth while again to see and hear some of the members of this talented troops.—“Contributed.”
