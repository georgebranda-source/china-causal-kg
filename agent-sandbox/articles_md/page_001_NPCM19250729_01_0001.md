## [8] STRIKERS DEFEATED

**ABORTIVE RETALIATION FOR REDUCTIONS.**

**STILL MORE RETURNS.**

The firmness with which the returning strikers have been dealt, particularly those at the Kowloon Dock and Naval Yard, has resulted in a great deal of heart burning among those affected and it is known to the authorities that strikers' meetings have taken place with the object of retaliation of some sort but the lukewarmness among their own ranks is defeating their own ends and according to the police reports more workers have returned to the Whampoa Dock Co., the Green Island Cement Co.'s, Hokun works and Messrs. W. S. Bailey & Co.'s yard.

There is alleged to have been intimidation of Chinese who have been newly taken as tram drivers but apparently it has been without avail for the Hongkong (lower levels) Tramway Co. announce further extensions to their service. As from to-morrow, trams will run to Kennedy Town as in normal times and the Happy Valley trams will not turn back at the Post Office but proceed direct to Whitty Street.

Alleged to have in his possession seditious pamphlets, a Chinese was arrested at Wanchai yesterday. His excuse was that the papers had been handed to him by another man, but the latter, as expected, could not be found. He has been charged and will be dealt with in due course.

Fifty-eight of the 97 ocean-going vessels at present in the harbour are British, of the 6 departures yesterday 2 being British and of 11 arrivals 5 being British.

The Butterfield and Swire ship Nanchang, which has been lying idle at Swatow for some weeks, has been brought back to Hongkong by naval ratings from H.M.S. Bluebell and is at present anchored in Kowloon Bay.

The Chinese naval transport bont Kiangping has again passed through on its way from Shanghai to Pratas Shoal carrying a further 350 tons of building material for the wireless station that the Chinese Maritime Customs are erecting there.

## [9] "NO JUNE PAY."

**THE K. R. A. RESCINDS PREVIOUS RESOLUTION.**

In order that a united front may be presented to returning servants, the Kowloon Residents' Association has rescinded a previous resolution under which they recommended members to pay servants' wages up to the day on which they left service, and it has been decided that members now be recommended to pay no wages for June where servants left during that month. It was further recommended that rates of pay for returning servants should not be higher than those prevailing before the strike.

This was done by the Committee of the Association which held a meeting last night. It was not thought necessary to call a meeting of Kowloon residents to approve of the Committee's action having regard to the unanimous opinion of the very representative public meeting at the Theatre Royal.

## [10] H.K.V.D.C.

**FURTHER DEMOBILISATION OF MEN**

Over a hundred officers and men of the Hongkong Volunteer Defence Corps have already handed in their equipment at Headquarters and returned to civil life and dreams.

From to-night will take effect the further demobilisation of about fifty men from the Armoured Car section and the Field and Night sections of the Engineers, also numbering another fifty.

## [11] STRIKE LEVIES.

**POSITION IN KONGMOON DESCRIBED.**

**FOREIGNERS SAFE.**

An interesting letter from the “China Mall’s” Kongmoon (Pakkai) correspondent, which took several days to come down owing to a Post Office strike at that port, and written before steamers established communication between Macao and Kongmoon, reads:—

Thanks to Commander Li Yatongok preserving order, there has been no danger to foreign lives or property here. A notification by the Canton Government has been posted up on the Customs wall, warning agitators against interfering with the Customs foreign staff.

The situation is not very serious. There are tow-boats running here from Canton and Macao. Intimidators have tried to influence the Chinese employed by foreign bongs without success, but the position was once critical owing to the violence threatened. A foki of Messrs. Shiu Fat, compradores, was arrested by strikers for supplying bread to foreigners, but was released the next day. Prices of rice and flour have come down, but kerosene is very expensive, costing as much as $8 or $9. (Canton subsidiary silver coin) per t in! Tinned provisions are more expensive.

Motor boats for up-country ports have stopped for several days on account of a shortage of crude oil. The Sunning Railway runs only three trains a day, instead of four, because of a shortage of coal.

There have been few developments on the Chinese side with the exception, of course, of students treating crowds to street-corner speeches. Strikers are making levies from merchants for their cause. A large sum has been collected on the following schedule:—Ricci mills $50 each; bankers $80 each; large shops $25 each; small establishments $5 or $10 each. Most of the money has come from firms in Kongmoon or Sanwui city. An attempt to collect in Pakkai was nipped in the bud, the Chinese commanding officer pointing out that a false impression would be created and bad feeling would arise if permission were given in Pakkai where there is a number of foreign residents.

Pirates, however, have not been on strike but seem rather to be working overtime. Welcome appearances of H.M.S. Moorhen, and H.M. armed launches Kwong Lee and. Don Juan have had a salutary effect, and the Tung On, flying the naval ensign, is just as much welcomed.

Politically there is little to report except that Chan Wing-wai, magistrate of Sanwui, has been replaced by Au Ling-luong; a member of the Kuomintang.

## [12] MERCHANTS MEET.

**RICE EXPORT RESTRICTION DISCUSSED.**

The restriction on export of rice was again before the Trade Maintenance Bureau meeting yesterday, and reasons were advanced why it was desirable that it should be done away with. The rice, which merchants were having to hold, would feed the Chinese population for at least three months, as there were in all, some 200,000 bags. Business could still be transacted and these stocks not affected in any way.

It was decided that the Chinese members on the Legislative Council should be asked to go into the matter.

The bureau is now endeavouring to restore the steamship services between Hongkong and Canton.

The following typhoon warning has been received from the Manila Observatory by the American Consulate General, Hongkong: 3.24 p.m. July 28. Cyclone or typhoon, E. of Balintang Channel, moving N.N.W. or N.

## [14] $1,000 SQUARE

Chinese merchants in Hongkong who although they are opposed to the Bolshevik principles of administration of the Government of Canton have been forced by economic circumstances to do business with them are being very severely squeezed by them and, in fact, the temporary strike Governments in force at other ports.

A merchant informed a “China Mail” representative this morning that the Canton Government was forcing merchants to pay $1,000 for the privilege of discharging cargo and at Swatow the “squeeze” was considerably higher, averaging something like $2,500, and in some days, stated the “China Mail” informant, as much as $4,000 had to be paid.

The merchants were not doing business with Carlton from choice but to prevent themselves having to go into bankruptcy, large stocks of rice being at Bangkok, in connection with which they had incurred liability.

As regarded the food situation in Canton, there was no scarcity of rice, added, the merchant, although there was a market for it. The price had not soared up as had been stated, but was as a matter of fact a fraction lower than $8 a picul, (Hongkong money) less than pre-strike times. The period during which it had risen to $13 odd a peel (Hongkong money) was when the Hongkong dollar had gone down.

## [15] THEFT OF RECORDS

**EURASIM PLEADS CUY**

**A PLTP**

**CA TONTEUENON**

Through Mr. M. Girerdot, manager for Pathe Orient, pleading for leniency for an Eurasian who had admitted stealing gramophone records from him, a prosecution this morning was adjourned till to-morrow for Police inquiries.

George Clayson, pleaded guilty before Mr. R. A. D. Forrest to the theft of seven records from Messrs. Pathe Orient, at the firm's premises, No. 12, Queen's Road Central.

Defendant made no reply when His Worship asked why he had done such a thing. Sub-Inspector Richard Lanigan, prosecuting, informed His Worship that Clayson went into the shop and inspected a number of records when he suddenly dashed out with a number of them. Defendant was chased by the manager, intercepted and arrested.

Clayson claimed to have been born in Shanghai and that he had been here two years, having done “office work.” His employers were the Chung Wo Co., he said. Mr. Girardot submitted to His Worship that, in view of the little value attached to the records and seeing that defendant had been taught a lesson, the latter be dealt with leniently.

His Worship stated, however, that he preferred to put back the case till tomorrow to enable the Police to make enquiries of the defendant's employers.

## [18] OUR GLASS TRADE.

**NO INTERFERENCE WITH SUPPLIES.**

*(Heuter's Service.)*

*London, July 28.*

In the House of Commons, replying to Mr. Robert Morrison, Sir Philip Canlife Lister said that there was no reason to think that the relevant agreement between the Harriman combine and the Soviet Government, would interferes with supplies of manganeose and paroxide to British Glass-makers. He pointed out that satisfactory supplies of manganeose and paroxide were obtainable from other sources—for example India, also that other substances were available for use as glass decolourisers.

## [24] BRITAIN'S COAL

**INDUSTRY'S PROBLEMS REVIEWED.**

**CAUSES OF THE CRISIS.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

The court of inquiry investigating the coal mining dispute has reported that the workers are justified in claiming the incorporation of the minimum wage provision of any wages agreement. The court concurs with the view that wages at the agreed minimum rate should be a charge before the profits. It opines that there is considerable room for improving the coal industry's efficiency as a whole and thereby afford aid to its economic position. Improvement in the distribution of coal might lower the price and stimulate the demand.

The commission says that the present crisis has been to a large extent created by neither of the parties to the dispute, but is the inevitable sequel of the economic upheaval due to the war. The commission glides over the question of the royalty system, the decline in the demand attributed to the falling off of foreign sales, oil-burning ships, the growth of hydro-electric power and the great increase in coal production in countries formerly our best customers. Foreign competition is also noted.

International's Decision.

Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Holland, and Czechoslovakia were represented at a private meeting of the Executive Committee of the Miners' International, presided over by Mr. Herbert Smith, which examined the international position in relation to the coal crisis. They passed a resolution calling on Trade Unions to strive to achieve the nationalisation of coal mines; and international control of production. They promised international action in the event of mines' stoppage in Britain on August 1. If a stoppage does not eventuate in the immediate future, the committee demanded that production be limited in other countries so that Britain does not suffer.
