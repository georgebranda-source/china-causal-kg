## [1] WHOLE PROVINCE BEING INVOLVED.

**INCREASED FIRING**

### Decisive

Increased firing which is thought to presage a big battle in a day or two is the principal feature of latest reports from Canton.

Once more the interruption to telegraphic communication is hindering the gathering of news. However, if there had been any sonsational development, the Naval authorities would have certainly been informed by wireless. Our Canton correspondent writes up to the hour of the mail closing yesterday afternoon. The Senior Officer of the West River reported also yesterday afternoon, by wireless, that there had been little change but that snipping was on the increase.

## [4] Shots at Ships.

<sub>section under [2] NEW STOCK OF</sub>

Canton night boats returning to Hongkong this morning report having been fired on from the shore early yesterday morning in the neighbourhood of the village of Chung Chow, Collinson Reach, (not, of course, to be confused with Cheung Chau near Hongkong).

The shots were rifle shots and one report attributes them to bands of outlaws (still wearing the soldiers' uniform of the army from which they have deserted for their own purposes) who were noticed at the spot the previous day. The locality in question is Danés Island, part of the larger Whampou group. Collinson Reach is not usually used by the boats but this has been necessary since the outbreak of hostilities because of the position the gun-boats have taken up at Canton.
The Ko Chow (Captain Eb. Jones) was the first boat to pass the spot. She was keeping close to the shore and the shots were fired from almost point-blank range, loud demands being made at the same time for the ship to stop. Captain Jones, however, ordered full steam ahead and got clear of land without apparently any harm being done to passengers or ship. A Chinese patrol launch which at that moment came down the river proceeded to the spot where the firing had come from and fired four shots from its guns.

Shameen's Safety.

*(From Our Own Correspondent.)*

To-day the river steamers are not going alongside their wharves as these have been declared to be in the war zone. They are at anchor in the stream off Shameen.

All the foreign gunboats—three British, two American, two French and one Japanese—are standing by.

Allies of the Cantonese Communists are coming down the West River. Their artillery has been finding its mark and shots have passed over Shameen which is still immune from immediate danger.

## [10] Shanghai; June 9.

<sub>section under [9] STOP: PRESS.</sub>

Three hundred Chinese seamen on six Butterfield & Swire coastal and river steamers at Shanghai went on strike yesterday evening. Ocean-going steamers are not affected.

Japanese lines are also meeting difficulties and are considering replacing the Chinese crews with Japanese.

Jardine, Matheson & Co. report unrest but no actual crew strike. A Jerdine coaster sailed yesterday night.

The strike of the dockers has interrupted dock work. Very little cargo is moving because the Chinese banks and shipping hongs which finance and handle cargo are closed down.

## [12] A Bishop's Views.

<sub>section under [11] 3,000 MORE.</sub>

The Bishop of St. Albans, speaking at the Diocesan conference, said they must not lay all the blame for the disturbances in China on Bolshevism. They are largely due to the appalling conditions prevailing in industries in China and the fact that many Europeans have sought to make money from China without considering the native.

The conference passed a resolution declaring that greater sacrifices must be made by the Church at Home in order to further foreign missionary effort.

The question of the Shanghai shooting incidents was raised at a meeting of the workers' delegates of the International Labour Conference in a statement signed by Mr. Joshi (India); Mr. Lawson (Australia) and Mr. Suzuki (Japan) submitted to the Chairman.

The latter declared the question could not be raised in conference under standing orders, whereupon the meeting resolved to despatch through the Amsterdam International a message of sympathy to the workers of China.

## [13] Strikers' Demands.

<sub>section under [11] 3,000 MORE.</sub>

Various Chinese Commercial and Student Unions met in the Chinese Chamber of Commerce last evening. They passed seventeen resolutions, which were handed to the Chinese delegates. They include four demands, which the strikers consider essential as preliminaries to official negotiations.

They are:—(1) The cancellation of a state of emergency; (2) the withdrawal of the marines; (3) the release of the arrested rioters; and (4) the restoration of closed schools.

Approached this morning and asked how the system was working, Dr. Addison said that it was proving of the greatest possible use. At present nothing but the report of the nature of the disease and the number of deaths (or the recording of a clean bill of health is the case might be) was sent, but it was hoped that when the system was more firmly established, ed reports would be made of the nature of the steps taken to combat the disease and the measure of success which had attended them.

The number of ports making use of the Bureau was continually growing and his weekly cable from Singapore now included statistics from ports in Australia, said Dr. Addison. He himself sent off a cable to Singapore once a week telling them what was happening, and this was despatched by Singapore to all the ports who were partier to the scheme.

The advantages of the Bureau were that steps could be taken to deal with ships from infected ports arriving in Hongkong very much more quickly and with consequently more favourable results than when the news merely "filtered through" as Dr. Addison put it. He was confident that the result of the new system would be that the movement of infectious disease would be much more effectively restricted and when the system expanded and it was made possible to follow the result of the putting into practice of latest scientific discoveries he was confident that this would constitute an even greater step forward.

The Bureau was being made use of, said the P.C.M.O., by ports in Japan, Shanghai, French Indo-China, Siam, the Philippines, Dutch East Indies, Borneo, Australia, Singapore, Penang and British India, in fact practically over India.

It is interesting to recall that this matter of the incidence of epidemic diseases formed the subject of debate at the Medical Conference recently held at Hongkong and Dr. Lim, one of the first students to graduate from the Hongkong University, who has since interested himself in matters of health primarily in relation to Shanghai, had some interesting comments to make.

Dr. Lim pointed out that in these days of rapid communication and means of communication the control of infectious diseases, instead of being a matter of purely local significance, had become a subject of international concern. Improved means of communication had resulted in bringing cities and nations into closer touch, but this had not been without its attendant evils, as it had introduced diseases into territories where they had been non-existent. The fight would have to be waged on a worldwide basis if there was to be success, said Dr. Lim.

Not the least important of the measures which, in Mrs Austen Chamberlain's words will make Hongkong still further one of the "plans" of the Colonial Service is the recently established health Bureau, the working of which was described to a "China Mail" representative this morning by the B.C.M.O.

## [15] Marines Landed.

<sub>section under [14] HEALTH FIRST.</sub>

Two hundred marines from H.I.J.M.S. Tatsuta landed this morning.

Two Japanese gun-boats from the Yangtze have left for Shanghai. Four Japanese destroyers have arrived, two of which have gone up river to protect foreigners at Yangtze ports.

The student demonstration at Foochow was a fiasco, owing to the Tupan's precautionary measures.

Chinkiang, Tsingtao, Kiukiang and Hankow are now quiet. While there have been no disturbances at Shanghai, the strike situation is unchanged. Defence measures are continuing. Further arrivals of naval men have enabled the authorities to lighten the duties of the volunteers.

Diplomatic Investigation.

Reports from Nanking indicate that the situation there is becoming more serious.

A telegram from Taiyuanfustates that rioter's wrecked the local agency of the Asiatic Petroleum Company, smashing windows and tearing down the sign. It is understood that Chang Tso-lin contemplates issuing a circular telegram advising the students to refrain from acts of violence pending diplomatic investigation.

Members of the diplomatic delegation left for Shanghai this morning by the "blue express." It is stated that they will not sit as a commission nor take formal evidence, but make independent investigations returning within a few days with their report.

The directors of the local Chinese Bankers' Association this morning decided: Firstly, to telegraph to the Chinese banks at Shanghai urging them to support the students; secondly, to telegraph to public bodies urging them to pursue the present agitation to the end; thirdly, to furnish $10,000 strike relief funds; fourthly, to advise native banks to refrain from co-operating financially with the foreign banks concerned.

The students here have gone out on a sympathetic strike. There is growing intensity against foreigners and the situation is most serious.
