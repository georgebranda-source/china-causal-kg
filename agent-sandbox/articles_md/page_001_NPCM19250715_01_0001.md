## [8] COMING BACK.

**STRIKERS REALISING FOOLISHNESS OF ACTION.**

**A LONG WALK.**

Daily there are to be observed fresh signs of a more general realisation on the part of those who left work of the foolishness of their action and their desire to make amends by attempting to return to their former occupations. In the case of those who are near enough at hand to put their desire into practice they are finding, however, that their reinstatement at former wages is by no means an accepted thing; in fact, that in many cases they will have to begin all over again and that their action has discounted many years' service.

Those at Canton find it even more difficult to return to the service which they so unthinkingly left, but such is their plight and their desire to obtain work that many have chosen a walk which in this weather must be far from pleasurable. The "Chim Mail" bus it from an authentic source that 2,000 ex-strikers from Hongkong, endeavoured to return to the Colony yesterday by turn and were turned back on the borders. Not to be defeated in their object and under the pretext of returning, large numbers of them went back to "intermediate stations" and by a circuitous route eventually crossed the border.

It was probably one of these who made his way to the office of Dr. S. W. T'so, Assistant Controller of Labour, yesterday. Dr. T'so passed no opinion on the matter himself, but he certainly told a "China Mail" representative this morning that of the many coming forward and asking for work had been one man who was in great need of food and who had to pawn his trousers to get across the harbour. However, he must have had another pair, for Dr. T'so assured the reporter that he was respectably injured when he made his application.

Dr. T'so' felt convinced that the tide had well turned as regarded the return of general labour, and said that it had been noticeable ever since the issue of the proclamation dealing with idlers. Deck hands were among those now coming forward and asking for work.

Taken in conjunction with the fact that the police have seamen enough to man ships at present in the hubour, this might be taken to mean that there is nothing to prevent the ships here from leaving at once and for a complete return to be made to economic normality.

This, unfortunately, is far from being the case. The strikers in Canton may be anxious to return to employment; strikers who remained may have been frightened even more of being deported than of being intimidated, thus expressing a willingness to return to work; but the fact must be faced that trade here has been dislocated considerably.

Even crews to man the ships won't be much good unless there is the cargo to take" said a shipping man to a "China Mail" representative this morning. He added that as regards mercantile business there is "simply nothing doing" and boevcotting at Amoy, Foochow and other ports would still further affect the position. Earlier activity had, he said, been connected almost entirely with transhipped cargo.

## [9] NO INTIMIDATION.

**PARTNERS MERELY FALL OUT.**

At the Kowloon Magistracy this morning, a partner of the Mow Ling restaurant, of 450 Shanghai Street, was charged with alleged intimidation of a fellow-partner.
At the previous hearing of the case it was stated by the complainant that the accused threatened to destroy the shop if he (complainant) refused to join the strike.

The defence put forward by Mr. Elsley Zeitlyn (instructed by Messrs. d'Almada and Nephew) was that there had been a dispute between the two partners in connection with the purchase of stores. The accused had no desire to close the shop, as he was afraid of losing his money.
His Worship held that there had been a partnership disputed and discharged the accused.

The Government, in a crisis like the present one, can draw upon very extensive powers, usually held in reserve, and it is satisfactory to know that it will not hesitate to use them, if necessary; for, however true it may be to assert that the unrest in the first place had an industrial origin—and even of that, we have our doubts—it has long passed from that stage. The movement throughout China today is openly political and anti-foreign, more particularly anti-British, and in Shanghai and Hongkong the issue has been definitely joined between the forces of anarchy and those of law and order. In Hongkong, especially, there can be no shadow of an excuse for the outbreak, for as regards labour conditions and the social welfare of the working classes generally, that Colony has always been a model for China and only a day or two ago we were able to record the latest successful advance in legislation designed to protect the labourer's interests. In such circumstances there can be no thought of compromise. The worker, who downs tools because he has a grievance, genuine or imaginary, in connection with his work may be ill-advised, but, provided he breaks no contract, he is well within his natural rights. The strike is recognised as a legitimate weapon against excessive hours of labour, unhealthy or oppressive conditions, and harsh employers. ## [17] ICE SUPPLIES:

Only a few weeks ago and the fetching of the daily supply of ice was looked upon as the work merely of the household coolla. The Number One and even the house boy would look askinca if asked to include this journey in his daily round and common task, and his reply would probably be in the nature of a protest that that was "coolie, pidgeon." Then, the drawing up of even a ricsha at the door of the little store in Pottinger Street must have been somewhat of an event, but now its very important function has been forced to the notice of all and sundry. During hours it is favoured with an almost constant stream of cars, motor cycles and other conveyances. One day, for instance, His Excellency's car was noted side by side with a seemingly diminutive Austin Seven!

With the perversity which characterises human mentality and actions, we seldom fully appreciate a thing until it has to be obtained with difficulty and this certainly applies in the case of the household ice, which we accepted without comment so long as we were not required to participate in its conveyance. Now, however, many of us have had to think a little about it with the result that ice is being realised for what it really is, a really wonderful and indispensable thing. How is it made? we query and even the assurance that it is just water with the air forced from it and encircled whilst in tin receptacles with pipes containing ammonia and brine does not destroy our conviction that the process which enables it to be sold so cheaply in a climate like Hongkong's must be rather remarkable. It is at a time like the present, too, that one realizes the truth of the comment made on the occasion of the opening of the new ice plant of the Dairy Farm at East Point, viz., that the occasion marked an important point in the development of the Company. Before the installation of the machinery there, Hongkong was to a very large extent dependent on Canton for its ice supply but now, with eighty tons being turned out every 24 hours, our independence of foreign sources is almost complete. When the full capacity of the East Point Works and the additions in view are brought to bear on the problem, we shall be able to say in all confidence that the Colony's requirements in this respect are fully and adequately taken care of for a number of years to come.

## [24] PLEA FOR UNITY.

**FRENCH AMBASSADOR'S SPEECH.**

**DANGER TO FOREIGNERS.**

### (Heuter's Service). London. July

Ambassador Fleuriau, receiving the French Colony in London on the occasion of the National Fete, referring to the situation in China, said the existing system of treaties could not be touched without exposing foreigners in China to the greatest dangers. Agitators who excited good Chinese against foreigners would not engage in deadly work unless they were encouraged from outside.

He hoped all Governments concerned would unite to defend their nationals, not merely now by concerted measures for immediate protection, but in future by retaining the guarantee of old treaties until China was able herself completely to assure the safety of lives and property of all residents in China.

M. Fleuriau concluded by saying that world peace depended on the Franco-British Entente.
