## [10] HOME BOXING.

**LATEST NEWS.**

London, May 1.

Elky Clark knocked out Young Johnny Brown in the last round of a 20-round main event at the Albert Hall last night, after a thrilling contest at terrific pace.

Clark is the flyweight champion of Britain but the title was not involved as the midgets fought at 2 lbs. over the limit.

[There is well-founded gossip that the winner may try conclusions for world honours with Pancho Villa who recently gave an exhibition at the "Star," Kowloon, while on his way to the U.S.A.]

Johnny Brown, elder brother of the flyweight and the bantam-champion of Britain, won from Andre Routis (118 lbs. champion of France) in a 15-round bout at 121 lbs., the Frenchman being disqualified for hitting low when well ahead on points in the fifth round.

Bombardier Billy Wells, the ex-champion heavyweight, staged another come-back and was knocked out by Jack Stanley (Deptford) in the 3rd round after going down for a count of eight.

## [11] BALLOON RACE.

**MORE EXCITING INCIDENTS.**

**. CHANNEL DESCENT.**

*(Reuter's Service.)*

*London, June 9.*

*SHANGHAI, Juno 9.*

The first trial was resumed in the Mixed Court to-day of seventeen rioters, twenty-nine forfeiting their bail. Lengthy evidence was taken all day long.

Mr. Harry Westridge, a China Inland Missionary, gave evidence that he witnessed occurrences on the afternoon of May 30, which he recounted in minute detail.

Asked whether he considered the shooting necessary, he replied that as a missionary he hated shooting, but if he had been the officer of the law responsible for the station which was attacked he would. There was no option. He believed there was no evidence to show that the students had any personal feeling against the police and vice versa.

*SHANGHAI, June 9.*

Apart from the shipping strike, Shanghai continues to settle down. The bakers resumed work this morning. Some shops have reopened, and domestics are returning to work.

No riots have occurred in the river-ports but much unrest is reported. Women and children refugees from Kiukiang will arrive in Shanghai to-morrow. ## [21] BRIGHTER BANKS

**ARMCHAIRS AND BOOKS FOR CLIENTS.**

Banks are to be made brighter. Ordinary people usually leave banking premises as quickly as they can. They are oppressed by the melancholy opulence. Now this is to be changed, and the Midland Bank, Limited, will go down to banking history as pioneers.

To make their clients feel thoroughly at home they have opened a reading and writing-room and a ladies' rest-room at their handsome new branch in Piccadilly.

The step is one which will be welcomed by business men. In the beautifully panelled reading and writing room, furnished in the early Georgian style, customers can transact business, write letters, or pass a quiet hour in reading.

"Ample facilities are available in the shape of literature," the bank announces. At one end "of the room is a counter where officials will attend to the requirements of clients.

The ladies' room is decorated in green and gold, the walls are of plaster panelling, while the general design; "electrolier and furniture are all early Georgian.

This “reform” bank stands on the site of the old Vestry Hall of St. James, and is so built that it makes an admirable foil to Wren's church, which the master builder himself considered one of his best contrived churches. A notable feature of the bank is a magnificent Georgian ceiling designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens.

To mark the opening the Midland Bank, Limited, have published a quaintly-bound and illustrated brochure, Landmarks in Piccadilly, for which type purchased in 1672 by Bishop Fell for the Oxford University Press has been used.

## [22] ALL RED.

The Chiefs of Gambia expressed themselves when they closed their address to the Prince of Wales with the words, "We are black. We cannot speak or write English, but we are English." I am reminded (says an "Evening Standard" writer) of a story told me by a friend who happened to be in the East at the time of Queen Victoria's death. Going to the memorial service on that occasion he met a Chinese acquaintance, of whom he asked why he was there.

The yellow-skinned British subject replied, "You English—me English. Both alike—All red inside."

## [24] A "NATIONAL JOKE"

**DEFENCE OF THE ALBERT MEMORIAL.**

For some time I have felt rather sorry for the Albert Memorial, writes Kathleen O'Brien in the Daily Chronicle. From being a National Monument it has somehow passed into a National Joke. Now I find it quite reasonable to joke about a solemn thing that everyone else takes seriously. But to joke about a solemn thing that everyone else jokes about, too... no, that is sheer bullying. I am suddenly and perversely moved to pity. I find I must instantly take its part. It was in this spirit of compassion that I went and had a close look at the Albert Memorial.

## [25] A DETAILED STUDY.

You know, there are things about the Albert Memorial, if you honestly look for them.
For one thing, to see it properly you stand with your back to the Albert Hall, so that the enormous posters announcing contiguously a performance of the "Dream of Gerontius" and a puglistic bout between Bert Hammerjaw and Kid Basheye are temporarily powerless to inflict their yellow pain. Wherefore the site of the Memorial at least must command our respect.
Then let us look (as the guidebooks say) at the figure groups seated at the four outer corners of the base.

Who is this dauntless woman seated on a ferocious bull, with no more sign of nervousness than if she were riding down the Strand on a bus? Ha! Europa, of course. The Big Four are seated at her feet—no, not that Big Four; these are, obviously, Britannia with her trident, Italia with her lyre and palette, France and Germany—er—by the way which is France, and which Germany? One has a book and the other a sword, but that is little to go by. Neither can you go by their faces, for in feature and expression the might be twin sisters, which I am nearly sure is incorrect. However, let us not be fussy fussy about details.

## [26] THE INTREPID LADY

Next, America. America, intrepid lady, rides on a bison, with its head down; as if about to butt something. Another intrepid lady, one of America's daughters, is hitting the bison on the head with a nasty-looking stick. "I'll learn you to butt my mother!" she is saying. A man at her side has set down rather suddenly on an astonished lion, which he has completely flattened out. Its claws dangle helplessly in the air. Another standing lady, with a lamb's fleece over her shoulder, offers a bunch of, I think, rhubarb, to an indignant man in a hat and nearly nothing else, who is rightly refusing it. He is holding a rifle in readiness in case his wife offers him rhubarb any more.

Africa comes next. Egypt sensibly rides a camel, which, though possibly old-fashioned, is a far more reliable-steed than these new-fangled bulls and bisons. Near her is a wise man with a note-book in his hand, a kind of hassocky thing—or is it a bale of merchandise? And is his book the Precepts of Ptah-Hatep or a cash account book? Beside the camel a rather "school-marry" person shakes her, finger at a negro who is making faces at her behind Egypt's back. Don't do that, you naughty, rude boy!" she says severely, "after all the pains I've had educating you, too!"

And last, Asia. An elephant nearly as large as the lady who is seated on him has rather ignobly collapsed. You see it is not only the weight of his rider that oppresses him. There is a conquering young man with a far-away expression leaning against the elephant's ribs and sticking the point of his shield into the elephant's side, regardless of the pain he must be inflicting on that dumb, faithful creature. Round the other side a visionary is seated rupt in dreamy contemplation, with his foot very near the elephant's tail. I consider it a great tribute to the remarkable powers of concentration possessed by the visionary that he can retain that serene, placid expression with a justly irritated elephant well within kicking distance. I couldn't.

## [27] IN CHINESE EYES.

**GENERAL HSU ON LONDON**

In a signed article in the Daily Express General S. T. Hsu, head of the Chinese Mission, now in London to study our economic and political institutions, expresses his admiration of the ladies of London and of the limbs of the law who control the street traffic. The former he describes as "like lotus petals swaying in the wind," and the latter as "marvellous fellows."

London, he says, is to me such an incomprehensible multitude of people that I am overwhelmed by her enormity. I have been in your mighty capital for so little time that my impressions will not yet form themselves. But in my journeyings through her wide, open streets I have cast an eye on her beautiful ladies, her smart guardians of her law, her traffic extraordinary, and some of her marvellous buildings, and these have pleased me. Not yet have I had the honour of pausing in front of the Cenotaph, but I hope soon to be accorded the privilege of placing a wreath of flowers on the tomb of your illustrious dead.

I consider the ladies of London are elegantly attired, of most graceful carriage, and that their demeanour is quiet and modest. As they walk they look like lotus petals swaying in the wind. In China we say that the beauty of a pure and good woman shines like the beauty of a pearl, and that the grace of her carriage and her movements is heavenment. I think of this when I see the wives and daughters and sisters of London citizens, so beautiful, so modest, so full of grace.

Unhappily, it has not been my inestimable joy to meet and hold discourse with these fair daughters of the West, but from my observations I can see that many of them are also highly accomplished and of a great knowledge. It is said that they are as fortunate in the world of commerce as in the smaller world of the household to which in ancient times they were dedicated. Here they are different from my women, who still give an undivided attention to the homes of their husbands.

It is my invariable custom to study the relation of women to society, nor I consider that they exercise a wide influence on politics. These eminent, highly-born men who are so fortunate as to have cultured wives who are intellectual companions and intelligent help-meets to them and good mothers to their children are blessed indeed.

## [28] WAKEFULNESS

For your policeman I have nothing but praise. He is a marvellous fellow, for he does nothing at all! And yet I know that it is he who has made the London traffic the orderly, docile organism it is. He only seems to do nothing. Actually it is that white-gloved hand of his that has accomplished everything. I can appreciate perfectly the difficulty of regulating such a big and tremendous traffic. It is a pity and a shame to say so, but during the last four or five years since I have left the city the traffic control in Peking has become worse than before.

Your streets impress me with their cleanliness London is said to be a city of fog and grime. Yet everywhere I see bright, shining cleanliness, and—at this time—marvellous splashes of green. The parks, the quiet, dignified little squares, and the trees in the yards of the ancient churches all make London green and beautiful to me.

I went in a motor-car to Aldershot, where I was entertained to luncheon by Lady Chetwode, the honourable wife of the general commanding your troops in that town. Her home was a pattern of gentle grace and kindly dignity, and she a pattern of hospitality.

As we moved along in the motor-car, through your country roads, I was profoundly moved by that quality of the English spring of which our travellers have so often spoken. It was like the gentle waking-up of the earth child after the slumber of the long, dark winter months.

All about me in your mighty capital I can feel the tradition of centuries, but it possesses also that same spirit of wakefulness which I observed in the country, and which must make it for ever completely different from the slumberous age-old cities of the Orient.
