OUTLOOK GRAVER.

## Peking, June 20

### (Reuter's Service.)

The situation is unchanged. The removal of negotiations from Shanghai to Peking has not caused any outward excitement.

It is reported that the Japanese have handed in a Note of protest as regards the affair at Kiukiang; on June 18, but both the Chinese and Japanese authorities are maintaining complete secrecy.

The Union of Labour, Commerce and Education, which came into being in consequence, of the events of May 30, have decided to insist on the continuation of the strike of shops, despite the decision reached at the meeting of the Chinese Chamber of Commerce, which decided that the banks should re-open on Sunday.

## Terror Gang

The students yesterday terrorised the shopkeepers, bankers, and members of the Chamber of Commerce in connection with the decision to re-open the banks and shops to-day.

Two special meetings were held in the Chamber yesterday, at which the extremists contended that foreigners would view the re-opening as a sign of weakening. The business members pointed out that the closing of the banks and shops was not materially affecting the foreigners but was causing tremendous Chinese losses, instancing that enormous supplies of tea and cocoons were piling up in Shanghai, which were at present unfinanceable and not movable.

Yesterday morning the meeting resolved to postpone the opening until June 24. Last night's wholly rescinded the resolution of June 19, and resolved to postpone settlement day until the end of the lunar month, namely July 21.

## Chairman Attacked

During the discussions, the extremists bitterly attacked Yu Ya-ching, the Chairman of the Chamber of Commerce, charging him with having received $500,000 from the Imperialists to re-open the banks and shops.

Yu Ya-ching vehemently denied this. He hurried to Shanghai from Peking, and since worked day and night in the interests of China and her people. He had not influenced the voting, and he had sufficient money for his few remaining years on this earth.

The attack on Yu Ya-ching silenced other solid business men, and the rescission was carried by a huge majority.

While a definite split between the business members, and the extremists has not occurred, each group is attempting to justify itself, and the outcome will depend largely on the attitude of the Chinese Government.

Delegates, representing 48 colleges, met at the Law College this morning. They sent a deputation to interview the Foreign Minister and War Minister, this afternoon, demanding severance of diplomatic relations with Britain and the impeachment of the Hankow Tupan. The situation, however, at least outwardly, is becoming quiet. There are no signs of the boycott or strike. Numerous students have left for their homes during the past week.

## More Bluster

A message from Chungking yesterday, states that after student agitators worked up popular excitement, rioters on the 17th. attacked foreign customs, employés and assaulted Britons on the streets, though none were seriously hurt. Meanwhile a demonstration occurred at the British Consulate, where the rioters-threatened-the-consular Chinese stuff. The Consul, thereupon, requested the city commandant to furnish a consular guard and restore order. As there was no response, the Consul evacuated the Consulate and went aboard a gunboat.

## Chungking Trouble

He sent messages to all Britons, advising them quickly to assemble on the gunboat. The community, on June 18, found accommodation at the riverside, under the protection of gunboats where they are remaining. Order has

been restored in the city, but excitement has not subsided. It is unsafe for foreigners to appear in the streets, though no danger is apprehended under present circumstances. The British Consulate is damaged to an extent unknown, and troops are now guarding it. At Chungking, as at many other centres the rivalries of the militarists have proved an important factor in the situation. Leaders, who are inclined to restore order feared popular ill-will and held aloof at a critical period.

The Foreign Office Note, in reply to the Powers' note regarding the riots against foreign sentiment in China, states that the accounts of different incidents given therein are not in accord with the local Chinese authorities' reports.

The Note gives a lengthy Chinese version of the recent occurrences. It says the Hankow British Volunteers suddenly opened fire on an entirely unarmed crowd and the British authorities should assume full responsibility for their violent action.

At Kiukiang, during an altercation between the police and a small number of workmen, fire suddenly broke out at the Taiwan Bank. During the confusion at the time of putting out the fire slight damage was "caused" to sundry articles in the British and Japanese Consulates. Such damage was accidental. Regarding the killing of a British subject at Shanghai, the Note says that he was outside the Settlement, where the Municipality has built roads without China's consent. The motives of the criminals have not been ascertained. It is regrettable for the above reasons that the incident occurred.

### Peking "Stands Firm."

The Note goes on to say that with the exception of the killing of a British subject at Shanghai all the incidents happened as a result of failure to obtain a prompt and fair settlement of the Shanghai case.

There never existed any anti-foreign tendency.

Since the Shanghai occurrence, the Government has issued mandates ordering the people to exercise self-restraint, and has instructed the provincial authorities to maintain order. In view of the present circumstances, the Government hope the Ministers of the Powers will promptly settle the Shanghai case, then the present indignation will be appeased and- excitement subside. The Note, in conclusion, declares "I declare the stand I have taken in the previous protests will be insisted upon."

## The Chinese View

But, at the subsequent two meetings, the commission evinced a desire to shift onto the Chinese authorities, the responsibility for indirect causes which led up to the shooting. The commission further stated that not having authority to deal with questions of an international nature it must leave immediately for Peking. The conference was thus brought to an end: The Chinese delegation much regrets that its sincere efforts hitherto have failed to bring about a satisfactory solution.

Responding to the diplomatic commission's statement, the Chinese delegation have issued a statement in which they express the belief that a permanent basis of settlement could only be achieved by solving certain questions which have been sources of misunderstanding between the Chinese and foreigners in Shanghai, including the rendition of the Mixed Court, the amelioration of conditions of labourers and the participation of Chinese ratepayers in the Municipal Council.

The statement is signed by Messrs. Tsai Ting-kan, Cheng Chien and Tseng Tsung-chien.

## Need For Prayer

At the annual meeting of the conference of British Missionary Societies, a resolution was passed recording the conviction that an urgent need exists for prayerful study of various movements underlying the present situation in China. The Conference considers that the true solution can only be found by mutual confidence and conciliation resulting from a patient and sympathetic investigation of the difficulties with which China at present is confronted as a nation.

## From Afar: EDINBURGH, June 20

Over a thousand delegates, representing sixty countries, attended the opening of the World Women's Christian Temperance Convention. The Conference opened with a devotional session, at which delegates from South Africa, Australia, Denmark Canada, India and China gave short addresses.

Miss Tinling from Shanghai said that in the present trouble the Chinese should not be blamed for the effects of Bolshevik influence. The rest of the world was threatened with the same changes. The Chinese were only doing what mobs do elsewhere.

## Shanghai Position

## SHANGHAI, June 20

Shanghai is quiet, but there is no cessation of anti-foreigo propaganda. The decision to reopen the banks and shops is favourably affecting the general situation; but merely reflects the necessity of the Chinese to end the phase of the situation causing them much severer losses and inconvenience than foreigners.

The shipping strike, which is a strong anti-foreign weapon, is not weakening. Strikers are beginning to kidnap Japanese employees who are taken to Chapei, robbed and beaten and only released on payment of money and promises to quit employment.

A wireless from Chinkiang says the strikers have resolved that coal will not be sold to British or Japanese.

At Swatow anti-foreign demonstrations continue.

No danger is now apprehended t Anking.

At Foochow an anti-Japanese boycott has been decided upon but no date has been fixed.

Ample Strike Funds.

The extremists are not concerned in the deadlock, for sufficient funds for strike purposes continue to flow in from China and abroad.

The strikers' programme includes a huge demonstration in the native city to-morrow, which together with the growing activities of organised bands of ruffians, is giving rise to additional precautions. Chang Hsue Liang is bringing a second brigade to Shanghai of Fengtien soldiers and is taking up strategic positions in the vicinity of the native city.

The vernacular papers all published Mr. Chamberlain's speech, some commenting on it as nonsense, and others treating it impartially.

The position here is improving. Foreigners are remaining across the river. The other Yangtze ports, and Peking and Tientsin are quiet. The Chinese Delegation has been ordered to Peking.

## Chungking Situation

Message From The Pope.

The University professors recently solicited the Pope's support in the Shanghai affair. The Pope empowered Mon-signor Constantini, the Apostolic Delegate to China, to signify his sympathy with the great Chinese people and keenly deploring the shedding of fraternal blood and praying that concord and order would soon be restored in a spirit of mutual understanding, according to the principles of justice, equity, and Christian charity.

## Fengtien Troops Mutiny

The 3rd Fengtien Regiment mutinied at Chensin, on the Peking Mukden line, damaging the station. The gendarmerie commandant of a neighbouring town has sent a detachment to round up the multi-neers.

A Briton, Mr. Moalem, a driver of the Volunteer Machine Gun Co., was going home in uniform last night by way of Lord Lane when he was struck between the shoulders and the neck with a meatchopper. He was seriously wounded and taken to Hospital.

The strikers yesterday afternoon attacked the wharf coolies coaling the Blue Funnel liner Pootung. Messrs. Butterfield and Swire's assistant compradore, intervening, aroused the ire of the strikers who set upon him and chased him to the river-side, where failing to reach a sampah, he fell in and was drowned.

[A message received later in correction of the foregoing states that it was the younger brother of the Holt's Wharf compradore who was killed, not Messrs. Butterfield & Swire's compradore]

### MRS. LANGTRY

THE DUKE AND THE WATER-LILIES.

"In The Days I Knew" by Lady de Bathe or Mrs. Langtry (to give her the name by which the London of her glorious youth knew her so well) provides here a wealth of good stories and interesting recollections of the great figures in the social world.

Hers is a most entertaining book. Almost from the hour of her first appearance in England as a young bride she became the vogue and was photographed, painted, and worshipped for her extraordinary beauty. The eighth Duke, of Devonshire, who was not usually susceptible on my admiring the lovely coloured water-lilies reposing in marble pools, drenched his clothes pulling them out as an offering, as also the gorgeous liveries of the footmen into whose arms he flung them, and who strewed our brougham with such quantities of the dripping blossoms as to make the latter conveyance rather molster than was convenient.

Simplicity was the note of Mrs. Langtry's life in her first season: Through all this procession of opera, dinners, and balls I wore, extraordinary as it may sound to members of my sex, my one black evening gown, the creation of Mme. Nicole, the fashionable dress-maker of St. Heliers, Jersey.

## MET THE PRINCE

The black frock became the rage, and so with everything she wore:

One morning I twisted a piece of black velvet into a toque, stuck a quill through it, and went to Sandown Park. A few days later this turban appeared in every milliner's window labelled "The langtry Hat." It was very embarrassing and it had all come about so suddenly that I was bewildered.

At her first meeting with King Edward, then Prince of Wales, she says:

I was immensely interested in watching the Prince, and soon realised that, while good-natured and pleasant to everyone, he preserved his dignity admirably; in fact I decided that he would have been a brave man who even at this little intimate supper-party, attempted a familiarity with him. His affability to servants was well known to all who entertained him.

Queen Victoria appears to have looked askance at her. A portrait of her was bought by Prince Leopold, who hung it over his bed in Buckingham Palace, where it remained until one day when he was ill, the Queen went to see him. She evidently did not approve, for I was told she then and there took it down, standing on a chair to do so.

## DONKEY IN BED

With various Princes she indulged in amusing pranks. Thus at a friend's house at Cowes:

One night a donkey was hoisted to the bedroom of the son of the house as a surprise for him—a proceeding in which both the aforesaid Princes took part. I believe they dressed him up and put him in bed, though how to keep him there must have puzzled them.

She tells a charming story of

Disraeli:

He remarked quizzically, "What can I do for you?" As it happened to be just before the Ascot Races—a meeting where women like to wear fresh, beautiful frocks, I answered jokingly, "Four new gowns for Ascot," on which he laughed, patted my hand, and said: "You are a sensible young woman. Many a woman would have been made duchess in her own right."

An amusing passage in these lively memoirs is where she tells of an attempt to dye her hair raven black, at the advice of the Austrian Crown Prince Rudolph:

It met with enthusiastic approval on the part of the artist who had achieved the innovation. But, alas! after several washings it became yellow again, with the added glory of heliotrope-zebra-like streaks. The Viennese dye had only worked partially.
