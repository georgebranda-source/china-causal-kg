## OUR EXPORT OF GOLD

Many people who read Mr. Churchill's statement that the Act prohibiting the export of gold coin or bullion was to be repealed may be under the impression that gold has not been exported from Britain since 1919 when the Act was passed, says the "Daily Chronicle."

Nothing could be further from reality. During the last five years gold has been among the chief exports of this country, under Government licence.

In 1920 re exported 92% of the world's pounds' worth of gold; in 1921, 59 millions; in 1922, nearly 45 millions; in 1923, over 57 millions; and last year, close on 50 millions — a total of about 303 millions.

The greater part of this, no less than 177 millions, went to the United States to pay our debts. The rest, 49 millions were shipped to India, which always make a heavy demand on our resource.

## ITS ORIGIN

Whence comes the gold which we scatter so freely? Mainly from South Africa; the greater part from the Transva'th though Rhodesia is a steady contributor of about £2,000,000 a year. And there are windfalls unex- mined in the official returns. For example, France sent us £710,799 in 1920.

But on balance we have patted with nearly 9 million of gold in the last five years, and it is consulting to knc from the Chancellor of the Exerer that our accumulated reserve is as high as 153 million.

Rats are a change has come over the scene during the first three months of the present year. We have exported £11,636,685 in gold and imported £8,542,548. But of the imports the surprising thing is that £4,246,000 come from the United States.

There is another interesting feature about the Board of Trade Returns. At the end of last year we exported over £2,000,000 in gold bars to Russia, the first transaction of the kind for a very long time.

## FAMILIAR COIN

Already this year we have sent another £342,111 in gold bullion or Russia. Russia is a large place, and it is not all under the Soviet Government, so probably there is a simple explanation.

There is, at any rate, no indication in the imports of any Bolshevik gold coming to this country in a solid form.

Travellers tell us that Great Britain is about the only place in the world now in which it is difficult to come by an English sovereign. We have been broadcasting them for a long time past. Should you travel in Albania, you will find the sovereign well represented in the very much mixed coinage of that country. Among the Arabs on the shores of the Red Sea the sovereign is a familiar and highly-prized coin. Already this year two and a half millions have gone to India.

It is, perhaps, the best advertisement this country could have, for the sovereign is always "worth its weight in gold" and can now hold its head up, even in the company of dollars.

## BANK OWNERS

### MR. RUNCIMAN ON THE DANGERS OF NATIONALISATION

"Those who are conducting the campaign in favour of what they call nationalising the banks should understand that the banks are owned, not by a few rich men, but by an immense number of individual shareholders, and that the average holding is remarkably small."

This is a passage from a letter which Mr. Walter Runciman, director of the Westmioster Bank, has sent to "The Daily Chronicle," with reference to proposals at the recent I.L.P. conference for the nationalisation of the banks.

Mr. Runciman gives the following figures of five big banks:—Barclays.—Capital, £15,592,372; 51.011 shareholders; average holding, £306.

Lloyds:—Paid-up capital, £14.372;956; 55,668 shareholders: average holding; £258.

Midland. — Paid-up capital, £11,976.890; 57.250 shareholders' average, £209.

National Provincial.—Paid-up capital, £9,479,416; 41,603 shareholders; average £225.

Westminster.—Paid-up capital, £9,051,718; 69,882 shareholders; average, £130.

Total paid-up capital of the five banks, £60,463,352; amount of average holding of the 275,414 holders £210.

Mr. Runciman adds that one effect of nationalising these banks would be to buy out the tens of thousands of shareholders, and transfer control by expert financial directors to control by politicians for political purposes, which might become corrupt, and would be very dangerous.

## ALLENBY

The directors of Allenby Rubber Co., in their report for the twelve months ended February 28, last state that the net profit for the period after making due provision for depreciation was $44,255.38, to which has to be added the balance of $26,756.65 brought forward from last account making a total of $71,012.03. After deducting the interim dividend of 5 per cent. paid on January 10, 1925, there remains a balance of $55,308.74 available as at February 28, 1925. The directors recommend that a final dividend of 10 per cent. be paid for the year, absorbing $31,405.58. The total distribution of 15 per cent. is very slightly more than the profit earned, but with improved prospects and the redemption of the debenture being fully provided for the directors feel quite justified in drawing slightly on the large balance which in very different circumstances was carried forward a year ago.

## WORLD THEATRE ATTRACTION

One of the greatest mystery plays of the year was "The Roar Car" by Edward Rose. The play ran for a long period at the Majestic Theatre in Los Angeles with Richard Bonnett in the role of "crime defector." It was so successful that it was immediately taken back. Many were of the opinion that it was the best mystery play since "The Bat." The Goldwyn company made the screen version of this mystifying piece "Red Lights" as the screen version is called, and has in the east many of the screen's best artists including Raymond Griffith, Marie Prevost, Jimmy Walker, Alice Lake and others. Raymond Griffith assumed the role of the crime defector.

In "Red Lights" which is coming to the World Theatre on Wednesday next for a four days' run, the mysterious complications keep a tense interest throughout, and hold the audience in a state of suspense until the very last scene. During the introduction of the stage play the end of the audience shrieked the grasped at the thrilling situations. The screen version "Red Lights" promises to present much more of a thrill as the different scenes can be made more realistic than they could possibly have been on the stage. The greater part of the action transpires in the reascar of the Continental Limited.
