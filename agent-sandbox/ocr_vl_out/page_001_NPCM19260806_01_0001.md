## BROKER WINS

ARCHITECT FAILS IN ONUS OF PROOF.

JUDGE'S VERDICT.

Judgment was given this morning by the Puisne Judge (Mr. Justice J. R. Wood), in the Summary Court, for Mr. N. V., A. Croucher, of Messrs. Benjamin & Potts (sharebrokers), in the case in which he is sued by Mr. W. H. Ingrine, a local architect, for professional sums, for $900.

As reported yesterday, Mr. Bourne maintained that he had done work for Mr. Croucher in connection with a piece of land, at Wanchai Gap, purchased from the Government, on which Mr. Croucher intended to build a residence.

Mr. Bourne based his claim on an estimate that the projected house would cost $60,000. His fee being at 11½ per cent, he charged $900, Mr. Croucher had paid $150 into court, but Mr. C. Mienamara (for plaintiff) had described this amount as totally inadequate. Mr. M. M. Watson was for the defence.

The written judgment delivered by His Honour read:—

The plaintiff, sues the contract for professional services rendered. On the question whether a contractual relation arose between these parties the statements by the parties themselves are here the only material evidence. I accept their bona fide. Their evidence on the interviews between them is inconsistent; and this is not surprising as they deposed to incidents of twelve months ago—the present relevance of details not being then apparent to their minds. The plaintiff, in order to succeed, must show affirmatively that the services were rendered at the defendant's request. This onus of proof is here upon him. It has in my opinion not been discharged. There was most likely a genuine misunderstanding.

a genuine misunderstanding. The amount of any remuneration-need-not, therefore, be considered. I give judgment for the defendant with costs.

From the above it is implied that Mr. Bourne failed to prove that the sketch plan was drawn at Mr. Croucher's request; and that His Honour did not wish to pass any opinion on the amount of $150 paid into court by Mr. Croucher as being his (defendant's) idea of what the payment for services should be.

## BRIBE OFFERED

TRUCK WHICH OBSTRUCTED TRAMCAR.

A CONSTABLE'S CONDUCT.

A Chinese who thought he "could escape the vigilance of the law by proferring a bribe to an Indian constable found himself charged on an additional count at the Magistracy this morning.

Evidence was given that he and three other men pulling a truck held up a trumour near the junction of Des Voeux Road West and Hill Road West and continued on the track after repeated soundings by the tram driver.

An Indian constable who witnessed the incident approached the truck and those in charge of it, took the number and name of licensee (the defendant), indicating that a summons would follow. The defendant then offered him 70 cents to keep quiet about it and persisted in forcing it on the constable in spite of repeated refusals. The constable eventually took the money and reported the matter to the inspector in charge when he got back to the station.

Defendant denied having offered the money at the court this morning and said that one of the other men with him had done so. It was pointed out to him that in a statement made when first charged he had admitted giving the money but said that the suggestion came from one of the other men.

The Magistrate, Mr. R. E. Lindsell, fined defendant $5 on the charge of obstruction and $25 on the charged of attempted bribery, the alternative being three weeks' imprisonment.

### "SEE BRITAIN."

GOVERNMENT'S INTEREST.

Rugby, August 5.

Representatives of steamship lines, railways, the Chamber of Commerce, and other organisations this afternoon met Mr. A. M. Samuel, Parliamentary Secretary of the Overseas Department of the Board of Trade, to discuss how visitors can be attracted to Britain.

A movement with this object is being enthusiastically supported by many of the leading organisations and public men and Mr. Samuel to-day took the first step on the part of the Government to assist it. The meeting was of a preliminary character to decide along what lines action could best be taken. An official of the Board of Trade interested in the scheme pointed out that:—

## Some Anxious Moments Described

Mr. Alan Cobham's successful flight to Australia is regarded as an important achievement. It is not the first time that Australia has been reached by air, and Mr. Cobham did not attempt a speed record. But the flight was carried out against many natural difficulties, and the only incident was the unfortunate death of Mr. A. B. Elliott, his mechanic. The flight therefore, means that we are within reasonable distance of passenger flights from Britain to Australia becoming matters of every-day routine. It is also a triumph for British aircraft, for Mr. Cobham's machine is "the same which he used for his flights to India and South Africa. When he lands in Britain again the machine will have covered 60,000 miles. When Mr. Cobham landed at Port Darwin he received a telegram of congratulation from Mr. S. M. Bruce, the Australian Prime Minister, who said: "You have made a valuable contribution to British aviation."

Mr. Cobham describes in a cablegram to the Evening News how he flew the last 400 miles from Koopang, in the Dutch East Indies, to the Australian coast. "We had to trust first in the reliability of our Siddeley Jaguar engine; and, secondly, we had to depend on our compass to bring us out on a dead reckoning. For hours we were out of sight of land. It was lonely flying, and I had to content myself with keeping a correct course over hundreds of miles of water. I began to get a little anxious when the time which we had calculated for the flight had passed, but at last we sighted land at Bathurst Island."

Regarding other stages Mr. Cobham says "After leaving the Mediterranean we had dust storms and great heat in Syria and Mesopotamia. Then came the tragic death of Mr. Elliott. When we continued the flight we had heat and stormy mist down the Persian Gulf to Bundar Abbas, where rough seas detained us. From Karachi to Delhi we had bad dust storms, and at Allahabad and Delhi the monsoon, which increased in violence as we flew south to Calcutta and Akyab, in Burma. We managed to fly between the worst storms until, arriving at Rangoon, we were forced to land in a tropical downpour. As we continued southward the rain lessened. At Singapore we again had heavy rains. Then at Muntok and Batalvia we finally met fine weather and clear skies."—British Wireless Service.
