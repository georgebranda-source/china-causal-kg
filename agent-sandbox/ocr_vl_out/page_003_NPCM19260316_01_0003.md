## CO-OPERATION

Iri the course of his speech, at the sixth reunion annual dinner of the Queen's College Old Boys Association, Mr. C. G. Anderson, the Hon Secretary, said: "Mr. President and gentlemen, as I have pointed out, this is our Sixth Annual Re-union and incidentally this is the fourth occasion in which you request me to speak in this Hall. On account of my poverty in idea I may, perhaps, with profit refer briefly to a question upon which I had previously touched. I recolect that at our Second Annual Dinner in 1922 and when called upon to speak, I ventured the opinion that we, as a body in whom the wisdom of the East is blended with the knowledge of the West, are peculiarly fitted to promote Anglo-Chinese co-operation, which, to quote my own words, was now more than ever necessary for the prosperity of the Colony and the well-being of its citizens."

"Gentlemen, if co-operation be needed in 1922, its necessity in 1926 is imperative amid I found myself in good company when HE, Sir Reginald Stubbs said, in a speech made a few days prior to his departure from the Colony, to the effect that, should a motto be required for Hongkong, the word "co-operation" should find in it a prominent place. You will also recollect, gentlemen, that the English Press, whose representatives and their Chinese colleagues, we also have the honour of entertaining this evening, at once took up this standpoint and a symposium of the vjews of certain Chinese gentle-men as to the desirability of forming an Anglo-Chinese Club as a membership towards co-operation, was started by the "Hongkong Daily Press." This desirable innovation, if adopted, I daresay you agree with me, is merely one of the means to an end. But to my mind, co-operation that is real can only be achieved by the general recognition of certain principles.

On the one hand, Britons should realise that every Chinese has a soul, that China, having a long glorious past, will have a glorious future with the possibility of contributing largely towards world peace, the attainment of which must be the concern of every right-minded individual and that

must be the concern of every right-minded individual and that China does not take kindly to, nor does she deserve the shallow sneers and criticisms that have been flung at her by those least qualified to judge. On the other hand, Chinese should realise that a great part of the British public and I may say the best part of the British public has the best goodwill towards them, that the Briton who only knows force in his dealings with them is not representative of his race and frowned upon by his betters and that Great Britain is a great nation through the portals of whose world-famous universities and institutions the most useful, gifted and brilliant of China's sons, including those at Canton, have passed and will continue to pass.

"With these ideas embedded in the mind of the two peoples, understanding and sympathy will appear and co-operation follows as a natural result; by the side of these ideas any artificial attempt towards co-operation, though desirable and necessary, tends to fade into insignificance." These principles are the "sine-qua-now of co-operation that is real" and in giving them publicity, I indulge in no gestures, I am influenced by this fact, that the thought that Great Britain and China should work together to the advantage and glory of both countries strikes a responsive chord in the breast of every member of the association in which I have the honour to serve in a humble capacity."

## BOXER INDEMNITY

WORK OF THE COMMITTEE

NOT AN EMPTY-HANDEMB GIFT

This outlook is emphasized by Professor Soothill, one of the members of the Boxer Indemnity Committee now in Shanghai. He anticipates work lasting six months or more with his colleagues in connection with the subject, and his outlook on the task is more than interesting. Professor Soothill is, of course, thoroughly intimate with China, and while he has been away from the country for several years, he mentions that he has maintained touch with it through the medium of the "North China Herald" and the Chinese he has met at Home. He is thus thoroughly conversant with the spirit of the different movements in the country.

In reply to questions put to him, Professor Soothill said: "We are" not able to say yet what is going to be done with the money. The whole matter awaits the fullest consideration by the representatives out here, and we hope next week to meet to consider the matter. The British Government has been very anxious that the representation of British and Chinese on the spot should be absolutely equal. This was a matter that was improved very particularly on the Statutory Committee at Home, that never was done out here should be on the basis of an equality of membership. This is a matter of prime importance—that there should be no desire to outnumber the Chinese members in voting power, as seems to have been thought by some. It is in the power of the Chinese to create a similar advisory commission here of Chinese, as we created our advisory commission at Home, and the Government has appointed us to come out here and meet them in equal membership, and they are made members of the statutory commission. There has been no intention to have them in a minority, but the principal gives equal value to their vote, and we shall value their advice very highly.

A very great point to be emphasized with reference to the disposal of the British portion of the Boxer Indemnity fund is that it is not an empty-handed gift. The money has gone in the past to the assistance of British revenue, and in future this will not be so. It may be thought that, forming as it does only a very small part of the revenue of the British Government this might be regarded as almost a negligible matter, and doubtless many people in this country look on it as such, but this is a mistake—Burdened with debt as Great Britain is today, every pound counts, and the British taxpayer has to supply in taxes what the country loses by placing at the disposal of China this annual sum. Accordingly it is to be hoped that those who will benefit from the work of the committee now in China charged with allocating to some special object the Indemnity Fund will realise what it means from the purely financial point of view.

With reference to the different schemes brought before the Committee for the disposal of the fund, Professor Soothill was reminded of the proposal of Holland to use the Dutch portion of the Indemnity for investigation of the conservancy of the Yellow River. Professor Soothill mentioned that railway and conservancy schemes had been brought before the British Committee, and had been duly considered. While he did not wish to speak for the Committee, he expressed the view that given stable conditions in China such schemes as conservancy and railway development would find all the support, financial and otherwise, that would be required. The best men in the world could be secured to carry them out, and after all a comparatively small sum such as the Boxer Indemnity could very easily be wasted on an attempt to inaugurate them. While any suggestions of the sort would be sympathetically considered, he thought that very great care should be exercised in going into them for the reason he had given. Furthermore, he felt that any such schemes would be better entrusted to men who were interested financially in their success.

Professor Soothill stated that he was quite unable to say how the Indemnity money would be spent. The scheme they were looking for was one which would make an equal appeal to what might be described as the most responsible type of Chinese and the most responsible type of Englishman. The Committee wanted to get at the feeling of these men, and the intention of the British Government was to carry this idea out in whatever was done. Shanghai is to be the headquarters of the Committee, but if conditions indicate the advisability of such course, this members may visit other places. While a few in formal discussions have already taken place with local residents, nothing of a definite nature has yet occurred, but the Committee will welcome representations from responsible bodies in connection with their work. In regard to this, he again wished to emphasize that the views of the three Chinese members would carry great weight with the Committee at Home.



Lord Willingdon's Membership.

The presence of Lord Willingdon on the Committee is very greatly welcomed by Professor Soothill, especially on account of his rare character. Liberal in his outlook, and thoroughly generous in spirit, he is, in Professor Soothill's opinion, always able to see the point of view of the other man, and so is extremely valuable. He has great confidence in his judgment.

In this connection Professor Soothill paid a very fine tribute to Lord Willingdon when entertained at a Chinese dinner at Home prior to leaving. He there mentioned that Lord Willingdon was captain of the cricket side at Eton, and again at Cambridge. He had played cricket in the Government, he had played cricket in the administration of India, and he was going to play cricket when he came out to China. In fact he was a man who would always play the game. Missionaries and the Indemnity.

In the matter of the Indemnity money, " considered Professor Soothill, it would be very unwise for any Christian missionary society to use it for their ends, and he was very glad that these bodies had decided not to apply for or accept it.

At the same time he believed that they might have been justified in doing so, considering how this money came. It was supposed generally that, by remitting the Indemnity, Britain was merely handing back the money to the Chinese, merely wiping off a debt. On the contrary it must be realised that the people of Great Britain would have to raise an equal sum at Home, possibly by increased taxation, and really would have to pay just as much as the Chinese were paying. That was a point which did not seem to have been brought out previously, but it was very true. Nevertheless, as mentioned, he strongly approved the attitude of the missions in that it should not be used in their propaganda, and he considered that the scheme for the use of the money should be decided on the broadest possible lines.

Driven by the thoughts that they could never be united in lawful marriage, a geisha girl named Hideji, aged 24, at a Japanese restaurant on Chapoo Road, Shanghai, and a Japanese man named K. Ezaki, aged 28, an employee of the restaurant, attempted a double suicide at the Belvue Hotel in Woosung on March 6, at midnight which resulted in the death of the man and the near death of the geisha.

The two were found by boys at the hotel shortly after they had cut the arteries in their wrists. They were locked tightly in each other's arms.

Physicians were summoned and succeeded in stopping the flow of blood from the young woman's body, but were unable to save the man. The Japanese authorities in Shanghai were notified and an inquest was held over the body at the Japanese Consulate. The girl was taken to the Foo Min Hospital on North Szechuen Road.

The couple went to Woosung and registered at the hotel. Several times they took over doses of morphia which Ezaki took along; but always rallied from the effects of the drug.

According to the girl's story the two fell in love, but knowing that they could never be married because of the high social barrier they chose suicide.
