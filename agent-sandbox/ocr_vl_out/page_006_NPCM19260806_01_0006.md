## OVERWORKED!

In the publication of the report for 1925 on the Sanitary Department will be found overwhelming evidence in support of recent articles in this paper contending that this all-important Department is grossly under staffed and overworked.

These annual reports rarely get into the hands of the average reader, who has to be content with whatever excerpts are published in the newspapers. For that reason it is not surprising to find a lack of appreciation in many quarters of the work of the Sanitary Department. But the more one studies the latest annual report the more one is driven to the conclusion that, in view of the shockingly low European personnel, the Department accomplishes truly marvellous work—work that would be a credit to the most thorough and up-to-date business establishment at Home.

Everywhere the hand of the Sanitary Inspector is seen—and felt. It is easier to enumerate what he does not do than what he does. There is routine work under the Public Health and Buildings Ordinance; there is work under the Food and Drugs Ordinance; there is scavenging, refuse removal, and nightsoil removal to supervise; there are public bath houses to be seen to; there are water closets and public conveniences to be looked after; there are cemeteries, mortuaries, and crematoria that require supervision — and right on through the whole gamut of the sanitary requirements of the Colony. Strikes may—and do—occur; floods come and necessitate volumes of extra work on the part of the Sanitary Inspectors and their gangs; but everything is performed as if the unusual were just the normal.

The report for 1925 discloses a staff of one Chief Inspector, four Senior Inspectors, and 38 Inspectors. With an army of 1,500 subordinates they look after the sanitary needs of practically the whole Island and Kowloon. Last year alone they had 16,866 "nuisances reported" to deal with (including 554 outstanding on December 31, 1924). There was not one nuisance reported in which action was not taken.

There were premises to inspect summonses to be taken out, prosecutions to be initiated, and Police Courts to attend—all eating into an otherwise very busy day's work. How it is all done is a mystery that baffles us. With even treble the present European staff the verdict would be that they do wonders in a community in which the Chinese lower classes predominate and in which, therefore, widespread ignorance obtains in regard to sanitation and hygiene. There were no fewer than 441 prosecutions, the fines totalling $4,324. Moreover in the sanitary districts on the Island, 90,567 floors were cleansed, the number in Kowloon being 44,800. There were 27,557 cases of limewashing on the Island and 11,997 in Kowloon.

Thus we might go on! Even were we to reproduce a Sanitary Inspector's notebook for one day's duty it would give an inadequate conception of the amount of labour of the European personnel as a whole. Nothing more has been heard of the project to increase that staff commensurate with the growing sanitary requirements of the Colony; but

something must be done in this direction even if only to cope with emergencies. Whilst the P.W.D. rightly earned a meed of praise for its fine work as the result of the recent floods, its confreres in the Sanitary Department were not one whit behind. It is as well that this fact should be known; indeed, to make no mention of it would be invidious. But the point is that the present strength of the Europeans in this Department cannot be expected to go on from year to year, emergencies or no emergencies, with only a very trifling increase. There may arise an emergency for which even three times the existing personnel could not possibly cope with. in which event — well, Heaven help Hongkong!

THAT NAVAL RESERVE.

of the "China Mall."

Sir,—It is just six months since H.E. the Governor made a public reference to the formation locally of a Hongkong Naval Reserve and many young fellows want to know what has been done about it. Singapore and Ceylon had naval reserve units even before the Governor's speech and since then Malaya has fallen into line. Hongkong, it would appear, "with its usual "maskee" spirit has let the matter drift as one of those things which "will be attended to in due course."

At the Imperial Defence Committee meetings held early in the year, at which the Colonies and Dominions were represented, the latter indicated their willingness to shoulder a burden which was growing increasingly heavy on the Home taxpayers—the maintenance of naval vessels and personnel. Hongkong is hardly in a position to build ships or to shoulder more than a small part of the cost of Imperial Defence but the formation locally of a Royal Naval Voluntary Reserve would give local people the opportunity of indicating their willingness to do what is in their power. Incidentally, it would provide young fellows with invaluable experience and the opportunity of healthful relaxation and exercise.

Shortly after His Excellency's announcement was made a report appeared that the local naval authorities would consider the lending of a nucleus crew for the voluntary men to work with and of making over two mine laying sloops for the maintenance of which the Reserve unit would be responsible.

Having regard to the fact that Hongkong, as His Excellency remarked at the time, as one of the most important outposts of the Empire, is dependent for its prosperity on the maintenance of its shipping connections, it is to be hoped that this promise of the naval authorities will be made use of and that the whole thing will not fall through for lack of interest on the part of the powers that be.

There are several ex-naval men at present resident in the Colony who would be prepared to give their services to such a unit and we have the Royal Hongkong Yacht Club which undoubtedly would provide other enthusiasts with the advantage of a knowledge of our local coasts.

The formation of such a Reserve in Hongkong will not only be a gesture to the Home Government but a very practical solution to the problem of Imperial defence. If the appeal is launched it may safely be said that it will not fail through lack of volunteers. I only hope that this letter will catch the eye of someone in a position to launch it.

## Yours, etc

QUESTION OF JURISDICTION.

In an action in the Supreme Court in which an American wild animal collector named Frank H. Buck, was successfully sued by Meyer Franklin Kline to recover two sums amounting to $880, there was argument, between the Chief Justice, Sir William Murlson, and Mr. C. Smith, who appeared for the plaintiff, "ns to the extent of the jurisdiction of the Court. According to pleadings the defendant was employed by plaintiff to solicit advertisements" for an O.S.K. guide called the Official Shippers' Guide, of which the plaintiff was the publisher. Plaintiff claimed $375 which he alleged the defendant had been paid in 1920 by Messrs. Latham and Co. in respect of an advertisement which should have appeared in the 1921 edition of the guide; and a further sum of $505 paid to defendant, after his contract had expired, by the Singapore office of the O.S.K., who were the agents of the plaintiff in Singapore. The defence was that the claim was barred by the Ordinance of Limitations.

The evidence of plaintiff, taken de bene esse, stated that Buck was an American and ordinarily resided at Los Angeles. At the time of his last visit to Singapore he had a farm there where he collected wild animals. Plaintiff also stated that he met Buck at Raffles Hotel during 1925 and during the interview defendant admitted that he owed the money but said he had no money, and would pay plaintiff some day.

It was in the course of his argument against the contention of the defence that the claim was statute barred that Mr. Smith pointed out that the contract was "to be performed in a number of places all over the East, but not in Singapore, which was expressly excluded from the contract. The contract, therefore, could not be performed within the jurisdiction.

Replying to the Chief Justice Mr. Smith said the jurisdiction of that Court was Singapore. The Chief Justice said the Court was the Court of the Straits Settlements.

Mr. Smith: "It is the Settlement of Singapore. The heading of the writ is for the Settlement of Singapore. There is not a general writ which applies here and in Penang.

The Chief Justice—Do you mean to say that a writ issued by me in Penang would be bad?

Mr. Smith said that if the cause of action did not arise in the jurisdiction the case could not be tried here.

The Chief Justice: Where the case is tried, is different matter. There is only one Court, the Court of the Straits Settlements. It may sit in Penang or Malacca, but it is the same Court.

Mr. Smith submitted, with respect, that the Court sitting in Singapore is limited to Singapore. He submitted that the position was analogous, with an Assize Court in England. The Assize Court in Staffordshire could only try cases which arose within the county of Staffordshire.

The Chief Justice replied that a judge of the High Court could try anybody wherever the King's writ runs.

Mr. Smith: If a plaintiff in Penang came to bring an action in Singapore he would cause endless trouble.

The Chief Justice: It is only for convenience. Do you mean to say that I could not go to Penang to try a case?

—Mr. Smith: The jurisdiction of this Court is limited to Singapore but your lordship's power to try cases is not limited. Mr. Smith added that in England a man in Staffordshire could not bring a case at the Warwickshire Assizes.
