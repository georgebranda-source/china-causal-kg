## CORRESPONDENCE

"SHORTS" OR "LONGS."

(To the Editor of the China Mail.)

Sir,—We are evidently a little more enlightened or broadminded in Hongkong than they are in Shanghai, for no one entering the Hongkong Club in "white shorts and light grey stockings but otherwise in accordance with tradition" would consider that they were laying themselves open to be called even a "bold spirit" let along—"Bolshevik" and be forced to "pay for his own drinks," which I gather from a article published from a Shanghai newspaper was the fate of a "bold spirit" in Shanghai with "the courage of his convictions."

Even so, shorts have not yet received the measure of recognition in Hongkong to which they are entitled as a comfortable, smart and economical medium of clothing one's nether limbs, and I venture to state that in most offices in the Colony askance would be looked at any junior who appeared too often in them.

"One likes to look smart sometimes," was the remark, intended to be caustic, which was made to me when I asked someone the other day how it was that we did not for good "cast off those works of darkness" and put on something allowing air to circulate freely, but I fail to see how the comparison between the two should be against shorts for smartness particularly at the end of the day by which time trouser creases have disappeared and a bagginess has set in at the knees.

For motor cyclists who are called upon to use their machines in the course of the day a pair of khaki shorts is the only practicable garb, as trousers present but a sorry appearance if dust and given a speck of oil gets on to them, let alone the liquid mud accumulating in puddles which are all too numerous because of the bad state of many of the roads.

The only drawback to other than the objective decency, attributed to "and which is too fantastic" considered, is that one is more easily accessible to the attacks of mosquitoes, but it would only be a question of time before one's skin would toughen sufficiently.

It is doubtful if such an opportunity as is now given will ever again return if those who really believe in the advantage of “shorts” over “longs” allow it to slip. Personally, I shall continue to don the former even if the groove of tradition proves too strong for most when the amahs return in more considerable bodies and there is less strenuous work to do, but to those who would like to agree with me but who feel that they must go with the crowd and do as others do my advice is that they should see this opportunity is not lost through lack of action on your part.

## EMPIRE FOODSTUFFS

STIMULATING CONSUMPTION.

(Reuter's Service.)

London, August 10.

The Imperial Economic Committee's first report on marketing Empire foodstuffs declares that the time is ripe for a national effort to simulate the consumption of Empire produce in the United Kingdom. As far as the State is concerned the scheme should rest on, firstly, legal requirements with a view to identification of Empire goods; secondly, financial assistance for education and publicity which the committee regard as mutually essential.

The Merchandise Marks (Imported Agricultural Produce) Bill at present before Parliament will form a convenient basis for

The enforcement of the law in respect of marketing at the time of importation, and labelling in time for retail sale should rest on existing departments of the State.

Executive Commission should be formed on the model of the Development and Forestry Commissions, for the purpose of supervising the expenditure of the annual grant from the British Parliament, and should be charged as a duty in conducting movement for trade in Empire produce.

The Commission should start by allocating about 65 per cent. of the annual grant for the promotion of trade in Empire produce and about 15 per cent. for research; the remaining 20 per cent. should be reserved for certain other schemes, including the promotion of fruit-growing in tropical portions of the Empire, and the carriage of pedigree stock from the United Kingdom to overseas parts of the Empire.

## HONGKONG POLICE

THE TALLEST MAN IN

A NEW ARRIVAL.

There arrived by the s.s. Khybar on Friday a new recruit for the Hongkong Police Force in the person of Mr. H. G. Baker, son of a former Chief Inspector who retired in 1913.

Mr. Baker rendered distinguished service as a Second Lieutenant in the late war which earned him the M.C., and until lately held an appointment in the Gloucestershire Constabulary.

The new officer is 29 years of age, and stands six feet five inches in height. He thus becomes the tallest officer in the local Police Force.

## ARMED BURGLAR

By virtue of the power given them by the Emergency Regulations Mr. E. W. Hamilton and Mr. H. D. Forrest, sitting together at the Kowloon Magistracy yesterday, sentenced a Chinese to four years' hard labour on a charge of unlawful possession of a dugger.

## WATER SUPPLY

In spite of the fact that last year there was a three-day intermittent supply in certain of the rider main districts while this year there has been a constant supply in all distriets, the Water Return for the month of July shows a decrease in the consumption as compared with that of the same period of 1924.

This year the consumption for an estimated population of 303,000 an increase of 10,320—is 21.4 gallons per head per day, against 22.5 for July, 1924. There is approximately 20 million gallons less water than last year in storage in spite of the decreased consumption, the total storage this year amounting to 2,097 millions gallons.

Storage in Kowloon is greater than last year owing to the inclusion in the return of the recently constructed Shek Li Pui Reservoir.

The comparative rainfalls for July 1924 and July 1925 are given as 71.68 inches and 67.54 inches respectively, and the Government Analyst's report shows the quality of the water to be satisfactory.
