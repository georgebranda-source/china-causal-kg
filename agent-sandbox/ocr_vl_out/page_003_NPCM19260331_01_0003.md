## ITALIAN CONVENT ANNUAL FUNCTION

## INSPECTOR'S REPORT

Giving away the prizes yesterday at the annual distribution (private) of the Italian Convent (Canossian Institute) in Caine Road, the Rev. Father G. M. Spada, Vicar Apostolic of Hong Kong, congratulated the teachers and pupils on the satisfactory results obtained both at the University and school examinations.

Pupils in general attend school regularly, and the number of pupils lost owing to the "strike" is now not only regained but surpassed. From Class 8 upwards the pupils are accommodated in the new premises where the classrooms are cool, airy and light.

The headmistress's report was presented as follows:—

The work of the school continues to be good as can be gathered from the inspector's report and University successes.

Discipline, writes Mr. E. Ralph, is excellent. Reading and recitation are good throughout the school, and the dictation tests generally very satisfactory. It was noted with satisfaction that practical English lessons take the place of formal grammar lessons in the junior school.

## Keynote of Happiness

Drawing in all its branches is well taught, and much of the work shewn, especially in the senior classes, was of a very high order. Nature study drawing in connection with object lessons was very satisfactory. Written work in all classes was very neat. and carefully corrected. In the kindergarten and junior classes very successful work was shewn.

The children sang and recited and obviously thoroughly enjoyed doing so. Happiness appeared to be the key-note of these classes—this is as it should be—singing throughout the school has greatly improved during recent years.

The special classes for Chinese girls preparing for the English classes are very well taught; the written work shewn was excellent.

Mrs. Sutherland was kind enough to inspect the needlework and was much impressed with the usefulness and excellence of the work done.

University successes: During the year 15 pupils entered for the senior local examination of the Hongkong University, and 12 passed; 14 entered for the junior local examination and 12 passed; Miss Lily Williams, Miss Carolina Mather, and Miss Tulie Barreto with distinction in biblical knowledge; Miss Emerenina Santos and Miss Molly Franco with distinction in needlework.

Scholarships: Miss Conchita Milian is the winner of the Lugard scholarship for the Year 1926-1927. Miss Olga King and Miss Carmen Xavier are the winners of Lady Ho Tung scholarships for good conduct and application.

## PLEADS GUILTY

At the resumed hearing of the case against a Chinese coolie who was caught in the act of boiling illicit opium inside the laboratory at the Import and Export department (reference to which was made in yesterday's "China Mail") the accused tendered a plea of guilty. Mr. M. W. Lo, for the defence, said the amount of opium was not large and the man's object was to use it for an injury to his leg.

Accused, who was stated to have been in the service of the Government for eleven years, was sentenced to four months' hard labour, the Magistrate (Mr. R. E. Lindsell) remarking that it was a serious offence for a man in his position.

## OUR EMPIRE

## PATIENT UNDERSTANDING AND INDIAN PROBLEMS

Colonial Institute at a farewell lunch, on March 3, Lord Fitzalan presiding, and 200 were present, including the Earl of Meath, Lord Aberdeen, Lord Emmott, Lord Glendyne, Sir A. Chatterjee, Sir Francis Younghusband, Sir Francis Wingute and Sir Benjamin Robertson.

Proposing the guest: Lord Fitzalan said without hesitation: "In Lord Irwin we have the right man." (Applause.) He paid a tribute to Lord Irwin's up-rightness, conscientiousness and courage. He believed that he would act up to his terribly responsible duties. Lord Irwin, responding, pointed out that great public affairs often competed for attention unequally with football, murder and similar things. This was often true of the British attitude towards the Empire, which was accepted like the sun and the moon. The British were often puzzled at the position in India, finding that the reforms which they expected to lead to peace, were only criticised.

The British under such conditions turned away to mind their own business and left the solution of the difficulty to others. Lord Irwin regarded this as a dangerous attitude. He added that all portions of the Empire had their own problems, all depending on the health and welfare of the British Commonwealth. Many of them were most obstinate, but the solution could often be found in patient understanding. For with politics, as with bootlaces, patience alone could untie knots where a short temper and bad language would only confound confusions. The problems of India were not the least intricate. They demanded courage and goodwill by all interested in India's history, which was mainly directed by Britons who had given time, means, health and often their lives.

"In future we are pledged to carry on our work in India in partnership with the peoples of India. The partnership will bring its own difficulties. Nevertheless, we are convinced that in India's future Britons and Indians have both a vital contribution to make, demanding, on the part of Great Britain, interest and knowledge, for interest without knowledge seldom brought great illusion (sic) or public profit." The prospect of a contented India within the British Empire was a British no less than an Indian interest and on its achievements in great part, depended the relations which their children's children would see existing between the civilisations of East and West.

BETRAYED BY AN AMAH'S

## HOUSE BOY'S PROMPTITUDE

There has been a disconcerting series of petty thefts from Kowloon flats of late, household linen and perambulators being carried off by the light fingered gentry.

Following the theft of four sheets from the roof of one flat and sheets and blankets from an adjoining flat, a washing amah at 318 Nathan Road set out to he in walt daily for potential thieves after she had hung the washing on the line on the roof. Her patience was rewarded yesterday about 1.30 p.m.

From the roof of No. 311 Nathan Road she espied a Chinese making his way to her roof, and she immediately uttered a yell, which was heard by the boy on the second floor. The latter dashed out and intercepted the would-be thief as he was making his exit from the ground floor passage. The intruder was eventually conveyed to the Yaumati Police Station.

This is the second "undesirable" caught red handed at the same address within a few weeks, and both were taken to the Police Court by Mr. W. J. Stoneham. The offender in the first case received a sentence of two months' hard labour.

### “100 SECREETS.”

## BOOK SHOP MANAGER IN COURT

How many secrets does Shanghai hold? That is the question. One individual evidently is of the opinion that it holds one hundred and evidently thinks that he is in possession of them all. An advance, notification has been circulated in Shanghai that there will shortly appear on the market, a certain book containing these one hundred secrets and this advance information was the immediate cause for the appearance of Li Tsung-yung in the Mixed Court on March 24, says the "Shanghai Times."

The police obtained information that a certain booklet had been issued in Shanghai to the effect that in a short time there would be published a book entitled "Hundred Secrets" and which was to reveal the most interesting information on subjects and individuals in this city. The booklet was issued from the Kung Wo Book Store and it was the manager of this establishment who appeared in the Court on March 24. There was a sample article in the booklet published by the accused and as a result he was charged with publishing and selling this paper with the indecent article.

The book of the "Hundred Secrets" is alleged to contain the most immoral tales and literature of the worst kind and as Detective Sullivan informed the Court several attempts had been made to have it published in newspapers in the city. These attempts had proved futile as the newspaper refused to take the responsibility of publishing such matter.

Dr. G. Sellet appeared in Court in behalf of a certain Chinese lady who claims that by innuendo and other subtle evidence she had been slandered by the article published in the advance booklet.

A copy of the "Hundred Secrets" which has not yet been published was produced for the benefit of the Court.

The Court imposed a fine of $50 and ordered the accused to put up a bond of $500 to insure his appearance when the editor and the brother of the editor are arrested and charged with the editing of the book.

ADVENTUROUS VOYAGERS REACH COOKTOWN.

A REMARKABLE JOURNEY.

Brisbane, March 7.

The Japanese lugger. "Kelsia" arrived on March 4 at Cooktown from Thursday Island, bringing Captain Carl Fitzpauhl, a German, and Waldamar Tregouboff, a Russian, who were picked up at Margaret Bay. When interviewed, Captain Fitzpauhl stated that five men, Edward Clements, an American, Poynton, an Englishman, Colin Dodd, a Scotsman, Fitzpauhl, and Tregouboff, left Shanghai on June 7 last, in a partly decked-in bont named. "The Sampan," 28ft. in length, and 7ft. 6in. in beam, for Australia.

Their intention was to visit Hongkong and other Chinese ports, but monsoons frustrated their plans. They visited the Philippines Island, and intended to make for Cooktown, Calms and Townsville.

They left Thursday Island on February 13, and on February 16. "The Sampan" became sluggish, and it was decided to "bonch and clean her." A strong south-east wind sprung up and drove the boat high up on the bench. She sprung a leak, and it was decided to abandon her. Dodd left the party at Thursday Island. Clements and Poynton left on February 16, to secure help from a supposed store-keeper's miles along the coast, were picked up by the lugger "Amy", and taken to Lockhardt Mission Station.

There was no shortage of food or water. Although in splendid condition, the party is financially destitute.

NO COUNTRY!

HOTEL PROPRIETORS APPEAL TO AMERICA.

FORMERLY IN HONGKONG.

The Shanghai "Evening News" of March 25 says:

An appeal to the president, congress and people of the United States for restoration of his citizenship and recovery of his property from the Chinese, has been filed by William Frederick proprietor of St. George's Hotel and dancing pavilion.

According to his appeal, which is entitled "Chinese Justice", Mr. Frederick came to the United States from Germany in 1875, was naturalized in 1896, came to Hongkong in 1808, entered the Chinese Maritime Customs service, and was registered as an American citizen in China until 1918 when registration was refused by the American Consul-General in Shanghai.

## Was "Squeezed" Dry

After thus becoming: "a man without a country," he opened a hotel in Chapel and for two years barely made a living. Then he opened a dancing pavilion and when he began to make money the Chinese authorities began to "squeeze" him until the process become unbearable.

When he refused to be "squeezed" further he was thrown in military prison. He was released after a time, but his hotel valued at $25,000, is still being held by the Chinese, as well as $1,100 stolen from him.

In his appeal, Mr. Frederick declares that since he opened St. George's in the international Settlement he has been accorded full justice by municipal authorities and that no effort whatever has been made to "squeeze" him.

In the course of his appeal he says:—

"I was born in Germany on October 8, 1875 and came to the United States in 1888. For three years I attended the Barrington, Ill. High School after which I went to work. In 1896, on October 6, in the Superior Court of Cook Country, Illinois, I was admitted to citizenship, by certificate of naturalisation as a minor. In 1898, I made a voyage to Hongkong on the Boston bark "E.C.Mort," and was paid off in Hongkong at the American consulate. On the advice of the Consul and assisted by a letter from him to the Customs head at Canton, I obtained a position as Tidewaiter in the Chinese Maritime Customs service as one of several Americans who were appointed to Customs positions about that time. This was in June, 1898, and I was registered as an American citizen in the Canton consulate immediately.

“I have been registered as an American continuously until 1918, when I was refused registration by the American Consul General in Shanghai.”

[The remainder of the appeal is not of any local interest.]

OTHER CASES

In the Bankruptcy Court yesterday before Sir Henry Gollan, the Official Receiver applied for adjudication in the case of Messrs. G. P. and H. A. Lammert. After protracted negotiations, he said, two schemes were approved by a first meeting of creditors but at a subsequent meeting they were not approved, owing to absence of assets. His Lordship granted adjudication.

The affairs of the Wing Shing Shun firm in connection with which allegations of fraud were referred to at a previous court, again came up for discussion at yesterday's Bankruptcy court, a receiving order being made. His Lordship directed the Official Receiver to withhold the sale until enquiries had been made.
