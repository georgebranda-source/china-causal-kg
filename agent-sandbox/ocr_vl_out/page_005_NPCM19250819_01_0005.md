## FOR SCHOOLS AND COLLEGES

"STRICTNESS" TO BU THE ONLY POLICY.

## ALL POLITICAL MOVEMENTS PROHIBITED

The following is a letter to the various educational institutions in Namtung, Kiangsu, by the learned Chinese educationalist: Mr. Cheng Chin.

Dear Sirs,—That Education can crush the life of a nation is absolutely beyond my anticipation. What is more unfortunate is the fact that our students play so eminent a part in leading the nation to disgrace. Never perhaps, is there a thing more lamentable than a ruined nation. Yet a kingdom is never subject to destruction unless it causes itself to decay.

Mencius said, "When superiors do not observe the rules of propriety, and inferiors do not learn, then seditious people spring up and that kingdom will perish in no time."

Kun-tsung said, "Propriety, righteousness, self-denying purity and the sense of shame are the four indispensable principles that uphold a kingdom. When these four principles are neglected, then the kingdom will not stand." These sayings have been proved to be true.

For some thirty years Chinese has been in an almost perishing state. At the end of the 19th century, the ministers, civil and military, alike, neglected their duties. The people, superior and inferior alike, were idle and dilatory. The government acted against the standard rules of governing the kingdom and they also failed to understand the situation in those days. China was all confusion, due to the ignorance of the ministers.

The trouble in the administration gave birth to anti-foreignism, which in its turn, led to rise of the Boxers that menaced North China. With the allied forces entering Peking, China we, scarcely a nation, thanks to the seditious people.

These disastrous actions of the ignorant ministers and of the seditious people naturally resulted from "want of common sense" of the people at large. Then it was suggested that the only remedy for the situation was education. The government accepted the proposition and proceeded to work. The establishment of certain colleges was the first step.

However, I was of opinion that the first step should consist of upbringing well-educated teachers for the primary schools. Therefore, without the aid of the government, I organised a number of normal schools. Subsequently, there arose other normal schools in the provinces of Hupeh, Hunan, Kiangsu and Kiangsi.

Personally, I thought that if the future of our country depended upon the students, there might be a chance of success. My reason was this. The educational system was just like a river. The normal schools were to open the source; the primary schools to proceed from the source; the middle schools to direct the course; the technical schools to divide into tributaries, and finally the colleges to collect the whole volume of water, and bring it out to sea. Having passed all these necessary stages, a scholar could justly be called a scholar and the farmer, the workman, the trader would deserve their names. Then with a thorough education, the people might subsequently become members of parliament or ministers, understanding perfectly the meaning of self-denying purity and shame. Or, they might become soldiers or sailors, at once brave and submissive. Everyone might then have an education that is practical, so that when alone one kept the rules of propriety, and, in intercourse with others, one held the principles of righteousness. I have traced the history back to the earliest times and have watched the progress of every kingdom under the sun, yet I have not known a nation, well administered and upheld by propriety and righteousness, to perish. And I believed what I hoped of the students would not be in vain.



The years 1911 and 1912 saw Imperialism overthrown. Before the nation had time to recover, there came the wars of Restoration, Civil wars continued, and practically every part of China was torn. There was no day in the year that could be called peaceful! There was no one within the boundaries of China, that could earn an easy living! Covetous politicians! Barbarous soldiers! Outlaws! Bandits! Thanks to the militarists, China was again faced with a critical situation!

While the patriots were sorry, indignant but powerless, there took place in Shanghai, on May 30, the beginning of a fresh trouble. The tragedy at Nanking Road led to the general strike of the students, labourers and merchants, to be followed only by other catastrophes in Kowkiang, Hankow and Shameen. At Shameen the number of students killed is greatest, so the question is the most difficult to settle. What the future has in store can never be predicted.

But let us consider the students. Instead of striving for fame in the field of letters, they take to actual fighting. Instead of giving way to reason, they utter the warrior's cry. Instead of following examples in history, instead of basing their arguments upon law and logic, instead of judging their own capability and studying the conditions of their own opponents, they rush forward in hopes of putting down all "unequal treaties" and of taking over the national affairs in their own hands, as if their anger frightens the princes and their words suffice to subdue the universe. I have traced the history, back to the earliest times and have watched the progress of every nation under the sun, yet I have not known a case like this or men like our students. Our students compare themselves with sages. Are they right when they act so foolishly?

The trouble spreads from Shanghai to other parts of China. At first one or two agitators stirred up the students and when the worst came to the worst, left them to die. Others followed, ignorant of the actual situation and propaganding most exaggeratingly. The students seized the opportunity, thinking that by so doing, they could escape school examinations, violate school regulations, call such and such meetings and form so and so societies; or they could, at will, force their headmasters to resign, and cause their teachers to be arrested. As their actions became queerer and queerer, the state of affairs became worst and worst, until, at length, it attained the most disastrous climax of to-day. The other nations stand by, alert with watching eyes. The Government is unable to improve the present condition. Those, dangerous and wicked, who have made use of the students as their instrument and have started the trubble, fail now to put an end to it. Those, selfish and unscrupulous, who take advantage of the crisis at the expense of the students, now add oil to the menacing fire. And so, thousands of students, still in the flower of youth, are being led, step by step, towards the traps as deadly as those laid by Ch'in-ching (1st emperor of Ch'in Dynasty), Pak-hi and Hong-u. Many are at work to destroy the nation, and they are now destroying the poor students, on whom the very soul of China depends. What can we do?

Miss Gertrude Ederle, the seventeen year old American lady champion, started this morning to swim the English Channel.

As soon as the summer vacation ends, the schools I organised shall resume their usual policy of "strictness," increase the hours of working, pay special attention to the occupation of students after school, prevent any outside communications as considered necessary and stop all sorts of political movements conducted by the students. ## DOES IT AGAIN

YET ANOTHER CENTURY.

Hobbs has now beaten the record of 126 centuries in first-class cricket standing to the name of Dr. W. G. Grace and he has also beaten the record of 13 centuries in a season, a feat accomplished by C. B. Fry, Hayward (T.) and Hendren (E.)

Yesterday telegrams were published that J. B. Hobbs, the Surrey and England opening batsmin, had equalled those two records by scoring 101 in the first innings against Somerset at Taunton.

Last night another cable arrived stating that in the second innings Hobbs made another century thereby beating two records which had stood for some years.

By a coincidence Hobbs's scores in both innings were 101, the second time "not out." This, of course, means two separate hundreds in one match; earlier in the season. Hobbs obtained two centuries in one match (for Surrey against Cambridge University) and the only other occasion he has done this was some years ago. Incidents in his career were given at considerable length in last night's "China Mail."

The latest cables, from Reuter, read:—

### London. August 18

At Taunton Hobbs, in the second innings against Somerset, made a hundred not out.

[Note: As will be seen in the results, Hobbs's score was actually 101 not out when play-ceased and victory rested with his side.]

At Taunton, although the crowd was by no means so large as yesterday, there were very enthusiastic scenes.

Spectators surged to the pavilion and cheered continuously for a quarter of an hour and insisted on a speech in which Hobbs said he was happy indeed to have achieved a lifelong ambition in beating Dr. W. G. Grace's record.
