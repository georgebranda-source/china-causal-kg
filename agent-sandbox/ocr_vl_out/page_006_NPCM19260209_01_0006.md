## Hongkong, Tuesday, February 9, 1926

## OUR VOLUNTEERS

His Excellency the Governor did the cause of the local Volunteers a good turn yesterday evening when he carried out an inspection of the Corps and afterward delivered a brief but telling address. The days for Volunteer duty and the Volunteer spirit are far from past. Wherever he may be situated it is the first duty of an able-bodied citizen to offer his services for the defence of his own town or port or Colony. It is one of the finest forms of insurance for the preservation of the community's tranquillity and peace of mind. Volunteering duties, however, cannot be learned at one drill or even in one year. There must be constant training for any undertaking that may arise, otherwise the whole aim of the movement may be destroyed when the call comes for something more than pleasant parades on the drill ground or enjoyable practices at the rifle range. Moreover, in a floating population such as Hongkong it would be non-sensical to expect the strength of the Corps to be maintained year in and year out at a given ratio. There is always a demand for re-cruits, and just because of that demand and because the service is voluntary, the Corps must be made as attractive as possible for our young manhood.

It was only to be expected that His Excellency the Governor would dwell on that side of local Volunteering arising out of the anti-British boycott. It is common knowledge that the mobilisation of the Corps last June had an excellent moral effect, and during the period of "carrying arms" our citizen soldiers earned the gratitude and pratise of the entire community.

To get work well done voluntarily is often the precursor of a request for yet more. His Excellency remarked that he was encouraged to ask for even more service from the Volunteers, in respect of which he promised that

such would come up for public discussion. The first request is in regard to the period of annual training, which both His Excellency and the General Officer commanding are of opinion is too short at present. That may be expected to be the subject of a conference between the authorities and the Chamber of Commerce. There is, and will be for some time, a shortage of staffs in business firms, and it may be argued that a longer period of training not only would be a hardship on firms but at the same time impede the flow of recruits to the Volunteer Corps.

There ought to be more accord with Sir Cecil Clementi on the subject of a Corps of Naval Volunteers. Such a body formed the basis of an article in this paper a few months ago, when a similar movement was talked about in Singapore. This is certainly one direction in which there can be enlisted “additional help from civilians in the defence of this Colony.” His Excellency invites public discussion on these and other schemes; to that invitation it is to be hoped there will be a ready response.

MURDER CHARGE AGAINST

We are approaching the fact that the tongue is mightier than the sword. A League of Nations communique shows that during 1925, 248 treaties, conventions or other international agreements were registered with the League secretariat, making the total so registered 1043. The number is formidable; but out of these documents has come a considerable amount of peace consequent on the straightening of tangled webs of misunderstanding. In the 1925 list are treaties of friendship concluded by Turkey with Austria, Denmark, Sweden and Czecho-Slovakia; a treaty of friendship between Persia and Afghanistan; treaties of conciliation made by Switzerland with Brazil, Italy, Austria, Hungary and Sweden; an arbitration between Uruguay and Venezuela, and a conciliation and arbitration convention by Latvia, Ethiopia, Finland and Poland. The list does not include the Locarno treaties, yet to be registered, nor does it take in the arbitration treaties now being negotiated with various countries by Spain. All this is splendid. It points to the fact that although “hot air” may escape from those charged with high office in a particular country, its possible evils are likely to be offset by the fact that Europe, at any rate, is tired of war. Napoleon suggested that God was on the side of the big battalions. It is now more likely to be that He is on the side of the cause that has justice behind it. And it is in this connection that the League of Nations is more than justifying itself. When America gets to the stage of “injecting itself into the affairs of the Old World,” we may expect greater development. The League is not only interested in political treaties and arbitration, but in matters closely concerned with industrial and economic questions. Soon there is to be an international economic conference and much good is likely to result

INTERPRETER'S EVIDENCE

The hearing of the case in which a Shantung constable stands charged with the murder of an Indian constable was continued before Mr. R. E. Lindsell at the Central Magistracy yesterday.

Mr. T. M. Hazlerigg, Assistant Crown Solicitor, prosecuted. Accused was not legally represented.

An Indian constable stated in evidence that he met the accused in a frightened state on the night of the murder. In reply to questions by witness, accused said: "Your foki has died; I have bullet-

Another witness, Lance Sergeant J. S. Scott, of the Royal Artillery, said that he saw the deceased constable on the ground. The accused in broken English and gestures tried to make out that he had struck the Indian with his truncheon.

An interpreter said that when accused arrived at the charge room he made a statement to him in which he said that the Indian, in trying to relieve him of his truncheon, tore his pocket.

The Indian tried to get hold of his revolver in order to fire at him. Accused saw he was in danger and he turned the revolver away from himself and it went off. The bullet struck the Indian, although he did not intend to shoot him.

Witness added that accused further stated the revolver went off while it was in the holster, the bullet passing through the end of the holster.

The case was adjourned until today.

## ENGAGEMENT

RETURNED GRADUATES TO MARRY.

A COLLEGE ROMANCE.

A dinner was given last evening by Mrs. Lau Siu-cheuk at her house, 121 Caine Road, to a few near relatives, in honour of the engagement of her daughter, Miss Rose, to Dr. S. F. Tan of Tlentain. The betrothal is part of a long romance begun in 1919 when the happy couple first met at Cornell University, Ithaca, New York, from which institution they both graduated.

After taking her B.A. degree in 1922, Miss Lau came back to Hongkong the following year and since then she has been very prominent and active among the younger Chinese residents of the Colony.

Dr. Tan received his B.Chem. degree in 1920 and his Ph.D. degree three years later from Cornell. Upon his return to China in 1924, he was appointed general manager of the Yunshing Motor Co. of Tientsin where he is very well known. Dr. Tan made a short visit to America again last October on a Government mission and returned to Hongkong by the President Jackson on February 1. The wedding will take place early in the spring.

Mrs. Lau Siu-cheuk is the widow of the elder brother of Mr. Lau Kwai-cheuk, the well-known rice merchant and banker.

Hongkong, February 9

With four hundred and thirty on parade, the Hongkong Volunteer Defence Corps marched from Headquarters to Murray parade ground yesterday evening for the annual inspection by the Governor.

Lining the banks and in the assembly in the pavilion on the parade ground were friends and the generally interested public. The Corps was drawn up in companies forming a hollow square, the Artillery, Machine guns, Armoured Car section (together with the mobile column), being at the northern end the Scottish and Reserves at the southern, the Infantry and Engineers at the eastern and the saluting point at the western.

The Corps was called to attention when H.E. the Governor, accompanied by His Excellency the General, Major-General C.C. Luard, alde-de-camps and Fun-jabis officers, entered the ground promptly at 5.45, the royal salute being given with the Corps at the "present" when the saluting point was reached.

H.E. the Governor in the course of his inspection was accompanied from front to rear of each company by the respective platoon and company officers, exchanging

The march past, which followed, was most impressive, particularly at the saluting point where company after company swung past, nothing being heard but the skirt of the pibroch and the drums, the steady tramp of the marchers and the orders for marching acknowledgment when the actual point was reached by the leading files.

With the Corps drawn, up in close order, His Excellency, an impressive figure in levee dress, addressed them as follows, his words being plainly audible to all: — Officers, N.C.O.'s and Men of the Hongkong Volunteer Defence Corps, in addressing you my mind instinctively travels back to the days when I myself stood in your ranks as a private in what was then known as "A Machine Gun Company". A great impetus had at that time been given to volunteering, because owing to disturbances which occurred when the New Territory first came under British rule the Volunteer Defence Corps was mobilized. To-day the call for volunteers is even more imperative because of the anti-British boycott in which, for no fault of curs, we find ourselves involved, and I am glad to see so fine a muster on parade. I congratulate you all on your soldierly bearing and I desire especially to compliment the newly formed Medical Section, a unit of great value to the Hongkong garrison. It is most reassuring to see the civil population volunteering in such numbers and with such efficiency for military duty in connection with the defence of the Colony and I thank you for your services and for the splendid spirit in which they are offered.

## A Naval Corps?

This esprit de corps, as I well remember, has always 'animated the volunteering movement in Hongkong; and it encourages me to ask even more service from you. HE the G.O.C. considers, and I am sure you will all agree, that the present period of your annual training is too short and I propose to see whether suitable arrangements cannot forthwith be made for prolonging it. I am also at the suggestion of H.M.'s Government inquiring whether it would not be possible to form in Hongkong a corps of Naval Volunteers, in addition to the units which already exist. Both these projects will soon come up for public discussion and I trust that you and the whole community will give full support to any well-considered schemes which the military and naval authorities devise for enlisting additional help from civilians in the defence of this Colony. Hongkong is for its size the most wonderful Colony in the Empire. We are very proud of it. We believe in its future and to a man, each in our several posts, we shall unite to protect it from harm. In the famous Roman phrase—Videtimus, ne quid detrimenti respublica capitat.

The Corps then marched back to Headquarters with the Armoured car section and the mobile column leading and before dismissal, Lieut. Col. Bird congratulated them on the excellence of the turn out. The inspection was the best that he had had the honour of commanding.

The Soviet Embassy says that the blood transfusion with which Mr. Krassin (Soviet Ambassador-elect to Great Britain) is being treated is producing the desired effect, but a long rest is necessary.

## AMERICAN STOCKS

After several weeks' slow and gradual advance heavy declines in many stocks have occurred in the stock market to-day as a result of a bold raid by the bears assisted by several adverse factors, including the announcement that the Government was planning to investigate the Ward, Baking merger and the rumours that the proposed merger between the Postum Cereal Corporation and the California Packing Corporation had been declared off. These two stocks crumpled precipitately, the California Packing Corporation's stock falling over twenty points and Ward-Baking's eleven points. The rest of the market declined sympathetically; but signs of recovery appeared during the last hour. Oils and railroads noticeably made a particularly good recovery.
