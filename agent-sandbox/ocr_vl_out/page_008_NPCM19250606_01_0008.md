## FRESH AIR FOR OUR KIDDIES

The Government might do worse than study books on town planning and municipal management, or send a deputation of its unhurried Cadets to study municipal effort in some of the great cities of England. Scarcely a city but has its parks, gardens; or playground committees, supervising these open spaces primarily meant for the young, but eagerly used also by those of an older growth: Municipalities recognise the urgent need of open spaces—provide them and look after them. In Hongkong and Kowloon it can be said that the Government has hitherto provided spaces for adults; it has seemed eager and willing to do so; but for quite young people it has not made provision in any way. Happy Valley is the great Hongkong centre of outdoor life where games are indulged in. But to suggest that there is either room of a welcome for quite young children

ment's curt announcement that it has no site in Kowloon for a children's playground will probably serve a good purpose. It will raise the question anew, and we shall see if Kowloon is really serious in its request and if there is a great need for what has been sought. We have no doubt upon the matter. The "China Mail" has repeatedly asked for playgrounds for the simple reason that they are an imperative need. Staute Square is often given over to football, to say nothing of side streets. Each evening parts of the Praya reclamation are covered with swarms of children making the most of this vast expanse of razed earth. True the use is by Chinese young people, but this does not alter the principle in the least degree. Kowloon adults cannot complain of the Government's provision of playing spaces for them. The supply has been generous in the extreme. From this it must be adduced that the Government is alive to the fact that contentment as an aid to living is a desiredatum and a policy which it is bound to pursue in relation to those who necessarily come under its jurisdiction. This fact makes its seeming indifference to the wants of the quite young-so incomprehensible. Parents anxieties are not to be pampered, and in plain words the appearance of the infant is to be discouraged!

## NEED FOR ACTION

The recent escapeable of a Chinese mechanic who is alleged to have stolen an aeroplane from the reclamation ground at Kowloon Bay where it was housed in a matshed and to have abandoned it after a forced descent, some thirty miles away in the New Territories naturally gives rise to the query "are there any regulations limiting the control of machines in the Colony to qualified men and the possession of machines to agents approved by the Government?" Reference to the local Ordinances gives reassurance on this point for one of the clauses in a special Ordinance introduced so long ago as 1912 covers this very point and gives the Government power to control licences and makes it illegal for anyone to bring aeroplanes into the Colony without permission.

The Ordinance does not go so far as to give the Government power to inspect machines used here with a view to ascertaining whether they are in good flying condition or not but the difficulty here would obviously arise as to who was a competent authority to judge whether a machine was safe as the Government would possibly have to assume a share of the responsibility if a machine is inspected and pronounced correct were to fail. As the matter stands at present passengers make an aerial flight at their own risk and until the number of planes here and the demand for them are sufficient to justify the employment of an expert to examine them it would appear on the face of it that this is the only practical way of dealing with the matter. All the same, it would appear desirable, too, that all local fliers—there are only two licensed at present Mr. Abbott and Mr. Ross—should be made to enter into a bond that their aeroplanes would not be taken from the Colony and disposed of without the knowledge or approval of the Government. In the case of the recent supposed unlawful removal of Mr. Abbott's plane from its shed Mr. Abbott could not, of course, be held responsible, but the danger of traffic in aeroplanes to agents other than those approved by the authorities is too obvious to need emphasis and calls for some action to be taken.

## Test Matches

The rulers of cricket in the Homeland have decreed that the old conservative method of confining test matches to three days shall apply in 1926, except with regard to the fifth test match, which in the event of the fate of the rubber hanging on it, is to be played out. This decision, to say the least, is a little disappointing to the public. Australian men, after travelling 12,000 miles, will feel that they are entitled to have all the test matches played out. The Home point of view is that to do so would dislocate all the cricket arrangements of the year and disorganise the whole complicated system of county cricket, which in the long run is more important for Home cricket than even the Tests themselves. The fact that the Test matches in Australia last season ran to such harrowing lengths probably assisted to confirm the Home opinion in favour of three day tests. There is this to be said about them, that they tend to make the play more lively, and it is a well-known fact that a premium is placed on the brisk forcing batsman when a side is being got together in Australia for an English tour.

## A THOUGHT FOR SUNDAY

### "In Simplicity of Heart Seek Him."

The history of the world is full of instances of the power of a simple faith to rule the most complex and perilous life and in these modern times it is the common experience that the faster and more confusedly things go, the more one feels the need for plain straight-forwardness in religion.

It is this truth which has been behind all the great secessions from the mother-Church down through the ages, and which is still the opposing force to all extreme ritualisms of the present day. The wonder is that the many earnest followers of Christ undoubtedly involved in those movements, have not suspected the hand of their Arch-Enemy behind these prevalent multiform complications in theology. As Christ gave it to us, and as it was conceived by the great saints of old time, the Christian Faith is a grandly simple thing, and every effort to adorn it humanly has but lessened its prime value as a weapon of defence against the world. It is the sheer temper of the steel, not the fine damascening of the blade and bejewelling of the hilt, that the average Christian man-at-arms looks to when his very life depends on the parrying power of his weapon.

# SHREDS AND PATCHES.

Sir William Maule, quoted by His Excellency the

His Excellency the Governor in the Council Chamber this week, was a judge, and master of an ironic humour which he did not object to using in his judicial capacity. His best-remembered utterance in this vein was addressed to a man charged with bigamy, who, on being asked what he had to say why judgment should not be passed upon him replied: "Well, my lord, my wife took up with a hawker, and ran away five years ago, and I have never seen her since, and I married this other woman last winter." The judge said, with appropriate severity of demeanour: "I tell you what you ought to have done, and if you say you did not know I must tell you the law conclusively presumes that you did. You ought to have instructed your attorney to bring an action against the hawker for criminal conversation with your wife. That would have cost you about one hundred pounds. When you had recovered substantial damages against the hawker, you would have instructed your proctor to sue in the ecclesiastical courts for a divorce a mensa atque thoro. That would have cost you two or three hundred pounds more. When you had obtained a divorce a mensa atque thoro, you would have had to appear by counsel before the House of Lords for a divorce a vinculo matrimonii. The Bill might have been opposed in all its stages in both Houses of Parliament; and altogether you would have had to spend about one thousand or twelve hundred pounds. You will probably tell me that you never had a thousand farthings in the world; but, prisoner, that makes no difference. Sitting here as a British judge, it is my duty to tell you that this is not a country in which there is one law for the rich and another for the poor."

Another instance of Judge Maule's irony may be given in the following address to a jury:—“Gentlemen, the learned counsel is perfectly right in his law, there is some evidence upon that point but he is a lawyer, and you are not, and you don't know what he means by some evidence, so I'll tell you. Suppose there was an action on a bill of exchange, and six people swore they saw the defendant accept it, and six others swore they heard him say he should have to pay it, and six others knew him intimately, and swore to his handwriting; and suppose, on the other side, they called a poor old man, who had been at school with the defendant forty years before, and had not seen him since, and he said he rather thought the acceptance was not his writing, why there would be some evidence that it was not, and that's what counsel means in this case.”

### A FALLACY. That Plenty of Space Does Away with the Necessity

There should be 1,000 cubic feet of space for each individual in a room, and in addition to this, 3,000 cubic feet of fresh air should be introduced into the room per hour for each person. For example, if the room be 10 ft. long, 10 ft. wide and 10 ft. high, it contains the right number of cubic feet of air for one person; but it is wrong for him or her to sit or sleep in that room if every door, window, or crevice where fresh air might get in is closed up and luted with indiarubber, felt, sandbags, etc.; for 8,000 cubic feet of fresh air should be passing into the room per hour, and of course, a corresponding quantity of foul air should be passing out through the chimney or the ventilator. If the room be 20 ft. long, 10 ft. wide and 10 ft. high, then it is suitable for occupation by two persons, if 6,000 cubic feet of fresh air be passed in per hour.

If the cubic space individual be less than 1,000 cubic feet, then it is even more necessary to have thorough ventilation; but if the air of a room be changed more than four or five times per hour, a draught will probably be felt.

# A SOLDIER'S AWKWARD QUESTIONS.

Sir Ian Hamilton is a soldier and not a statesman by profession. But he has at any rate one of the qualities of those statesmen who succeed. He has the habit of judging diplomatic actions by the political effects which they are likely or certain to have, and not merely by their value as reliefs for our own feelings or as means for scraping through some little perplexity of the moment, remarks the "Manchester Guardian." The action which he submitted to this kind of scrutiny in his address at Frome was our own Government's failure to keep its treaty promise to evacuate Cologne at the end of last year. It saved our Foreign Office a little trouble with the French Foreign Office, and that was the only real reason for this unlucky breach of faith. Of course a formal pretext had to be put forward, and the one chosen was that Germany had not got on fast enough with her disarmament. But no one took the pretext seriously, and our own Foreign Office took it so far from seriously that it did not even attempt to draw up any specific charges against Germany at the time. So we figure in the eyes of Germans as a nation who breaks promises when it suits her. If the main aim of diplomacy were to let off a nation's emotional steam perhaps this breach of faith might be valued as enabling some

of us to say, "Well, 'anyhow, it will show Germany how" precious little we care whether she thinks us honest or not," or "Well, 'anyhow, it will show Germany who's "master now." There are actually people who say such things without any consciousness of degradation. But Sir Ian Hamilton has the hobby of not liking to act basely. And also he can see beyond his nose. The control of Germany, he sees, is now being fought for by two parties. One of them, the Nationalist party, wants to persuade all Germany that any reliance on the honesty or moderation of England, not to mention France, is hopeless, and that Germany's only future hope lies in the gradual reconstruction of the old German military machine, with a view to a war of revenge. The other wants to persuade Germany that there are limits to what England, if not France, will do in the vindictive lines, and that a British promise, at any rate, is a thing which can be counted upon. Why, then, should we strengthen the German Nationalists? Why give them an apt illustration to make their anti-British speeches convincing? Why make moderate German politicians look foolish? It is odd that it should be left to a famous British General to press these questions. Might they not have occurred to the professional politicians of the Calmese?
