The Spring issue of St. Andrew's Young Men's Club magazine is a bright and interesting production on which those concerned deserve congratulations.

A record snowfall for April is reported from Takayama and other towns in Hida province, Japan, where the depth is said to be over five inches. The snow and cold weather marred the festival of the Hiya Shripe in Takayama.

A plan for harnessing the electrical energy believed to be possessed by the Nitherto: lifeless lake known as the Dead Sea is announced. It properly exploited, it is declared, the lake can produce a maximum of 350,000 horse power.

Mr. Charles Talbot, of the Union Oil Company organisation in Japan with headquarters in Kebe, says that recent flows in California in which millions of dollars worth of oil in tanks was burned would not affect the distribution of the company. "In the least."

Japanese labour leaders are making arrangements for the celebration of May Day, which, according to present plans, will be observed by a gathering in Shix park, and a parade from there to Uyeno park, Tokyo. D'tails of the programme will be arranged at a meeting of representatives of labour organisations which are to participate in the celebration.

Two of the oldest saloons in England are shortly too pass out of existence. The "Two Brewers" saloon at Perry Mill, Catford, near London which was in operation before Columbus discovered America and which has been dispensing drinks ever since is shortly to be demolished to make way for a modern hotel. Here, Dick Turpin, the famous highwayman who was hanged at York in 1739, used frequently to step in for a drink, to the consternation of the "bar-flies." "Ye Older Whittington" near Kinver, Staffordshire, which dates from early in the sixteenth Century and where King Charles stayed in his flight from Worcester, is shortly to go under the auctioneer's hammer.

# AND GENERAL.

The Water Authority announces that a constant supply of water will be turned on in all rider main districts until further notice.

The Yokohama Municipality is to build a five-storeyed steel frame, fire and earthquake building designed for the hotel business.

A cash box containing about $30 was stolen by an armed robber early yesterday morning from a Chinese widow, living at Taipo Old Market. The woman was struck on the head when she made an endeavour to recover her property.

The Union of Chinese Chambers of Commerce, with headquarters in Peking, has submitted a memorandum to the Peking Government urging the immediate opening of formal negotiations with Soviet Russia for redemption of "ruble notes" in possession of the Chinese people.

"I do not believe," says Lord Hugh Cecil "that there is in the whole of Christian history any settled part of the Christian Church which is less remote from the Kingdom of Heaven as sketched in the New Testament than is the Church of England today."

China's capital is one of the few walled cities in the world the gates of which really close every night and remain closed until daylight. The time for opening and closing the city gates has now undergone another change. For some time past they have opened at 5 in the morning and closed at 6 at night. But now they are open at 6:30 a.m. and close at 6 p.m.

Washington policemen are wondering just how one can hit a prisoner and still be courteous about it. In a lecture cautioning policemen to dispense with "hard-boiled" tactics, Edwin B. Hesse, Superintendent of Police, said there was "no excuse for gruffiness or discourtesy" in dealing with law offenders, and added: "Even when a prisoner becomes violent, there is no reason for it. If you must hit him, hit him in courteous way."

The death occurred on March 20, in Osaka, of Mr. P. Paterson Fox, after a serious internal operation. Mr. Paterson Fox was a scholar of singular merit and had previously followed a distinguished teaching career in England, having taught at St. Peter's Court, Broadstairs (patronised by the British Royal Family) and at Uppingham. He went to Japan about a year ago to fill a Government appointment in a school at Shizuoka, but relinquished this in December last to occupy a better one at the Maizuru Naval College, when the first symptoms of his final illness appeared, and prevented his ever fully occupying his new position.

The Home Secretary, Sir William Joynson Hicks, M.P., has informed the Lord's Day Observance Society who wrote to the Home Office regarding the battling on girls' races on Sunday mornings at the North Walthamstow Athletic Grounds that these races have now been discontinued.

Aerial tours of Great Britain are to be offered American visitors this summer. An English aircraft company is building a fleet of four and eight-seater planes to meet the American demand for speed. Three, four and seven-day tours of England, Scotland, Ireland and Wales, with landings at the principal places of interest are to be offered. Some of the machines will be seaplanes, equipped to descend on lakes and rivers, designed especially for tours of the lake country and the coastal towns.

Mr. Frank Booth, of Tokyo, representative in Japan for the Baker-Perkins Company, suffered a broken leg in the driveway of the Imperial Hotel, "Tokyo" when his lower limb struck a projection as he was driving out on his motorcycle. Mr. Booth was leaving the driveway at low speed and placed a foot on the ground to aid in guiding the machine. The motorcycle was driven too close to the wall and his limb struck the brick. The bone snapped. Mr. Booth was taken to hospital and is resting easily.

# SOCIAL AND PERSONAL.

Mr. Harry Tobias, of N. Lazarus, is due in the Colony next month on his return to Shanghai from Home leave.

A, tempest, in a teapot has been raised in Stratford-on-Avon. The Town Councillors, some of whom are also trustees of the Shakespeare Memorial, have changed the historic if not exactly magnificent names of some of the streets. For instance, Skinner's lane, leading to Anne Hathaway's Cottage, is to be Anne Hathaway Road; Back Lane is to be dressed up as Grove Road and Swine Street is to be changed into Ely Street. A protesting letter to the "Times" from one Shakespeare trustee will probably be followed by others.

The engagement is announced of Dr. Herbert Manchester, pastor of Yokohama Union Church, to Miss Mary B. Gersbacher of Syracuse, N. Y.

"Rose Marie," the first production which has ever run for a year at Drury Lane Theatre, has celebrated its birthday. The theatre was opened in 1663, so "Rose Marie" establishes a record for 263 years. Over a million people have seen the play in London and the provinces, and a wealthy Indian merchant, who has now returned to the East, bought a box for every Wednesday matinee of his six months stay in London. So far the members of the company have worn 1,400 costumes, 1,600 pairs of shoes, and 2,000 pairs of stockings.

Capt. Daniel J. Keating, M.C., who has been promoted to that rank in the East Yorkshire Regiment, is with the 1st Battalion at Tientsin, North China. He was commissioned in the Leinster Regiment in April, 1916, and in 1922 was transferred to the Sappers on the disbandment of the Liensters. Capt. Keating was mentioned in despatches in 1917, was given the Military Cross in recognition of his conspicuous courage in action, and also wears the two-war medals.

Mrs. Douglas Robinson, a sister of the late President Roosevelt, underwent an operation, seeking restoration of her sight. She has been blind for some time in one eye, and recently discovered that the sight of the other had dimmed to a point where she could scarcely see.

King Alfonso was returning from a drive on the outskirts of the city, when his car was pulled up for a minor adjustment to be made. At that moment a tired-looking workman, walking citywards, not recognising the King, asked for a lift, as he was overcoming with fatigue. Dr. Henry Coward, addressing the members of the Leeds Rotary Club, remarked that only those who had been brought up in a musical atmosphere knew how it sweetened life. "A man's business is more likely to go west if he shuts music out of his life," said Dr. Coward. "The subtle, stimulating, wit-sharpening, spirrit-rousing, nerve-soothing influence of music is Nature's pleasant medicine. The most thriving business districts of Britain are the most musical. In sleepy, dead-alive places, music is always at a low ebb. That is not an accident; Musical people are the brightest, and most alert."

Bolton Wanderers who have won the English Cup final are popularly known as "the Trotters."

Mr. John Fitzpatrick, of Eckett Street, Bermondsey, has just celebrated his 100th birthday. He has lived in Bermondsey all his life. His chief pastime is walking, and he attributes his long life to hard work.

Mr. Mitsugu Hara, adopted son of the late Premier Hara, accompanied by his wife will soon leave Japan to study in England again. He recently married the daughter of the late Dr. Tokichi Masao, former Japanese Minister to Siam. Mr. Hara will continue to write from England for a literary magazine started in Tokyo by his friends. Mrs. Hara will continue her study of oil painting.

The announcement of the engagement of Miss Diana de Warzee, the daughter of the Belgian Minister to China, and Baroness de Warzee d'Hermalle, has created much interest in Tokyo where the family lived for nine years. M: de Warzee was Secretary and for a time Charged Affairs of the Belgian Embassy. After leaving Japan about five years ago, Baron de Warzee served as Belgian Minister at Havana, Cuba, and afterward as Charge d'Affaires at Washington. He inherited the family title of Baron after leaving Japan.

Mr. Hou Tsung-line, a Chinese medical student, has won a doctor's degree at the age of 23. As a special honour, the degree will be conferred by the Japanese Minister of Education. He recently graduated, from the South Manchurian University medical college and has been made assistant professor at that college. His degrees was won by his research work into the blood circulation and amount of oxygen needed in the brain of a dog, which was finally approved only a few days ago by a board of professors of the Medical Faculty of Kyoto University.
