## CABLES WITH A MORAL

Amongst the numerous cables regarding the general strike being received here from Home it is to be hoped that the loyal Chinese Press will deem it fit and proper to publish those portions showing how the Government is dealing with agitators and rioters and all who stand in the way of the people being supplied with food stuffs and other necessaries of life. They should also note, for the information of their readers, that convictions were obtained at various Police Courts in England yesterday for impeding the Police and circulating false news, whilst a Trade Union local Secretary was sentenced to three months' imprisonment for urging the workers not to join the special constabulary.

The moral is there if it can properly be brought home to those who may be most in need of it, even although the possibility of any fresh outbreak of industrial trouble in this Colony may seem extremely remote. British Governments, Home and overseas, may appear sentimental and soft at times when the "mailed fist" might bring more immediate practical results, but once the dogs of industrial war are set loose by the agitators and extremists, these same Governments have the happy knack of bringing into being powerful machinery for the protection of the populace and for the safeguarding of the supplies of food and necessaries of life. With that machinery they will brook no interference. The strikers may waver or the strikers may rally, but that machinery remains unimpaired so long as the crisis exists.

## THEFTS

Several thefts from dwellings were reported to have occurred yesterday.

The occupants of No. 23, Hill Road, third floor (western district) lost clothing to the value of $126.60. Access was gained by creeping along the verandah of an adjoining house which was vacant.

Property to the value of $64 was taken at each of two places—No. 260 Queen's Road West and No. 164 Shanghai Street, Yaumath. The loss at the latter place included an electric massage machine.

At No. 54, Main Street, Shaukwan, thieves took clothing and jewellery to the value of $1,87.

Bank Note Theft.

A Chinese servant girl was charged with stealing a $500 Bank note from a widow living at No. 32, Third Street.

It appeared that the complainant's daughter opened the safe to place a bangle and forgot to lock it. One of seven $500 notes was later found to be missing. The servant girl admitted that she stole the money at the request of a pork dealer at the market who promised to release her from a woman who acted as her mistress.

The girl repeated in Court that she handed the money to the pork dealer. This was denied by the man. There being no witness to support the girl's story, she was sent to prison for six weeks.

Acting on information given by a lodger, a detective arrested a Chinese for stealing two canvas awnings from a shop at Connaught Road West.

The arrested man gave an accomplice away and he was also secured at Centre Street.

Both men were sentenced to undergo three weeks' hard labour on a charge of larceny of the awnings.

## SOLDIERS' THREE

Captain Gee, a distinguished V.C. and M.P. has expressed his opinion that "Mr. Kipling's "Soldiers Three" is the most cowardly and dastardly book ever written on the British Tommy Atkins." A correspondent writes to an English paper that Captain Gee does not know what he is talking about, for he certainly can have neither knowledge nor experience of the particular type of soldier described by Mr. Kipling in that book. The papers, later published under the title of "Soldiers Three," first appeared in the "Civil and Military Gazette"—the Lahore paper—in 1886, at a date when Captain Gee was only ten years old, and he did not enter the Army, according to "Who's Who," until 1898. "Soldiers Three" was at the time generally accepted by the majority of men serving in India in the British Army of that day, as a very fair and accurate presentment of a type which was then dying out and has long since entirely disappeared, possibly to the advantage of the Army—possibly not.
