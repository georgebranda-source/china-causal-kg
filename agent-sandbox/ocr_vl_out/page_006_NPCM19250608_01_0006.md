## Hongkong, Monday, June 8, 1925

## SHANGHAPS - CRISIS

With the despatch from Peking of a Diplomatic Mission to Shanghai to examine the position on the spot, hopes are entertained of at least a temporary lull in the disturbances. The beginning of "discussions" always is regarded as a hopeful sign by those in the wrong. They set out to demand the impossible, in the expectation of exacting some concession which never would have been dreamt of before.

The only thing that may tend to upset an optimistic view of the position is the likelihood of outbreaks elsewhere throughout the country strengthening the irresponsible students in Shanghai. In that event, even the arrival on the scene of the Diplomatic Mission from Peking may not be attended by immediate beneficial results. So long as the Chinese Foreign Office takes for granted that the Police in the International Concessions are culpable in firing upon “unarmed students”—without pausing to enquire into the nature of the attacks upon the Police—half a dozen Diplomatic Missions will accomplish little. There is a time for everything, and the time for an enquiry into the part played by the Police has not yet arrived.

It is obvious that the duty of the Chinese Government is to influence the students to return to their classes and the strikers to return to their work. Until that is done it cannot be expected that the foreigners will make the first move toward either compromise or settlement. To weaken in this respect would merely be the signal for more serious disturbances not

only in Shanghai, but right throughout the great cities. It is evident that students outside of Shanghai are only too ready and willing to seize the filmsiest pretext for breaking out. Incidents at Chinkiang, Hankow, and Peking itself during the week-end amply confirm this. The Diplomatic Body will be the first to welcome the fullest possible impartial enquiry into the Shanghai affair—but the students must first cease from their reckless actions.

## A Plum

The Foreign Secretary describes the Governorship of Hongkong as a plum—something rich, ripe and juicy. From his speech made before visiting American and Canadian doctors presumably interested in tropical disease, it is to be assumed that he ascribes this to the advance in research devoted to tropical diseases which has evidently made the Colony less a fever spot than it once was. Hongkong has become a plum from reasons other than those which have to do with health. Its geographical position is not the least of these. As a distributing centre its influence has never been challenged, whilst its place in the industrial world is just being made evident. It is trade—and the problems connected with trade—which has made Hongkong a plum. Secondly is its influence in matters diplomatic, although this is not often seen. Trade has brought the British and Chinese into contact as nothing else has or could. With it, has followed British traits of character, customs and ideas in the arts generally, which have had their own particular influence to be seen in such institutions as the Hongkong University and the effort to give the same facilities in education to Chinese girls as those given to boys. All these and more, have helped to make Hongkong such an abiding influence, and the Governorship such an important office in the Colonial service. This being so there may be justification in taking time to fill the office whenever vacancy occurs.

The optimists should begin fine spirits these days. Whilst the pessimists in the language of Parliament "have it" the opposite side should see much in the present situation of the world that calls for the practice of optimism. There is scarcely a spot but has its own peculiar troubles, accentuated or minimised according to the quantity and quality of the two forces we have indicated. Europe still seethes with that peculiar manifestation common to bombs and explosions. It is a pleasure to note that the attempt on the lives of the rulers of Spain has been frustrated. Optimists score in this case; and they can well point to the exercise of patience in the deliberations between various countries which is surely soon to reap a rich reward.

## Unrest

In the Far East—in China particularly—the optimist is tried to the uttermost. Whilst the present situation is indeed bad, it might indeed be much worse. It will be if the forces of optimism are not predominant and exercised with caution. The optimist—if he is true to his cult—works towards an end that helps to make pessimists into optimists. He will see in present conditions forces of progress and enlightenment, temporarily submerged. In the words of a well-known melodrama they have taken the wrong turning. The forces are all right. They are suffering from attacks of astigmatism; or what is really the case, from bad management—bad leadership. It has been so from the beginning of time. If the optimist cannot condone neither will he condemn. He will meet the hasty innumendo with calm and solid fact. If he deplores soap-box dogmatism he will show by his own manner that he does so. And if violence becomes necessary, it will only be adopted when all other things have failed. Whilst the generality of human nature is the same the world over, different angles of view obtain with different peoples. Recognising this elementary fact it should not be difficult, when sauer counsels prevail, to get together and reason on the matters, which have caused a temporary hatred and a dislocation of human understanding. This is not to suggest weakening of the attempt to resist wrong and to uphold right principles. On the contrary. At the same time, no contention, or accusation, which is being made by those representing the forces of disorder in the north, should be allowed to go without it is first refuted by convincing fact. In this way will the dawn of intelligence come and those in error see wherein they have failed.

## Mimic Welfare

The most important stage of the American naval manœuvres has been passed. The result, it is stated, has demonstrated the possibility of a foreign fleet, equal to that engaged in this mimic war, capturing Oahu, the principal island of Hawaii unless this state-legal naval base is more effectively defended. Situated as these island possessions of the United States are at the very cross roads of the mid-Pacific, and providing in that wide ocean, as they would in the dread event of war, a base from which a strong naval Power could prosecute successfully raids along the entire western coast of America, the naval authorities of that country are concerned that they should be made impossible.

The defensive weakness disclosed by the recent operations, mainly, it appears, consists in a shortage of air craft, though it is pointed out that both the infantry and artillery arms of the local garriors would also require to be augmented. The anxiety displayed over the position disclosed by the recent counterfeit invasion does not, unfortunately, suggest that the existence of the Three Powers Washington Pact is quite as re-assuring as the peace loving world had anticipated.
