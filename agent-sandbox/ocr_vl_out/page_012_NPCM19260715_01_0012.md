### "BRITISH AID."

REQUIRED TO SUPPRESS BOOT-LEGGING.

AMERICAN DELEGATION.

Rugby, July 14.

An American delegation has arrived in London to discuss suggestions laid down by the British Government in its memorandum to the State Department of March 27, 1926, regarding the administrative measures that might be taken by the British authorities in order to assist the United States Government in the prevention of liquor smuggling from sea.

The delegation consists of General Lincoln Andrews, Assistant Secretary of the Treasury, Mr. Valhance, Assistant to the Solicitor of the Department of State, Rear-Admiral Billard, Commandant of the Coast Guard Treasury Department, and Mr. Arthur Henderson, Special Assistant to the Attorney-General. It will meet at the Foreign Office tomorrow representatives of the Board of Trade, the Admiralty, the Colonial Office, the Customs Administration, and the Foreign Office.

In regard to these discussions it should be understood that there is no question of amending the existing international agreement regarding liquor smuggling. The sole object of the conference is to see what can be done by the British authorities in an administrative way to make smuggling more difficult. It has, for instance, been suggested to the United States that permission might be granted to special American revenue cutters to enter British territorial waters in the Bahamas under certain conditions. The possibility has also been suggested of taking steps to make more difficult the transfer to the British flag of vessels intended for the smuggling trade.

Another of the British suggestions concerns the possibility of rendering it more difficult to make false declarations as to the ultimate destination of liquor cargoes.

These suggestions were contained in the British memorandum of March 27, in which it was proposed that the United States might send representatives to London to learn from the British authorities exactly what they can do and see how far the American authorities can co-operate with them by giving them information and other forms of assistance.—British Wireless Service.

## ORIENTAL LABOUR

IN' JAPAN, CHINA, AND INDIA.

CONFERENCE MOOTED.

## London, July 14

In the House of, Common a suggestion for a conference between Japan, China, and India to discuss labour conditions on the lines of the recent European Conference was made in the form of a question by Mr. Robert Young (Labourite, Newtown) in alluding to statements at the last International Labour Conference regarding the superiority of conditions in India as compared with China and Japan.

Earl Winterton; Under-Secretary of State for India, promised to submit the suggestion to the Government of India.—Reuter.

## SMYRNA PLOT

## FIFTEEN MEN SENTENCED TO DEATH

Constantinople, July 13.

The Tribunal have condemned to death fifteen of the accused in the Smyrna plot.

Three other accused are to be tried at Angora. The remainder were acquitted and liberated. The condemned men will be hanged at dawn to-morrow.—Reuter.

[An attempt was to have been made on the life of Mustapha Kemal Pashn, the Turkish Premier at Smyrna, but just before his arrival the Police discovered the conspiracy.]

## FIRE AT SEA

## LOSS OF A FRENCH STEAMER

BOUND FOR CHINA.

mail "boat" Fontainebleau, bound for Ceylon, the Straits Settlements, "China," and Japan, has been destroyed by fire.

The passengers and crew, and a small part of the cargo have been saved:

The mail sacks have been lost.—Reuter:

## Hongkong Passengers?

The "Fontainbleau" is one of a class of three Messageries Maritimes vessel; the other being the "Chantilly" and the "Compeigne," built in 1023.

It was specially designed for the Indo-China (Marseilles to Haiphong) run but it has been twice in Hongkong already, the last occasion being in February of this year.

A comparatively large ship, the “Fontainbleau” has the following dimensions: displacement 16,000 tons, 11,000 tons gross, 6,900 horse-power, 497 feet long, breadth 59 feet. Passenger accommodation: 1st class 120, 2nd 150, 3rd 80.

## TO MEET BISHOPS

DEPARTURE IN INDUSTRIAL DISPUTES.

MINERS' LEADERS ACCEPT.

Rugby, July 14.

Some interest has been created by the news that this evening representatives of the Miners' Federation will meet several Bishops and heads of the "Free Churches. at the offices of the Industrial Artisan Fellowship.

An official of this body states: ___

"Our fellowship is desirous of doing anything that it can toward helping in the mining dispute. Some days ago we had a meeting with the representatives of the coal owners, at which they explained their point of view. This meeting with the miners' leaders is a companion meeting to that. We feel that the least we can do is to hear the difficulties of both sides. What the next step will be after we are in possession of the facts as both sides put them it is not yet possible to say."—British Wireless Service.

## GLOBE CIRCLERS

WELLS AND EVANS RREAK THE RECORD.

GREETED BY MEARS.

## New York, July 14

The globe, girdlers, Wells and Evans, have arrived at Mitchellfield, New York, completing the world tour in 28½ days and beating the previous record of 35 days without aeroplanes.

Evans said that if the aeroplane schedule across the American Continent had worked better the trip would have been shorter. They had more trouble in that respect than in any place in Europe.

The first man to greet the globe trotters was John Mears, whose record they had broken.—Reuter's American Service.

## BROADCASTING

## IMPORTANT REFORMS AT HOME

ACCEPTED BY GOVERNMENT.

London, July 14.

In the House of Commons, Sir W. Mitchell-Thomson, Postmaster-General, announced his acceptance of the recommendation of the Broadcasting Committee and will transfer the present organisation on December 31 to a Corporation to be established by Royal Charter and holding the Postmaster-General's licence.

It will be invested, according to the Committee's recommendation, with the maximum freedom that Parliament is prepared to concede.—Reuter.

[A cable of March 5 announced:—A most complete scheme for reorganising the management of broadcasting services in Great Britain is outlined in the official report of the Broadcasting Committee appointed to decide the nature of the administration after the expiry of the present Company's licence on December 31. It recommends that broadcasting be made a public service; that a Corporation consisting of from five to seven nominees chosen by the Crown be appointed to take over the staff contracts, and apparatus of the existing Company; that the Commissioners be persons of judgment and independence and empowered to consult appropriate societies, organisations, and advisory committees to ensure due consideration of all phases of broadcasting; that generous provision be made for expejiment and research; that the Corporation operate the licence from the Postmaster-General for at least ten years; and that the Commissioners be invested with the maximum freedom, consistent with Parliamentary control, through the spokesmanship of the Postmaster-General.]

## JULY 14

## GREAT MILITARY REVIEW IN PARIS

The military review on the occasion of the fourteenth of July celebrations was more significant than usual, being in the nature of a thanksgiving for the completion of the Syrian and Moroccan campaigns.

Troops, after the march past of the brilliant gathering of Marshals, Ministers, and Ambassadors to the Arc de Triomphe, went down the Champs Elysees which was packed with spectators.

Aeroplanes wheeled above. An unusual number of Colonial troops participated, some fresh from Morocco and also the Sultan of Morocco's Black Bodyguard, and among the spectators were the Sultan and the Spanish General Primo de Rivera. As the troops passed there were hostile cries, mingled with cheers, and followed by scuffles between the Communists and Patriots, on which the Police intervened. Twenty arrests were made including the Municipal Councillor Joly.—Reuter.
