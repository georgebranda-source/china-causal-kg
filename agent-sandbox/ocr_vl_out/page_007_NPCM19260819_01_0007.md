## COAL STOPPAGE

IS THE END IN SIGHT AT LAST?

## HOURS THE CRUX

## Rugby, Aug: 18

The coal negotiations will be opened to-morrow, Mr. A. J. Cook, the Miners' Secretary, to-day addressed a letter to the Mine Owners' Association, asking that a meeting should be arranged between the Executive Committees of the two sides, and this has been fixed for to-morrow afternoon.

The Government was notified of this development, but, no request has yet been made for its participation. If the miners and owners require the assistance of an independent Chairman for their deliberations, the Government will be willing to appoint one.

The Prime Minister was present to-day at a meeting of the Cabinet Committee which has dealt with the dispute.

The latest development gives general satisfaction, and the belief is strong that an end of the stoppage is within sight.

It is understood that the only restrictions on the negotiations imposed by the miners' delegates on their Executive Committee is that there shall be a national as opposed to district settlement.

## Question of Hours

The crux of the negotiations will probably be the question of hours. The men's representatives are likely to maintain as long as is possible their resistance to an increase of the seven-hours working day, the extension of which, until the coal industry is once again upon its feet, is regarded by the owners as economically indispensable.

Elaborate tables have been prepared by the owners comparing the wages that can be paid to the the various grades of men employed in pits for seven and eight hours' work, respectively, and these are likely to form the subject of much technical discussion between the two sides.—British Wireless Service.

## Bögey of Taxation

Speaking here, Mr. Winston Churchill, Chancellor of the Exchequer reviewed the coal dispute. He said he had every hope in the belief that lasting and honourable settlement would be realised.

The possibility of reducing taxation had been postponed. "Indeed, my whole effort will be directed to avoiding increasing taxation on over-pressed industries or necessaries for popular consumption. Even if all the men on the coal fields go to work to-morrow it will only be by the very greatest exertions and contrivance that additional taxation can be avoided.

## SMUTS REAPPEARS

## BREAKS FOUR YEARS' SILENCE

JohnInnesburg, August 18.

Addressing a crowded meeting in the Town Hall yesterday evening, which was the first occasion in which he had delivered a speech in public at a political gathering in Johannesburg since the strike of 1922, General Smuts was frequently interrupted by a small and rowdy section, which continually shouted, hooted and waved red flags. There were many free fights and a number of the most prominent rowdies were ejected.

General Smuts, referring to the flag and secession questions, said that owing to the different voices with which Ministers spoke, nobody knew where they stood with regard to "the great question in which the country was interested. The Prime Minister was keeping quiet, while the country was very anxious to hear General Hertzog's views at the Imperial Conference, as to what policy he would advocate there. They wanted to know whether General Hertzog would demand the declaration of South Africa's independence, which he (Hertzog) had favoured in the course of a speech at Stellenbosch.

General Smuts said that such a declaration would mean the break-up of the Empire. They wanted to know General Hertzog's policy, for the policy he had pursued had not been in the interest of South Africa.

Interruptions became so serious that a Labour leader appealed for order, but ineffectually, and the uproar was so tremendous that the Chairman advised General Smuts to speak later, when a hearing would be ensured. A vote of thanks was carried with denfening cheers. There were more free fights while the hall was emptying.—Reuter.

## BLACK STAR

## MOSCOW COMMUNISTS' THREATENED

A SECRET SOCIETY.

An interesting article appeared in yesterday's Russian paper, the Shanghai Zarya regarding the formation of a new Society in Moscow known as the Brethren of the Black Star, which has been established for the purpose of eradicating Communist officials from office.

### Shanghai, Aug. 18

The article states that lately hundreds of mysterious letters, signed by the organisation and bearing a black star on the letterhead have been received by practically all the prominent Communist officials, threatening murder unless they resign from office and hand over a certain amount of money.

It is further stated that "the Society has carried out the threats in many instances. Already several high Communists officials have been found murdered in various parts of Moscow. In each instance a black star was stuck on the forehead of the murdered man.—Reuter.

## YEN'S CLIMB

New York, August 18.

The yen's courageous climb towards parity is being watched sympathetically throughout the world, especially in New York, where experts ascribe its advance, firstly, to improved conditions in Japan as shown in recent trade reports, coupled with the fact that exports are now understood to exceed imports necessitating the sale of dollars and the purchase of yen; secondly, to a widespread belief that the Japanese Government intends to take advantage of the opening of the export season to restore the gold standard; thirdly, to recent heavy purchases of yen by Indian and Chinese speculators, alarmed at the sensational collapse of the price of silver and the result of the expected abolition of silver currency in India; fourthly, to rumoured heavy buying during the last few weeks in all foreign centres, by Yokohama Special Bank, as the Bank of Japan's official agent; fifthly, to belated covering by "shorts" who sold heavily at the time of the earthquake.—Reuter's American Service.

## PROMISE

AMERICA TO WITHDRAW FROM PHILIPPINES.

ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT

Senator Osmena, a prominent Filipino leader, speaking at a luncheon in honour of Colonel Carmi-Thompson, who is the personal representative of President Coolidge investigating conditions on the Islands, declared that President Coolidge had promised that the United States would withdraw from the Philippines as soon as the natural resources of the Islands were developed.

Senator Osmena added that the promise was made when he visited the United States, President Coolidge saying:

“We will withdraw, but we do not intend to leave you with limited resources. We desire first to assist you in promoting economic development. We cannot perform the work alone and we want the Filipinos to participate.” —Reuter.

## STRANGE STORY

ALLEGED SALE OF LEGATION

SITE

CHINESE INDIGNATION.

Reports that the staff of the Chinese Legation in Tokyo has secretly sold the site of the legation to the Japanese Imperial Library have stirred 184 members of the old Chinese Parliament to issue a lengthy circular telegram to the country denouncing the alleged deal and calling upon the Government to order the entire Legation home and try them for their alleged betrayal of national interests.

The circular says that the Chinese legation in Tokyo is situated in the most prosperous district of the Japanese capital. When the site was acquired by the then Imperial Government, China paid more than $1,000,000 for it, but since the earthquake of 1923 the land has risen in value to such an extent that today it is at least worth more than $5,000,000. The M.P.'s then go on to say that the staff has no right to dispose of the property and that if their pay has been in arrears it is the business of the Walchiaopu to raise funds for them. For them to sell "the land to the Japanese Library is not only a greve reflection on the Chinese Government but will make both the Chinese people and China an international laughing stock.

The telegram concludes by urging the Government to recall the legation staff and send a competent man to Japan to negotiate for the rescinding of the agreement alleged to have been entered into between the legation and the Japanese Library authorities.

## SOLICITOR'S DUTY

SEQUEL TO TRIAL JUDGE'S CRITICISMS.

COURT OF APPEALS FINDING

### Singapore, Aug. 7

In giving judgment yesterday morning in the appeal brought by Shaik Allie bin Omar bin Abdullah Bhashuan against a decision of Mr. Justice Deane, the members of the Singapore Court of Appeal expressed their views upon the extent of a solicitor's duty in advising his client. "In the original action plaintiff, Inche Noriah Binte Mohamed Tahir, claimed that a deed of gift dated April 1922, whereby she transferred to Shaik Allie certain property in Singapore valued at $22,000, should be set aside on the ground that it had been executed under the undue influence of the defendant. In giving judgment for the plaintiff Mr. Justice Deane held that she did not have proper independent legal advice and commented strongly on the conduct of Mr. James Aitken, who acted as her solicitor. In their judgments the Chief Justice (Sir William Murison) and Mr. Justice Brown disagreed with the strictures passed upon Mr. Aitken by the trial judge, but Mr. Justice McCabe Reny considered that Mr. Aitken should have gone further than he did and have definitely advised the plaintiff to make the gift or not. The Chief Justice, with whom Mr. Justice Brown concurred, said he was inclined to conclude, although the matter was not free from doubt, that there was in fact no presumption of undue influence, but, even if a confidential relationship existed between the parties to the appeal, the appellant had discharged the onus upon him to "shew that the gift was not prompted by undue influence exercised by the appellant over the respondent; and that the respondent had adequate independent advice and well knew the nature and effect of the deed which she executed.

After referring to the evidence given during the case the Chief Justice said it appeared that Mr. Aitken thoroughly explained to the respondent the effect of the deed and was satisfied that she was mentally very capable, did understand the effect of it, and wished to present the property to the appellant because he had been kind to her. What Mr. Aitken did not do was to ask her if she had enough to live upon apart from the property, and to advise her whether she ought to make the gift or not. It was said that the onus in regard to the presumption of undue influence was still not discharged because Mr. Aitken did not enquire as to her other means and whether she could afford to make the gift; for these considerations were, it was argued, an essential factor in the giving of independent advice. His lordship then repeated that part of the judgment of Fletcher Moulton L. J. in re Coomber, in which he said a solicitor's duty on this point was clearly dealt with. The whole trend and obvious intention of Moulton L. J.'s judgment on this point, he continued, was to eliminate the idea that approval, as distinct from advice, is necessary.... No doubt solicitors often went beyond what was necessary and gave approval or disapproval, as, in fact, Mr. Campbell said he would have done. But they were only concerned here with the limits of a solicitor's duty; not with the extent to which friendly interest could and often did go.

## "Every Thing the Law Required"

### of Him."

The Chief Justice said he thought that the learned judge below, in holding that the respondent was not properly advised because Mr. Aitken did not go into the question of her means and advise her that she ought not to make the gift, had gone outside the principles laid down by Moulton L. J. "It appears to me," his lordship said, "that all the advice which the law requires was given and that the omission to go further and give or withhold approval from the point of view of the assignor's material interests is not a sufficient ground for saying that the assignor did not have independent advice. I ought to add that in view of the remarks by Moulton L. J. in re Coomber concerning the duties incumbent upon a solicitor advising a client in regard to a voluntary gift, I find myself unable to endorse the strictures which the learned trial judge has passed upon the conduct of Mr. Aitken. In my opinion Mr. Aitken did everything which the law required of him in the position in which he was placed."

### Mr. Justice Brown Agrees

Mr. Justice Brown's judgment was also read. It was necessary, he said, to make some reference to what he felt bound to characterise as an unfortunate incident in the case, namely the severe criticism passed by the learned judge upon the conduct of Mr. Aitken. "I am unable to see the justification for the very strong expressions that were used," he said. "It is a tenable view that Mr. Aitken, when he was called in to prepare the conveyance, did not make all the onquiries that he might have made, but that is a very different thing from saying that the plaintiff really had no independent legal advice; and Mr. Parsons, who appeared for the plaintiff on the appeal, at once disclaimed any imputation of Mr. Aitken's honesty in the matter. In point of fact, Mr. Aitken did exactly what, according to the opinion expressed by Fletcher Moulton L. J. in re Coomber, it was his duty to do; he explained to his client the nature of the proposed transaction and the consequences of it, but he did not go further and advise her whether the step she was taking was a wise step or not. The misunderstanding seems to have arisen from the fact that the plaintiff was regarded by the judge as a person requiring special protection. It seems to me that if the plaintiff was to be treated as in need of protection she had to prove that her mental powers had decayed to such an extent that she was in no better case than a young person just of age, but, as already mentioned, the evidence is all the other way."



### Mr. Justice McCabe Reay's Difficulties

Mr. Justice McCabe Reay, in the course of his judgment, said: "I have had great difficulty in coming to a decision, for the reason, speaking generally, that I think it very dangerous to apply the rules of English law regarding transactions between educated English people, either inter se or with their solicitors, to transactions between an old illiterate Malay woman and a capable native man of business on the one hand, and a transaction between such a woman and an English solicitor. In view of the whole of the evidence, which she was clearly the great trust imposed by the respondent in the appellant, I am of opinion that such a confidential relationship existed between them as would justify the Court in granting relief unless it appeared that respondent had independent advice. It is here that the great difficulty presents itself. The learned trial judge, who had all the parties before him, found that her solicitor failed in his duty to her and did not go far enough in the advice her gave her. What he said to her was, in effect, what was held in re Coomber to have been sufficient as between Mrs. Coomber and her solicitor, but it is precisely here that doubt arises in my mind. Can it be said that what was sufficient in England was necessarily sufficient between an English solicitor in this country and an old illiterate Malay woman? The cases are, on the surface, very similar. Mrs. Coomber's gift was to her son; the appellant is related to the respondent. But the fundamental difference remains and, in view of the age, nationality, lack of education and general state of dependence of the respondent I think it was not sufficient for her solicitor merely to ask whether she knew and understood what she was doing. He should, in my opinion, have gone further; he should have treated her as if she was a young person in England and definitely advised her to make the gift or not. I agree, however, that no moral blame whatever attaches to Mr. Aitken for what was at most an honest error of judgment, and indeed, I do not think the learned trial judge intended his strictures to be read in that sense, although he certainly used some expressions which are open to this construction.

The Chief Justice remarked that he did not quite follow Mr. Justice McCabe "Reay's judgment; it seemed to differ from theirs.

Mr. Roland Bradell (for the appellant) said it did not come to any conclusion. If it was a dissenting judgment, however, it did not affect the decision of the Court. In the report it would probably be treated as a dissenting judgment.

Mr. Dudley Parsons, for respondent, applied for and was granted a stay of execution for four months. He stated that they wished to apply to counsel at home for an opinion; it depended on that opinion whether the matter would proceed further or not.—"S. F. P."
