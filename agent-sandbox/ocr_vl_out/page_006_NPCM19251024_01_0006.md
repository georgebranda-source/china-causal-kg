THE NEW TERRITORIES

In view of the references by H.E. the Governor and Unofficial members of the Legislative Council to New Territories possibilities in " many cases latent, it is interesting to recall the findings of the Economic Resources Committee which was appointed by the Government, in 1921 with particular relation to these districtists. Live stock breeding prospects formed the subject of considerable investigation and the advice of experts was sought with a view to ascertaining what particular breeds of cattle might thrive and what methods might best be adapted to local peculiarities of soil and climate. The backward condition of livestock generally in the New Territories was considered due largely to the indifference, or the part of the farmers to the whole question and to the ignorance which bred that indifference. Even when the farmer had heard of better things, he had/no knowledge as to how these might be achieved. He lived in an isolated world and the successes in money making which he heard of were in commerce rather than in agriculture. Another industry which was the subject of considerable investigation was that of "sericulture" and here's a note of optimism was struck in view of the success which had attended the experimental garden of Lady Ho Tung; this, it was thought, might well form the basis of further experiments in the future. It was thought that with the encouragement of settlers in the New Territories, the industry could be made a thriving one and it was recommended that sufficient attractions should be offered to natives to take up allotments for the cultivation of mulberry bushes, that facilities should be given expert silk worm rearers to take up allotments contiguous with the others and that an expert adviser should be appointed whose duty it would be to assist and advise farmers on technical matters connected with the industry. On the advice of the latter, expert examination of the soil with a view to securing technical advice of fertilisation and otherwise improving the flexibility of the soil could be made and also grants of small capital to families requiring money to start the industry. Another industry investigated was that of "salt manufacture and here the tone of the report was not very hopeful as the industry in nearly every case was found to be unremuneral. The amount of salt produced locally was considered insignificant compared with the "total amount of that imported and as about the only use of it in any considerable quantity here is for the fishing industry, it was not considered that the amount required for this purpose could be supplied by local production except at greatly enhanced cost. Varying degrees of prosperity were attributed to other activities such as tobacco cultivation, cotton growing, tea, fruit and vegetable growing, sugar cane, fruit, sweet potato, onion and peanut cultivation and in nearly every instance the chief difficulty to be overcome was the apathy of the Chinese farmers scattered about the districts of the New Territory. This presumably has not been overcome and in his remarks at the Council meeting H.E. the Governor said that other than hearing with their customary politeness what he (His Excellency) had to say to them on the matter nothing further had been done by the Chinese elders. All far-seeing residents—with confidence in the future growth of the Colony will, however, echo the sentiments of His Excellency that the last has not been heard of these plans for development. The plea of isolation, at any rate, can no longer be said to hold. Roads and railways have made intercourse easier and the time cannot be long distant when the time honoured methods of the New Territories, farmer may be broken into, that by example and precept he may be led to make more of his soil or stock and that are long Kowloon with its hinterland in the New Territories, may come to be referred to as the "bee-hive of in- industry of South China." Already private enterprise is showing the way.

## FOOD PRICES

## NORMALITY NOT YET REACHED

## AMPLE SUPPLIES:

The strike was a signal to the Chinese restaurants to increase their tariffs. A reduction has been made lately, but there is still an increase ranging up to 20 per cent.

No accurate comparison can be made of the prices of vegetables, fruit, and other produce, as much depends on quality and the seasons. Here, too, stallholders are not agreed in the matter of prices, and hold on to their perishable stocks as long as they can at maximum prices.

The market stalls show much activity, but it is not generally known that the employees are almost all new hands who were hurriedly engaged when the holders were faced with the risk of losing their licences.

Plentiful food supplies are now available at the markets, although prices all round do not appear to have yet reached the pre-strike level.

The prices of meat, fish and poultry fluctuate with the law of supply and demand, and, curiously enough, there seems to be no fixed standard of prices between the various stalls. Quality also governs the selling prices.

## THE GOVERNOR'S DEPARTURE

Sir,—To-day next week will be the occasion of Sir Edward Stubbs taking his final farewell of the Colony and as has been made known, presentations from the British and Chinese communities will be made to His Excellency on Thursday next in the City Hall. The ceremony is timed to take place at 4 p.m. This is my opinion, is most awkward as a good proportion of public citizens who wish to assemble in the farewell parting will find it inconvenient to leave their work at that hour. I consider that it should be the duty and privilege of public citizens to attend ceremonies of this nature provided that time is made suitable. May it not be better to change the time to 5 p.m. sharp? I hope that the Committee in charge will adopt my suggestion, as it is only too true that a very large gathering representative of the Colony's community is desired to be present.

## NASTY SMASH

BUS AND MOTOR CYCLE COLLIDE.

EUROPEAN GIRL INJURED.

A fractured rib was sustained by Miss Bessie Mackay, the 13 year old daughter of Mrs. Mackay of Jordan House, Kowloon, as a result of a collision yesterday afternoon between a motor cycle and side car and a bus returning from Laichikok. The former vehicle in attempting to pass the bus opposite the old Kowloon Tong struck the latter vehicle which was overturned into a pond of water on the side of the road.

An Indian warder, who apart from Miss Mackay, was the only other passenger, sustained minor injuries and shock.

Oil and gasoline which escaped from the bus as it ran down the bank towards the pond became ignited and the structure was burnt before the arrival of the fire brigade in spite of the partial immersion. If the passengers and the driver and conductor had not been thrown clear of the overturning vehicle, more serious consequences could not have been averted.

The driver was slightly injured in the fall and the conductor escaped unhurt.

## REDS IN COURT

SCRIM MAGE IN STREET.

(Reuter's Service.)

London, October 23.

Twelve arrested Communist leaders were charged at Bow Street with unlawful conspiracy to publish seditious libels and inhale mutiny. A large crowd was outside the court, including fashionably dressed women and many young men and women yearning red badges. A section of the crowd sang the "Red Flag." During a scrimmage the police took into custody a man carrying a red flag. Mr. Tom Mann was present in court.

Sir Trayers Humphreys, prosecuting, said the accused was being prosecuted as the leaders and principal executive officers of two illegal organisations, namely the Communist Party of Great Britain and the Young Communist League which received orders from Moscow. The prosecution's view was that all persons die, seminating the doctrine of Communism were liable to prosecution for sedition. Communism as explained by the accused persons was illegal because it involved the forklift overtook of the government, the creation of a class war and seduction of the armed forces of the Crown from their allegiance.
