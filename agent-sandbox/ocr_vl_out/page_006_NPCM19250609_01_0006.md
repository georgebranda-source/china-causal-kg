## MISCHIEVOUS MEDDLERS

It would be too much to expect the Shanghai riots to pass without the usual crop of mischievous meddlers, thousands of miles away, giving to the world their version and their “protest.” Yesterday the cables told us that

the Union of Chinese Associations in London have held a special meeting and solemnly adopted a resolution protesting against “the use of armed forces by interfering Powers to suppress the strike who shot down defenceless workers and students” and recording “full support” of the strikers.

The very wording of the resolution condemns those responsible for it. They have only received garbled and inspired versions of the origin of the disturbances, and read into these just what they were pleased to believe. They have taken their cue from the Chinese Foreign Office, which straight away also "protested" against the use of arms against the frenzied students, little knowing that those who had resort to arms were fighting with their back to the wall and only fired in self defence of their own lives and in the hope of saving the truculent Chinese themselves from a more terrible disaster. The members of the Union of Chinese Associations in London may know something about the traits of their compatriots in Shanghai, but obviously they can know as little of the actual cause of the outbreak as the natives of Timbuctoo. And even were they to be placed in possession of the facts, it seems too much to expect them to appraise these at their true worth and refrain at least from comment and protest until such time as the inevitable inquiry is made by an impartial tribunal.

Even if it be admitted, for the sake of argument, that the members of the Union of Chinese Associations in London have a certain right, by virtue of their nationality, to give expression to their views on the disturbances in Shanghai, what is to be said of the “sympathy” expressed by the International Labour Conference in Geneva? That conference comprises delegates of nearly sixty States, and although the Governments are entitled to send two delegates the representative organisations of workers may be expected to take greater advantage of their right to be represented by two delegates each. It is a labour affair pure and simple. What do workers' delegates from Sydney or Brussels know of the facts of the Shanghai disturbances? It is all very well for them to sit in conference twelve thousand miles away from the actual scene of the strife and adopt resolutions of “sympathy” with the Chinese workers—“sympathy,” be it noted that is impliedly a vote of censure on the foreign authorities in Shanghai.

Why must labour organisations invariably act the role of mischievous meddlers? Why cannot they concentrate on those problems for which the International Labour Conference in Geneva was convened instead of digressing and rushing in to express their opinions on a subject about which they must know next to nothing? There are some sane leaders in the labour organisations: men who take a sensible outlook on life, men who can express themselves reasonably on current world topics. Where were these good men and true at the Geneva Conference when they could not foresee the danger of trespassing on international preserves in Shanghai? That there is a real danger of fomenting the strife by utterly needless interference even by way of mere resolutions; is obvious to those much nearer Shanghai than is Geneva.

The Colony has had as its governors' soldiers of distinction and administrators of ability, but never, so far as we know, one whose shone as a man of letters equally as a diplomat. Should Sir Charles Eliot be appointed to this. For eastern "plum"—surely persimmon and li-chee would have been more appropriate—the Colony will gain in having a man whose towering height is "only exceeded by the many qualities he possesses. At the University it was not a pleasant thing to be "on the mat" before the Vice-Chancellor: there was a something about the ordeal which made you—if you were a somewhat wayward student—vow that the thing would never be repeated. The Vice-Chancellor exuded personality of a potent kind. Since those days Sir Charles has done a number of things entitling him to respect. There is his work in Siberia at a peculiarly vital time. There is also his ambassadorship at Tokyo, also during critical times. In the world of letters there is his recent monumental work on Buddhism. But behind everything is a distinguished career in many walks of life, entitling him to such a "plum" as Hongkong is now semi-officially described.

## Sir Charles Eliot

Eighty years' occupation of Hongkong have produced a wretched sanitary system; trained nullahs; and the Central Market. But with all these blessings of civilisation there has not been produced anything that will stay the sudden appearance of those incredibly awful creatures that come, like a baby, "out of the nowhere into here," fly about us, shed their feeble wings, and then wriggle and crawl to our innermost disgust. Apparently, they serve some useful purpose—but why should they appear just as the gong announces the dinner to which we have been looking forward? What is their purpose—if any—beyond that of being sent to try us? Big fleas, scientists—or is it poets? tells us are sent to gobble up the lesser fleas. But this does not seem to hold good in the case of flying ants. Given a spell of a certain kind of dampness and we know what we may expect towards eight of the clock in the evening. Has our Sanitary Board any knowledge of these vermin? Can it tell us what to do beyond putting bowls of water under or near a light, and "sloshing" at the pests with a newspaper? Presumably it cannot. And until it, or some other body can, it is useless Mr. Austen Chamberlain who has never been here, telling medicine gentlemen that Hongkong is a plum.

# THE CHINA OF THE PAST.

Reviewing "The Chater Collection" of Pictures relating to China, Hongkong and Macao, 1655-1860, "The Times Literary Supplement" says that Mr. James Orange was happily inspired when he persuaded Sir Paul Chater to acquire this interesting collection of pictures, engravings and books relating to the early days of European traffics and discoveries in China, with a view to presenting it to the Colony of Hongkong. Like Hakluyt, Mr. Orange in compiling this collection set himself to bring to light antiquities smothered and buried in dark silence, and to preserve certain memorable exploits, of late years by our English nation achieved, from the greedy and devouring jaws of oblivion; to gather likewise, and as it were to incorporate into one body, and torn and scattered limbs of our ancient and late navigations by sea, our voyages by land, and traffics of merchandise by both.

To the fulfilment of this evidently congenital task he has brought a wide knowledge of the subject and the discriminating taste of m connoisseur; the result is a volume worthy of the object in view and of Sir Paul's munificent gift to the Crown Colony.

Of the 430 paintings and drawings in the Chater Collection, 261 are reproduced in colour and half-tone plates by the Nickeloid Electrotype Company's "Ultimatt" process. Mr. Orange has divided them, chronologically and geographically, into twelve sections, each separately catalogued and annotated with numerous quotations from the works of contemporary authors, the whole supplemented by biographical lists of the artists and authors represented, and an excellent Index. For the student the interest of these beautifully printed pictures is intensified by their association with important events in the early history of foreign intercourse with China; for the general reader, the elusive charm of the works of Chinnery, Allom, Alexander, Borget, and other artist adventurers in the Far East is emphasized by extracts, admirably selected, from the descriptive writings of Robert Fortune, George Wingrove, Cooke, Bernard (of the Nemesis), Laurence Oliphant, Robert Swinhoe, Sir George Staunton, and many other authors. Their combined effect is as of "magic dasements" opening on the whole fascinating panorama of life in China, as Englishmen knew and lived it, from the beginning of the seventeenth century to the middle of the nineteenth century.

Even those who reckon themselves fairly familiar with the early days of British diplomacy and trade in the Far East may find their memories refreshed by this delightful book concerning many things which, in China itself, have long since been devoured by the greedy jaws of chilion. How many inhabitants of the Treaty Ports to-day, remember anything of our four years' military occupation of Canton, from 1857 to 1861, or the

fact that, by order of the Chinese Government, no white women were ever allowed to visit that city before 1841? How many underpaid diplomatists at Peking are aware that Lord Macartney's allowances as "Ambassador to China" (1793) were £15,000 a year? How many of Hongkong's prosperous citizens know that at a critical moment, just before the birth of the Colony, the now insignificant island of Chusan seemed destined to become the local habitation and base of British power in the China Sea? Or, to go still farther back, how many of us remember anything of the hazardous adventures, which these pictures recall, that led the Portuguese to plant their outpost of empire at Macao and to establish their trade, early in the sixteenth century; in rivalry with that of the Japanese, at Ningpo?

To those who have lived in the East these pages, that bring to mind so vividly remembrance of things past, will naturally suggest the reflection that, on the whole, the instincts and impulses which determine the attitude and actions of the Chinese, in the ever-recurring phases of their history, are far more consistent and persistent than our own. He who compares, for instance, the attitude towards the Opium Question of China's present representatives at Geneva with that of Viceroy Yeh at Canton in 1857 cannot fail to be impressed by the continuity of unbroken traditions which underlies Chinese statecraft. As far back as 1834, just after the arrival of Lord Napier at Macao, we find the Cantonese officials "burning some chests of opium in public, but still, in private, continuing to connive at and foster the opium trade." The words and gestures of a Sun Yat-sen may appear to be very different from those of the Imperial Commissioner Lin, who ruled over Canton eighty odd years ago, but their ultimate purport: and the racemind behind them, remain unmistakably the same.

Another unfortunately neglected lesson which these pages teach—amply demonstrated by Parkes at Canton in 1857 and clear for him who runs to read today at Hongkong, Shanghai, and Tientsin—is that no people on earth are more ready than the Chinese to accept and support quiet and orderly government, even though it be administered by foreigners; furthermore (a truth of vital significance to day), that the force required to establish and maintain law and order, in any city or province, need not be large. Nevertheless, as it was in the days of Macartney so it is today—China's rulers and leaders prefer pleasant fictions to unpleasant realities, and are still ready to jeopardize life, property, and the future of their country in blind adherence to their unchanging devotion to "face-pidgin." Any wealthy philanthropist concerned with the welfare of China might do good work and sow rich seeds of wisdom by sending a copy of this book to each of her Tuchuns, and to those of her modern Intellectuals who hold forth so eloquently on the abolition of extra-territoriality.
