## UNIQUE TRIAL

## EX-ATTORNEY-GENERAL CHARGED

ALLEGED BRIBES.

The trial of Henry Daugherty and Thomas Miller, which has been opened here, is unique inasmuch as it is the first recorded under the Federal Criminal Law of a member of the Cabinet for alleged misdeeds during his term of office.

Judge Mack is presiding at the trial: Mr. Max Stever, reputed to be one of the shrewdest New York lawyers, is leading the defence.

"It is claimed that Daugherty, and Miller and other accused received large fees for their part in authorizing the return of the so-called American Metals Company" to its European owners after the war. It is alleged that these sums were furnished by Henry Richard Nerton, of Frankfurt-on-Main, President of the German Corporation which originally owned the metal concern.

The United States Attorney, Mr. Bancory Buckner, is conducting the Government brief.

The Government contends that approximately $400,000 was paid in bribes whereupon Miller, with the approval of Daugherty, turned over to the European claimants the $7,000,000 realised from the sale of the Metals Company.

Herr Morton, who is the Government's star witness, in his evidence gave dates of his visits to the United States and his meetings with an alleged intermediary named King (who is now dead) and others.

## WEIRD DOCTORING

A party of distinguished medical men has just visited Horton Mental Hospital, Epsom, to see the manner in which the malaria-carrying mosquito is used to cure general paralysis. In 1917 German investigators discovered that the bite of the anopheline mosquito was an effective remedy for general paralysis. In British mental hospitals the treatment has proved so successful that world-wide interest is now being taken in it. Before being employed as "prescriptions," the insects infect themselves with the malaria germ. They are then made to bite patients, suffering from general paralysis, and when malaria is thus set up it has the effect of "killing" the paralysis. Quinine is then given the patient to free him from malaria. Weird though this form of doctoring may seem, it gives a high percentage of successes, and is the only known process by which general paralysis can be combated effectively.

## ILL-STARRED

## CLAIMS FOR LOANS ADVANCED

YACHT "ST. GEORGE."

The story of the financial difficulties of a scientific expedition to the South Pacific undertaken by a party of experts in the steam yacht "St. George" in October 1924 was told in the Admiralty Court, London.

The matter came before the Court at the instance of Major Archibald Douglas, a director of the Company, who organised the expedition, and Mr. William Alexander Grant, of Hillersdon, Cullompton, Devon, one of the passengers on the yacht.

The former claimed £200 and the later £1,000, advanced to assist the expedition when the yacht had got through the Panama Canal and was stranded for lack of funds.

Mr. Balloch for the plaintiffs, said a company called Research Expedition (Ltd.), with a capital of £3,000, was formed to own the yacht. The vessel cost £8,350 and a further £16,815 was spent in fitting her for the voyage. To meet these liabilities Mr. Gates advanced £25,000 on mortgage. The mortgagee had already obtained judgment on his mortgage and the vessel had been sold by order of the Court, realising £6,600.

Major Douglas said the expedition achieved most interesting results. They had hoped to repay advances by means of a film of the trip, which unfortunately did not pay.

Mr. Grant said that he, like the six other passengers who accompanied the expedition, paid £700 for the trip. He advanced the loans to enable the ship to get on. They were held up at one point for such a long time for lack of funds that he could not stand it any longer. After a long legal argument as to the validity of the bottomry bounds the President reserved judgment.
