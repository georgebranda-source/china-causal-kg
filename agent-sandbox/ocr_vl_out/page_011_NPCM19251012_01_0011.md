Englishmen's Foreign Titles.

My reference to the Maltese title which Sir Gerald Strickland has the Royal permission to use, has prompted a correspondent to send me the names of some of the other Englishmen who bear foreign titles which they do not use in England. Among them are Sir Herbert Cook, the art critic, who is Viscount Montserrate in Portugal; Sir Edward Barry, who is Baron de Barry in the same country; and Sir Alexander Nelson Hood, at once time private secretary to Queen Mary, is Duke of Bronte in Sicily. Amongst the peers, the Duke of Wellington is Prince of Waterloo in the Netherlands: Duke of Ciudad Roderigo in Spain, and Duke of Victoria, Marquis of Torres Vedras, and Count of Vimira in Portugal. The Duke of Richmond is Duke of Ambigny in France; and the Earl of Clancarty is Marquis of Heusden in the Netherlands, writes "Gad-about" in the "Daily Chronicle."

No one can envy the men who are employed on the railroads of Alaska, for in winter all the metal work is so cold that to touch a piece of steel with the bare hand will be to strip the skin just as if the metal were red hot instead of being icy cold. The temperature in winter drops to seventy degrees below zero, and whilst in the summer months the lives of the railwaymen are rendered unbearible by reason of the deer and horse flies that swarm around them, tunneling into the skin so that the men have to swathe their heads in netting and cover their hands with an oily mixture; the intense coldness of the winter months presents equal discomfort from frost bites and other evils that abound in the heart of the dismal tundra. ## Kidnapping Mystery

A st mysterious case of kidnapping is reported in Paris. The victim, Roger Bouveau, a 17-year-old apprentice to a joiner, was on his way to work when, he says, he was kidnapped by a party of motorists and chloroformed. Bouveau's story is that just as he entered the Rue St. Maur, quite a busy street, in which his employer's workshop is situated, a motorist, driving a closed four-seater car, stopped him to ask the time. At the same moment two other men, whose descriptions he has given to the police, stepped out from a doorway and bundled him into the car, pressing upon his mouth and nose a wad of cotton wool or a handkerchief soaked in chloroform. Bouveau remembers nothing more until the moment when he found himself lying in a ditch in Vincennes Wood, with a priest and a keeper bending over him and trying to revive him. The priest and keeper relate that they were taking an early morning walk together in the wood when they heard groans from a ditch near the main road, and found Bouveau unconscious with a handkerchief gag stuffed into his mouth and held in place by a scarf. The victim had been robbed of 40f and a small gold watch, but he bore no marks of violence. The police have two theories which may throw light on this mysterious case of a lad being carried off in broad daylight from a busy street. One is that Bouveau may have invented the whole story with the idea of getting himself talked about. In support of this idea it is pointed out that there is no trace of any narcotic or anesthetic on the gag. But, on the other hand, Bouveau seems to be suffering from some kind of poisoning. It is possible that he was mistaken for his brother, a locksmith, and abandoned in Vincennes Wood only after the kidnappers had realised that they had carried off the wrong man.
