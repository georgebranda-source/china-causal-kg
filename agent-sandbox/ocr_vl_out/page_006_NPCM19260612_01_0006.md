## EAST OF SUEZ

Life at Home or East of Suez? The conveners of debates at the European Y.M.C.A. are to be congratulated on introducing a topic which concerns us individually and particularly young men who, in the course of their first or second period of service, have to decide whether or not they will, by a continuation of such service, commit themselves permanently to a life of absence from their native land. The glamour which, to those unacquainted with it, attaches to the East largely as the result of the efforts of novelists and writers who concern themselves only with its strangeness and picturesqueness — signifies little to those with whom such conditions have lost their novelty. What then is left on which to base a decision as to whether life East

of Suez is still preferable to that at Home? Conditions of comparative ease and greater financial inducements were stressed at the debate in question. These are strong inducements but in themselves it is doubtful whether they can be said to constitute a desirable argument for those on the threshold of life. To allow the capacities with which we have been endowed to decay by reason of an existence in which one's £.s.d. is assured for a few years is surely not conducive to the production of the type of men who will be a credit to the Empire they represent in such an outpost as this. The argument, however, that by reason of the greater conditions of leisure in the East one is able to develop capacities which it is difficult to under conditions of greater stress such as at Home, is harder to most. If full use is made of such leisure to undergo a course of reading or training which will add to one's capacity as a working unit and a citizen, then

to the development of one's inherent gifts, such conditions may be considered as highly desirable and a very strong argument in favour of life in the East. With regard to the lack of opportunities for entertainment and for getting away from one's environment except at the termination of one's agreement; after all, as the Chairman said in his remarks after the debate, life in any place is what we choose to make it. If we for ever pine for that Home life and Home facilities which are absent from the East then it were far better that we recognise the inevitable and decide that the East is not for such as we. If, however, in the light of the broadening of mind and greater realisation of the need of incorporating the manner of life and policies of other nations into our scheme of things — which follow the breaking away from an insular life—we recognise the desirability of studying life as we find it abroad and finding interests to replace those which may be no longer possible to us; then life in the East may lead us to things higher than would have been possible under the more cramped conditions of Home. We have only to refer to the lives of many whose sphere of activity has been the East and who have performed signal service to the Empire by lives which have been characterised by foresight and the initiative to give practical expression to that foresight. One such we have had occasion to mourn the loss of in very recent times. A too narrow view of the limitations of life in the East in regard to entertainment and other conditions of Home life and failure to recognise the many useful spheres of activity in which success may be attained and signal service to the Empire performed may have been responsible for what in fact amounted at the Y.M.C.A. gathering to a vote of censure on life in the East. Given a few years' longer experience and growth of vision of the many paths in which they can still be "pioneers" in the cause of humanity and progress—and that same gathering might return a very different verdict on this subject. However, it was a very narrow majority which the upholders of Home life obtained and the law of the survival of the fittest will probably function in such a manner as to eliminate from the East those who are carrying on "under protest" in favour of those who, recognising its difficulties, are prepared to accept them and to create conditions under which their lives here may be pleasurable and a credit of themselves and their Empire.

## TENANTS EJECTED BY GAS

PARIS POLICE ONSLAUGHT ON AGED WOMEN.

Recently the so-called "gas brigade of the Paris police, armed with shields, gasmasks, and tear-gas projectors lent their services to a landlord" to expel two elderly women from a flat they had occupied over twenty years and refused to leave in favour of the proprietor's son, barricading themselves in. A hole was made in the wall, lachrymatory gas injected, and the two women taken out in a state of extreme physical distress. On the affair being made by the press, much public indignation, and the police have been asked by the municipal council to explain their conduct.

Somewhat comic relief is given to the story by the sequel. The Tenants' Union, to which the women belonged, learned that a flat was vacant in a neighbouring building. They marched in procession to the flat, seized it, installed the two women in it, and transferred their furniture. It is unlikely that the tear-gas will be used again to eject them.

Progress of the World.

Such is the progress of the world, exclaims the well-known writer M. Jean Pierrefeu, in a comical article in "Information." The whole civilised world was revolted when the Germans first used gas in the war. Now gas apparatus is part of the normal equipment of a municipal police force, and it is put at the service of a bailiff. The police defence is that gas is a less brutal method of ejectment than dragging old ladies out of their home by physical force. "Thus what was a horror has become the weapon of masculine courtesy, an elegance of the police."

Where will this progress cease? asks M. Pierrefeu. "For myself, I can well foresee a future in which all we thought horrible in the war will seem quite a matter of course. When schoolboys make an uproar in the classroom the professor will pull a hand grenade out of his drawer, and drop it lightly among the scapegraces to restore silence. To keep the crowd moving in front of the Elysee on the day of the Presidential reception, a tank will be called out to eliminate a score or two of curious sightseers. And who can say whether ten years hence the President of the Chamber, when he wants to suspend a sitting, will no longer bother about putting on his hat and all the rest of the ceremony, but will turn on tear gas and rush out of the House, certain of being followed, pell-mell and without the slightest resistance by the whole mass of depui

"It is sufficient in this age of machinery for the practical character of a device denounced at its inception as an abomination, to be recognised for it to be put into the common service. For progress is no mere empty word, and it is not high-sounding phrases that will arrest it."

## DRAGON BOAT DAY

NOTABLE ANNIVERSARY ON MONDAY

### [By "Li Chung-yin.]

Monday (June 14) is the 6th day of the 5th moon according to the Chinese (lunar) calendar.

A reference to a well-known diary also tells us that it is the 8rd day of Zulhijjah, a Bank and Customs holiday.

The Chinese classical name da T'ien Chung Chieh. In the Cantonese colloquial it is Tuen Yeung Chiet. Essentially, the day is the Dragon Boat Festival.

Until 1924, the festivities in Hongkong were mainly confined to the dragon boat races. The festival has not been observed as a general holiday until recent years when the advent of labour unions brought about a day of rest for the toiler. With the strike intervening last year, there were no celebrations—and none has been announced for Monday.

Sir Henry May's Favour.

When the late Sir Henry May was Governor, he seldom withheld his patronage from the races at Aberdeen. The events at this place gradually lost favour for want of a generous person to donate prizes of roast pork, banners, firecrackers &c. During the last few years, races on more practical lines have been staged at North Point where they used to attract large crowds.

Canton is having a big celebration this year. Latterly, the boat races have been practically non-est as the authorities found difficulty in curbing the attendant disorderly outbreaks.

The dragon oarsman's sense of sport was that his crew must win at all costs—even if he had to strike, or open fire at a rival crew that dared lead by a length or two. #### DR. JOHN B. FEARN OF SHANGHAL

Dr. Fearn came to China in 1895 under the Southern Methodist Episcopal Mission Board and was stationed at Soochow until in 1906 he was appointed business manager for the Methodist Mission and Treasurer of the Associated Missions' Board in Shanghai, which position he retained until January, 1917, when he resigned to take up a commission in the Chinese Labour Corps, for which his experience so eminently fitted him.

On his return from France he was appointed Resident Medical Superintendent at the General Hospital, Shanghai, which post he retained up to the time of his death.

Last year, while on Home leave, although suffering from the sickness in its early stages, the-gave up his whole time to studying hospital management, in order that the improvements might be carried out in the hospitals, the welfare of which he had so much at heart.

Dr. Fearn was married in 1896 to Miss Anne Walter, M.D., then in Soochow acting as locum tenens at the Women's Hospital.

The death of Dr. John B. Fearn occurred at 30, Route Pichon, Shanghai, says the "North China Daily News."

The late Dr. Fearn's gift for organisation, which amounted to a sixth sense, was exemplified in the many undertakings in which he was interested, particularly in the General Hospital which he brought to its present high standard of efficiency. He was also Resident Medical Superintendent of the Country Hospital and, though suffering from the disease from which he succumbed and though he realised that he had not long to live, he devoted himself unthiringly to carrying on his work at both hospitals, hoping that he might be able to get the Country Hospital well organised before his end came.

## AT THE QUEEN'S

## FINE RACING STORY NEXT WEEK

“The Dixie Handicap” will have a big place in the programme at the Queen's Theatre next week. It is a screen version of a story called “Dixie” by Gerald Beaumont that appeared in the Red Book Magazine.

It tells how the lost glory of an aristocratic Southern family was brought back by a horse and the young man who trained her. Full of the thrilling incident, and also possessing an unusually beautiful love angle, the story makes perfect screen material. Waldemar Young, who wrote the continuity for "The Great Divide," was the adapter of "The Dixie Handicap."

The cast includes such notable players as Frank Keenan as Judge Roberts; Claire Windsor as his daughter, Virginia Lloyd Hughes as "The Dancin' Kid," who, in spite of his slangy title, is the hero; John Sainpolis in the villain role; Otis Harlan as a faithful darky; Joseph Morrison, Otto Hoffman, Edward Martinel, and Ruth King.

### DAMAGES FOR DEATH OF MR. SADLER

The owner of the motor car which killed Mr. C. R. Sadler, on April 19 on Route Stanislas Chevalier, Shanghai, must pay Tla. 7,000 to Mrs. Sadler according to the verdict of the French Mixed Court. The chauffeur, who drove the car at the time, was sent to jail for one month and fined $200. Mr. du Pac de Marsoulies appeared for Mrs. Sadler, while Mr. du Auxion de Ruffe defended the chauffeur. As may be recalled from the account of the proceedings in the U.S. Coroner's Court, deceased met his death when he was thrown from his motor cycle which driving toward the Bund on Route Stanislas Chevalier, the motor car going in a westerly direction. Although in the French Court the defence wished to establish contributory negligence on the part of deceased alleging that he tried to pass several riches, police officers, who made a map of the situation showed that the motor car was on the wrong side of the road.
