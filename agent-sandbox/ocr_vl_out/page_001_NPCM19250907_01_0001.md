## ANTI-RED COUP?

CADETS DISARMED AT KONGMOON.

LAND AND SEA ATTACK.

A GENERAL TORTURED TO

A coup, resulting in an appreciable check to the Whampoa Cadets, has been reported from Kongmoon, where several hundred of Canton's crack regiment are stated to have given up their weapons to preserve their lives.

"To-day's "Kang Seung Ynt Po" ("Industrial and Commercial Daily Press")" says that the expedition of Cadets sent from Canton has met with disaster. After last week's crisis in the capital, the Reds attacked General Leung Hung-kai's "Troops in Kongmoon and its hinterland. The Canton force landed without opposition. Of the 2,000 men, the majority were sent inland to round up the wavering anti-Reds and several hundred stayed behind to maintain communications.

Taking advantage of the situation, General "Li Chai-sum of the West River," launched an offensive against Kongmoon. The vernacular report goes on to say that his fleet of armed vessels moved down river with the tide and a combined land-and-sea attack succeeded. Not only was the garrison of Cadets disarmed, but the line of the up-country force has been threatened.

[Note: Li Chai-sum has already been appointed one of General Cheung Kai-shek's army commanders but the Chinese papers hint that he is aiming at the overthrow of the Cadets to ensure his own safety.]

## Tortured to Death?

The “Tsun Wan Yat Po” is in receipt of a message which says that General Leung Hung-kai, the former Kongmoon commander, has died from privation through being tortured in his trial by a military court appointed by Canton's autocrats.

General Leung, it will be recalled, was captured and has been in custody for over a week. His fate is not definitely known but Canton is said to allege that he is indirectly connected with the murder of Liu Chung-oi.

## Generals Shot

Other vernacular papers report the shooting, under orders from Canton's military directorate, of Lam Chik-mien (former Superintendent, Kowloon-Canton Railway, Chinese section), Commander Lam Shu-ngai and Commander Yeung Kam-loong, who fell into Red hands when the Cadet chieftain effected his coup.

Little is also known of Hu Hanmin. A Chinese paper says that it has now transpired 'that his arrest was not due to suspicions of his loyalty to the Kuomintang—or the Bolshevik party as it is designated—but that the trouble is only an outcome of the periodic scramble for power, events which happen in Canton regularly when several aspire to rule Kwangtung as a despot.

Chan Ping-sang, the former notorious seamen leader who was pardoned by the late Dr. Sun Yat-sen for the murder of his wife, is once more in the public eye.

Recently his actions as chief of the strike pickets at Nam Tau were reported. It now transpires that for the space of 36 hours he occupied the post of district magistrate at Nam Tau, which is just beyond Deep Bay and, of course, in Chinese territory.

Shortly before the arrival of the proper magistrate the embargo on cargo and passengers for Hongkong was withdrawn with the result that in two days several hundred people returned to the Colony.

The restrictions have been re-imposed but Chan Ping sang is a mandarin no longer.

### BARON HAYASHI. COLONY'S DISTINGUISHEI VISITOR

Baron Hayashi, who is returning to Japan on the conclusion of his term of office as Japanese Ambassador in London, arrived in Hongkong yesterday by the s. "Fushimi Maru." He was met by the Japanese Consul (Mr. Takahashi) and other well-known Japanese members of the community. He launched at Government House and in the evening was present at a dinner given by the Japanese community in his honour at the Consul's residence. Prior to leaving London, Baron Hayashi had expressed in interviews, the valuable advice he had received from British statemen and to the "allace at heart" between Japan and Britain. In his opinion, he said, the greatest present menace to the world's peace were the questions regarding China and Russia owing to the efforts of the Soviet Government to create disturbance. There was no doubt, he said, that this interests of the Powers were being increasingly centred in the Pacific but he did not think that the Singapore base was a solution of the problem for Britain. He thought the scheme would be a waste of millions of pounds.

## A SAFE RETURN

LOCAL PRESSMEN'S CANTON EXPERIENCE.

SHACKLED AND JAILLED.

Mr. E. Oliver, of the "South China Morning Post," and Mr. J. S. Cox, of the "Hongkong Daily Press," returned to Hongkong by the 8.5. "Honum" yesterday after having been captured by strikers in Cinton, by whom they were subjected to indignity and imprisonment. After being protected by armed Chinese soldiers from an angry mob to whom the mere fact that they were foreign and in their power was sufficient for them to demand their death, they two journalists had chalms clamped on their necks and were incarcerated for 17 hours in a filthy cell with 14 Chinese prisoners.

The reason for their venturing into Canton at a time when affairs may well be described as critical was that they wished to despatch a cable to their respective newspapers announcing the safe arrival of the 8.5. "Honum" upon which they had travelled from Hongkong. It was 6:30 p.m. on Thursday, when they went and still light when they returned to the Customs wharf with their object achieved. Nothing unleavied had occurred and the reception they had had from officials and passers-by had been cordial. Their troubles began when arriving late, they found that the boat they were expecting to be waiting them at the wharf was not there.

The English bridge to Shamean at Shu Xi, not far from where they were, was barricaded with buried wire. Passage by boat also was impossible because the sampan people would not take aboard foreigners. The only course open to the two pressmen there for was to on the 11th of March. The crowd of Chinese had collected. Hostility, later to be replaced by funateak hatred, was not forthcoming until, under the pretext of finding them passes from the Government which would enable them to get a motor boat to Shamean, in Chinese in European dress persuaded them to accompany him to what must have been in the nature of a gathering place for strikers.

From then onwards the pressmen were virtually captives and there followed what Mr. Oliver terms a "ghustly" ride to further headquarters with the hostile and fanatical mob surging around. Only the armed Chinese guards with which they had been provided seems to have saved them from probable death and these same guards, reinforced, were called upon later to repel the mob which attacked the building when Mr. Oliver and Mr. Cox were undergoing cross-examination.
