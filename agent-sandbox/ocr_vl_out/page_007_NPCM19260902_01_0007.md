Following in the wake of the Times the Daily Telegraph is seriously perturbed by the chaotic conditions prevailing in China and more particularly the anti-foreign developments. The Daily Telegraph is a sound journal far removed from the Chauvinist type, and is not in the habit of raising the cry of "Wolf" for the sake of mere amusement. It would be idle, knowing the reputation of the Daily Telegraph, to inquire whether its volatile pronouncement on China, as cabled by Rêuter to-day, has been inspired or not. It may or it may not be. It is all a matter of surmise, and, in any event, that does not affect the issue at stake—the need for some form of intervention to protect the lives and property of foreigners in China.

The list of outrages committed by Chinese boycott pickets and by Chinese soldiers and banditti during the past three months alone makes formidable and almost incredible reading. There is no end to which the Chinese of to-day will not go to humiliate the foreigner and to encompass either his ruin or his death. Well may the Daily Telegraph re-echo the views of the men on the spot that the so-called policy of "pence and conciliation," about which we have heard so much during the past year and a half, is actually nerveless submission which has only made the position worse:

The challenge has been thrown down to Great Britain. She is, as is pointed out, the only Powerable to bring the United States and Japan together with herself and others in a common policy of intervention in some form which is simply warranted by the ghastliness of the tragedy at present being enacted in China. Like the

Times the Daily Telegraph does not state the exact form of the suggested intervention. It goes, however, a step further than the Times by suggesting that Great Britain should send out a special Plenipotentiary — for instance, the Marquis of Reading — who "will show that Great Britain is in earnest."

Unfortunately for the suggestion of the Daily Telegraph, it is one thing to say that Great Britain is the only Power able to bring Japan and the United States together with herself and others in a common policy of intervention, and another thing altogether to transform that idea into concrete form. Up to the present the main anti-foreign propaganda in China has been directed against Great Britain and Japan, and whilst their joint case for some form of intervention is unassailable they comprise only two of the Powers interested in China.

One is tempted to ask what does the League of Nations stand for if it is not to meet such cases as exist in China today? Only yesterday China made a special plea for a permanent seat on the League Council, in which she was strongly supported by Japan. Is it not time that the League was induced by Great Britain and Japan to "sit up and take notice" of what is transpiring in China against the interests of the nationals of members of the League other than China? If any form of intervention, diplomatic or otherwise is feasible, it ought surely to come from the League of Nations. That, on the whole, appears at the moment a better suggestion than that of sending out a special Plenipotentiary who might be in no better position than the British Minister in Peking—that is, in the absence of a common understanding with Japan, America, and the other Powers interested in China.

## Which Side Is Winning?

It is only human to exaggerate one's accomplishments and we think local readers will forgive the misleading claims made by the opposing sides in the present war in central China. A fortnight ago we published a rough sketch explaining where the rival factions were posted. A contemporary has produced a map which gives other useful information. For those who stumble over jaw-breaking names in the cables we offer a word of comfort. Working at high pressure, the journalist is often severely tried to fathom intricacies. Telegrams costing what they do, it is only natural that texts be very brief. The best way to find out who is winning is by reading the "tone" of a message rather than by pouring over minor details and referring to a map. All this is by the way. Canton definitely stated that the Nationalist armies had captured Wuchang and Hankow, two important cities on the Yangtze River. Having read of the advance by the Kuominchup—Canton's allies—from Nankow Pass towards Peking, whereas in fact the Kuominchun had to fall back and sacrifice Nankow, we were chary of the official despatch which arrived on Tuesday. We do not maintain that we knew all along, nor that we are wise after the event. Adopting the usual formula used in Chinese politics of dividing by half, it was thought

that there was reason for an element of doubt. Since then the pro-Canton section of the vernacular press has admitted in a roundabout way that the earlier reports were premature. By the foregoing we do not intend to convey that Wu Pei-fu has converted defensive into offensive. An independent source says that his side is now holding its own. Further than that we will not venture. Our experience of warfare in central part of China is that once the winter sets in, stalemate will prevail and unification will be no nearer.

## CORRESPONDENCE

### ALLEGED INCIDENT ON A. TRAM

DRIVER'S LANGUAGE.

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"] Sir, Years ago a rather unusual action was contested in court here over the construction placed on a book of instructions issued by the Tramway Co. to their native employees. Pondering over an incident last night, it occurred to me that some rules must still apply. This is what happened:—

I was on a tram, in the lower compartment, going west, about 6.30 p.m. The only other passenger was a Chinese lady. Rounding a bond after the Wing Lok wharf, the tram all but ran into a country yokul who was day dreaming. The motorman showed presence of mind by switching off and putting on the brake.

As friend farmer skipped out of the way," the motorman shouted some epithets at him in Chinese. My knowledge of the language is limited—admitted. It was enough to make out some abominable words and vile phrases.

I admit that the driver had every right to be annoyed and to call the yokel names. Had he, can you inform me Sir, the right to use such foul language in front of a lady of his own nationality? He knew no better; I grant, but it is the duty of the company to see that their employees neither offend passengers in such manner, nor provoke a breach of the peace by calling a pedestrian names.

## Enclosing my card:

### Hongkong, Sept. 1

## THANKS FOR COURTESY

[To the Editor of the "China Mail.] Sir,—Here is an instance of where a bit of courtesy earns permanent gratitude. I had occasion to make an inquiry by telephone at one of the police stations. The clerk gruffly told me that he was too busy to reply over the "phone and that I should call in person. Before I could get a word in he had rung off. I rang up again and asked for the inspector on duty. He very courteously took down the name of my friend for whom I was inquiring, asked me to wait a minute and then set my mind at rest by telling me that everything was all right. The difference in mentality counts for a great deal out here.

## CAR INCIDENT

## GARAGE MANAGER AND HIS DEBTOR

## DID INTIMIDATE

The case in which a Chinese garage manager was charged with using force to make a fisherman sign a promissory note for $100 concluded before Mr. R. E. LindseLL at the Central Magistracy yesterday.

At a previous hearing the complainant said that he borrowed the money and promised to repay it. He had in fact paid interest on two occasions. He alleged that he was seized by the neck while inside the motor car and forced to sign the note.

After hearing evidence, His Worship said he was satisfied that defendant intimidated the man into signing the promissory note. Defendant was fined $150, as he was held to be responsible for the car being driven away from the police. The driver of the car who was charged with failing to stop when ordered by the police was discharged.

#### H.E.'S BIRTHDAY

## ENTERTALNED AT TAIPO MARKET

GUEST OF KAI FONG.

## (1) 

In a specially erected matshed at Taipo yesterday members of the Kai Fong met to do honour to H.E. the Governor, Sir Cecil Clementi, who many years ago held the position there of District Officer. The occasion was His Excellency's "birthday".

The matshed was erected between two "Flame of the Forest" trees which Sir Cecil and Lady Clementi planted at Tripo Market on a somewhat similar occasion a year ago when the villages of the New Territories united in doing him honour shortly after his arrival to take up the duties of Governor of Hongkong.

Taipo Market was decorated with flags for the occasion and a Chinese band played during the afternoon in an specially erected bandstand. Strings of crackers were fired upon His Excellency's arrival, also a 17 gun salute. The vicinity was thronged with Chinese.

The proceedings opened with the singing of Chinese students of the local schools and the guests were then entertained to a Chinese dinner consisting (with the exception of the ingredients of the inevitable sharks' fin soup) of products in-digenous to the New Territories.

H.E. the Governor sat at the head of the table under an illuminated address containing a congratulatory Birthday message and behind a silver replica of the symbolic god of longevity.

In reply to Mr. Lee Chung-chong, who offered His Excellency the congratulations of his hosts and read an illuminated address in Chinese which he handed to the chief, guest. His Excellency thanked his hosts for the congratulations and hoped that they too would enjoy long life and good health.

His Excellency expressed the hope that the elders of the New Territories would always keep him informed of affairs in their district particularly of anything which they considered might promote the interests and happiness of the people. He would always be very glad to help them in such matters. (Applause).

At the conclusion of the dinner group photograph was taken outside the building.

## CHARMING DANCERS

## DENISHÄWN COMPANY AT THE QUEENS

LAST NIGHT'S OPENING

Ruth St. Denis is just as charming as she was when she last visited Hongkong.

Although palm leaf fans were in use here and there last night when the Denishawn dancers opened their four-day season at the Queen's, a bumper house thoroughly appreciated the exhibition.

"Aren't they clever?" was the remark heard on all sides.

With new features, special lighting effects that were used to excellent purpose, Mr. A. Strok's company easily fulfilled all anticipations.

Full of variety and interest, the programme was just of about the correct length.

Displaying perfect technique and much charm, each and every member of the company captivated the hearts of Hongkongites in no uncertain manner.

## Ted Shawn At His Best

Ted Shawn was at his best. In Adagio Pathetique, he personified a Greek god come to life in such a manner that there was a real thrill to be felt. As an American cowboy, he gave a pleasing, a realistic interpretation.

Miss St. Denis won the hearts of the Japanese members of the audience. Ladies of Nippon who were present refused to believe that the one who carried out "Flower Arrangement" with such fondness of the cherry blossom could be the same as Ishtar of the Seven Gates.

The Misses Doris Humphrey, Day, Douglas and Sherman "won deserved applause. As a crap-shooter, Charles Weidman pleased a good many and the supporting members were all in good form.
