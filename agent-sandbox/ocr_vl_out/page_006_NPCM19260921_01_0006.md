## PRETTY CEREMONY IN CHEFOO

[From Our Own Correspondent.]

Chefoo. Sept. 8.

A very, interesting wedding took place at St. Peter's Church on September 4 when Miss Gertrude Wadsworth was married to Ensign Burton Doggett of the U.S. Navy.

The “knot” was tied by the Rev. B. M. McOwan, the port chaplain.

The bride was beautifully dressed in a gown of white crepe-de-chiné, embroidered with seed pearls and "silver sequins" and wore a long bridal veil of tulle suspended from a lace circle, which had been worn by her mother when she was married. She was led to the altar on the

Arm of Commander Oscar Smith. Mrs. Oscar Smith acted as Matron of Honour, and her little daughter as bridesmaid, while Ensign Seigrist was best-man.

Reception and Honeymoon. Miss Emery and two of the bridegroom's fellow officers played the organ accompanied by a violin, as the bride entered, and as the married couple left, under an arch of swords held by officers of the Fleet.

The Church was most tastefully decorated by the ladies of the Fleet.

A reception was afterwards held at the Broadway Hotel, when the health of the bride was proposed by the Admiral of the Fleet, and the bride cut her wedding cake with her husband's sword.

Later on in the evening the happy pair left for their honeymoon which they are spending in Peking.

Many valuable and beautiful presents are on their way from Home in addition to those received here.

The bride's dress and veil were a present from her mother, Mrs. Wadsworth of Charlotte, N. Carolina, U.S.A.

# HUMBUG OF PEKING CORRESPONDENCE

THE FUTURE HOPE OF CHINA'S PROGRESS.

WASHBUCKLERS

Mr. C. L'Estrange Malone, who has just returned from China, in an interview, gives his impressions of two great rival cities—Peking and Canton.

Anyone who has met the Chinese in North, "Centre and South must realise that although there may be differences between those one meets in Peking, Shanghai and Canton, yet the Chinese are one people, one nation, bound together by an age-old civilisation, an extremely high culture and definite characteristics, needing only to find the form of government through which they may be able to realise national unification and end the civil strife which has broken China into the zones of many warring bandits.

We must open our eyes to the "Humburg of Peking." There is no central Government or authority in China at the moment. But as a result largely of its historical position, and also because it is easier to continue the humbug rather than launch into, the intricate complications of change which facing realities would involve, the foreign Powers continue to recognise as the Government of China whatever military swash-buckler has his nominees in the Peking offices, when its writ seldom extends outside those massive and ancient city walls and often has very little control within them.

In all my journeyings through China there are certain impressions, which remain vividly with me. There is "the contrast between the evangelical fervour and simplicity of life at the Kuominchun (National Army) headquarters which I visited at Kalgan, the fine discipline and enthusiasm, and, on the other hand, the obviously luxurious life of the staff of the Army of Chang Tsolin, when I visited his son's headquarters in Peking.

It is quite clear that Chang Tso-lin and Wu Pei-fu are equally the enemies of progress and national unification in China. I think of Hankow, the headquarters of Wu Pei-fu, his arsenal-belching forth smoke and fire night and day, and, adjoining, the Hanyang works, the only iron and steel works in the whole of China, its blast furnaces and rolling mills rusting for lack of use.

In Canton it is at once clear that we are in a new progressive China. With new roads, new bridges, new parks for the people, order and discipline, far and away the finest and most wonderful Chinese city in the Far East—admitted even by its opponents to be the best Government in China—Canton is the hope of new China.

The Government of Canton, and also that of the provinces, around Shanghai, now gathered under the rule of that intriguing unknown quantity, Sun Chuan-fang, are as much entitled to be regaded as the Government of China as are Chang and Wu. The "Humbug of Peking" cannot go on for ever.

Members of the Canton Government, putting the interests of the nation first, told me that they certainly do not want to be recognised as an independent Government. They do not want to dismember China. Undoubtedly, however, they should have their share of the Customs surplus instead of everything being given by its foreign controllers to Peking.

China's Future Capital?

The time may come, however, when the Canton Government will be recognised as the Government of China and the capital of China shifted from Peking to Canton. But that is another story.

Most of my time was spent in investigating Labour conditions and Labour organisations. I tried to visit every important trade union centre in China.

Trade Unionism in China is in its infancy. The Chinese have been, and still are, greatly in need of help in organising themselves in order to raise their terribly low standard of life; and if they have accepted the only helping hand held out to them—that of the Russians—who can blame them?

The conditions which I saw in cotton mills and silk flatures—the women, the little children working, standing twelve hours a day, the half-suffocated babies who spend the early years of their lives in those steaming reeling shedes—the conditions are so indefensively in human that I do not mind by whom the unions are organised at present.

## SETTLEMENT OF THE BOYCOTT

Sir, — Allow me space in your journal to disagree with the sentiments expressed in your leading article yesterday. To me it is plainly apparent that H.E. Mr. Chen Yu-jen makes a categorical statement. Whether he speaks on behalf of the Government of China or for the Government of South China makes not the slightest difference to me. As representative of the de facto power in Canton he says that the boycott will cease on or before October 10. What more do you want.

H.E. Mr. Chen Yu-jen also states that his Government will collect certain taxes. That decision has nothing to do with the settlement of the boycott. It is also outside the jurisdiction of Hong Kong, official or unofficial. Whether the Powers will interfere or not does not concern Hong Kong one jot. The signatories to the Washington Treaty are well qualified to look after their own interests. As one of these signatories, Britain will have to take her stand with the other Powers.

Please do not confuse the two issues. Canton is going to call off the boycott. Hong Kong is pleased. Canton is to find means to reimburse the strikers. Hong Kong has nothing to do with that. Leave well alone and get down to business. Through Hong Kong being concerned in the boycott Britain is not necessarily interested in Canton's methods to obtain revenue.

Yours, etc., MERCHANT.

Hong Kong, Sept. 21.

## OBITUARIES

MR. KUNGPAH KING.

The first Masonic funeral over the remains of a Chinese mason in Shanghai was held on September 13 at 53 Paoshan Road. The deceased was Mr. Kungpah King, aged 48, who was a returned student from England and a member of the Scottish Rite Bodies in Peking. The ceremony was performed by the local Scottish Rite Bodies at the telegraphic request of the Peking Bodies, and the officiating masons were officers of the Shanghai Chapter Rase Croix, No. 3 Messrs. J.S. Potter, B. P., Dillon, Chas. Irons, Wm. Yinson Lee, A. Q. Adamson, Dr. H. C. Mel, Luther Jee and some dozen others, including N. L. Han and Sohtsu King, a brother of the deceased who is also a member of the Peking Scottish Rite Bodies.

##### MR. W. H. DOP

## 0

A shocking discovery was made, when the body of Mr. W. H. Dop, a well known local merchant of Singapore, was found hanging in an upper room at his premises, Amalgamated Motors, in Orchard Road.

Mr. W. H. Dop was of Dutch nationality and was born at Utrecht, Holland, on February 11, 1895. He was formerly connected with Boasson and Van Overzee, Singapore, Wilson, Holgate (Far East), Ltd., W. H. Dop's Trading Company and was proprietor of the Singapore Advertising Bureau. Lately he commenced the motor business in Orchard Road, known as Amalgamated Motors, and it was on these premises that he was found hanging.

Mr. W. H. Dop leaves a wife and one child, for whom much sympathy will be felt.

## DEATH OF OLD RESIDENT

Ipoh, September 13.—The death occurred to-day of Mr. William Frew, for many years local manager of Cobb and Co. He was last in the employ of the Tekka Tin Mine.

John W. Plummer, for nearly 14 years an employee of Stevenson and company, died in England recently of blood poisoning. A cable telling his death was received in Manila. "Mr. Plummer was the brother-in-law of former Governor-General Francis Burton Harrison.

Mr. Plummer was in charge of the shipping department of Steven-son and Company. A few months ago he went to England on leave; and was expected to return here early next year.

He is survived by his widow and one child. His widow before her marriage, was Gwyneth Wrentmore, sister of Elizabeth Wrentmore, who married Governor General Francis Burton Harrison. His wife and child were with him in England, and it is assumed that they were present at the time of his death.

Mr. Plummer was well known in Manila, not only among his business friends and associates, but also in social and club circles. He was a member of the Manila Club and before his marriage, was one of the leading oarsmen of the Manila Boat Club.
