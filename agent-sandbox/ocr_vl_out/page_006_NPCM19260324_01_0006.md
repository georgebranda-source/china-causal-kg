PASSPORT TO TRADE

Although written over three months ago and only published now in the annual report of the General Chamber of Commerce the letter from the Vice-Chancellor of the University to the Chamber is pregnant with interest. Commerce and education are sisters. One is the natural complement of the other. Trade not only follows the flag but it can be created and maintained by knowledge—a knowledge of languages, customs, and customers' temperatures and requirements.

In his communication to the Chamber the Vice-Chancellor quotes an extract from a speech by Sir Frederick Lugard in 1912, which is well worth reiterating:

It we believe that British interests will be thus (i.e. by the use of English as a medium of instruction) promoted, we believe equally firmly that graduates by the mastery of English will acquire the key to a great literature, and the passport to a great trade.

In summarising the objects and principles of the University Sir Frederick Lugard wrote about the same time that its medium was to be English, so that those who graduated in the University might be able to read for themselves the works in English dealing with the subjects they take and British influence in the Far East might be extended.

The most obvious source from which that passport to trade—and incidentally to that upholding of the name and fame of the British in the Far East—can spring is our schools and our University. Our schools are the natural feeders of the University, and the elementary education there imparted must be such as to fit students for graduating to the University. And, to quote the Vice-Chancellor once more, "the University, if it is to justify its existence as the only British University in the Far East, must do more than impart technical and professional competence. Its teachers must study critically the signs of the times, and there must be among them those who are capable of inter

preting the West to China and China to the West.

In the splendid work of the University's business men of the Colony have a unique opportunity of assisting in the only practicable way that business men can be expected to help—by a generous response to the appeal for funds to take the University out of its financial difficulties. The British members of the General Chamber of Commerce have already done excellently by furnishing a grant of $12,000 a year since 1921 toward the support of the Department of Commerce in the University. Up to the beginning of this month subscriptions amounting to "some thousands of dollars" (the exact sum is not mentioned in the Chamber's annual report) have been received by the Committee of the Chamber in response to the Vice-Chancellor's appeal. It is gathered inferentially that more money is still required and expected of the British members of the Chamber of Commerce. The Committee is urging British members who have not responded to do so "if at all possible." There ought to be no doubt whatever as to the effect of this appeal once the objects are fully understood and once the merchants themselves realise the immense importance to British trade of the work of the University.

## SUBWAYS

The annual meeting of the Hongkong Automobile Association rightly had its attention drawn to the inadequate parking arrangements on this side of the haubour. It seems likely that the Association will prove of assistance to the authorities in solving the motor car congestion problem. Pedder Street, Ch'er Road and Statue Square are, at the present time, to things of beauty: they are certainly not a joy for ever. Motor cars and cycles have to be parked, and the present system is doubtless the best in the circumstances. But the best needs to be improved. Pedder Street is somewhat of a danger—a danger to motorists and pedestrians. The same can be said of the space opposite the Post Office. Has the question of sub-ways been considered? Is the idea feasible? Would it not be better to have cars parked underground rather than be clustered together, in such numbers and in so close proximity as to be a "danger"? Failing this, should there not be some huge garage near the centre of the town for "waiting" cars? Arrangements could be made for cars to be signalled from a standard near the Post Office with the numbers of all cars registered. Thus the owner of, say, car No. 467, would simply press a button indicating that number, to have it signalled at the garage. Here is an opportunity for the motoring community to show its willingness to evolve order. Chaos is far from being the rule at the moment. In the circumstances of our narrow and congested centre district, those responsible for the control of vehicular traffic may be said to have done exceeding well. The point is can the present arrangements be improved?

The remarkable experiences of the crew of a trading junk which left Hongkong for Tam Chau with a cargo of kerosene on February 20 and returned to the Colony on Saturday were reported to the police last night.

Between Tung-Ku and Lin Tin, in Chinese waters, a volley was fired in the air from two fishing boats and the junk was ordered to stop. About 20 men armed with rifles and revolvers clambered aboard the junk and overpowered the crew with fishing nets.

The junk was then taken to Kam Sing Mun where the robbers commenced to discharge the cargo. Whilst this was going on one of the robbers shouted out that a Chinese customs cruiser was approaching them. They hastily got into their boats and escaped, having taken 20 tins of kerosene and property valued at $848.

The occurrence was reported to the Customs cruiser "Chung Kang" which came alongside an hour later and the voyage was continued to Tam Chau which was reached on March 17.

On the return voyage with a cargo of firewood, the junk received the attention of strike pickets from a launch, who fired on the crew and wounded a man and two women. The fire was returned and the pickets were beaten off. The junk eventually reached Hongkong without further incident.

## SPECIAL MUSIC

"TOURIST" ORCHESTRA

The Hongkong and Shanghai Hotels announce that they have secured the services of the s.s. "Francoisia" orchestra for a special dinner dance at Repulse Bay Hotel to-morrow night. This orchestra of eleven artists is under the able direction of Mr. Harry Woodland. Mr. Woodland is by no means a stranger in this Colony, this being his third appearance as leader of the "Francia" orchestra. The orchestra, during the recent tour has played at all the leading hotels, that the "Francoisia" has already visited. At New York Colombo and Bombay, the hotels had the music broadcasted. Their appearance to-morrow night will be the first time that this orchestra has appeared in Hongkong.

The X-Ray apparatus which it has been possible to install in the Alice Memorial Hospital through the generosity of an anonymous donor has been worked most successfully and has been of great assistance in the work of healing that the hospital is carrying on. This was referred to at the annual meeting yesterday. of the hospital over which the Hon. Mr. P. H. Holyoak presided, supported by Sir Henry Pollock, Rev. T. W. Pearce, Messrs. A. S. D. Cousland, J. Owen Hughes, G. M. Shaw, W. L. Pattenden, T. N. Chau, J. M. Wong, D. H. Cameron, Dr. R. McLean Gibson, Rev. H. R. Wells, Dr. (Mrs.) J. A. Fraser, and Mrs. A. MacKenzie.

The number of patients treated was below the average for the year owing to the abnormal conditions, stated the Superintendent's annual report. Another matter mentioned was the scheme for rebuilding the Nethersole Hospital which it had not been possible to proceed with as yet, also the retirement of an account of ill health after twenty years service, of Dr. Mitchell.

With regard to the new building, the plans were approved in the course of the meeting, the Rev. H. B. Wells, stating that the erection of the building would be gradual and the old building would at the same time be demolished.

Special thanks are due, the report stated, to all the members of the Executive Committee for the time and thought which they devoted to the problem of financing the hospitals under difficult conditions. The Hon. Mr. Holyoak, Chairman, Mr. Greenwood and Mr. Butlin, Hon. Treasurers, and Mr. Wells, Secretary, were untiring in their efforts to do everything possible to maintain the efficiency of the work.

The Hon. Dr. Kotewall has been appointed to the vacancy in the Executive Committee caused by the death of Mr. Chau. Mr. T. N. Chau kindly offered to collect the Chinese Subscriptions and has done well in obtaining so many subscriptions.

We are greatly indebted to Dr. G. E. Aubrey who has again given valuable help as Consultant, to Dr. E. P. Minnett who has examined pathological specimens, and to Dr. Li Sun and Dr. (Mrs.) Fraser who have lectured in Midwifery to the nurses.

The work which the late Mr. Chau Siu-ki had put in for the Hospital was also referred to with gratitude.

## Financial Needs

It was stated in the course of the meeting that the year ended with a heavy debt owing to a dropping off in donations. "The estimates for the year showed a deficiency of $4,300. The need for a larger endowment fund to provide a regular additional source of income was referred to. Referring to the financial needs of the Hospital, the Chairman said he knew this was not the time to appeal for donations but he knew there were many in the Colony who would give a donation without being hurt. He, the speaker, would be leaving for England before very long but he was prepared to assist the Hospital as far as he could in the way of collecting donations if he were required to do so.

His Chairmanship of the Board, said Mr. Holyoak, he regarded as a labour of love and he looked upon the work of the institution as one of the noblest in the Colony. He thought it was a work of self-sacrifice, and a magnificent one.

Commenting on the work of the various hospitals, the chairman said that there were 69 operations performed in the Ho Miu Ling Hospital, and 63 in the Nethersole Hospital. The in-patients of the former institution were 424, in the latter 528, and in the Alice Memorial Hospital there were 445, giving a total of 1,402. The out-patients numbered 426 in the Ho Miu Ling Hospital, 7,838 in the Nethersole Hospital, 825 in the Alice Memorial Hospital and 11,779 attended the Dispensary, giving a total of 20,363.

In conclusion, he congratulated Dr. Gibson and his staff on the work they had done for the hospital.

## TOO COLD!

BISHOP OF THE NORTH POLE.

HIS WIFE'S HEALTH

The Rt. Rev. J. R. Lucas, Bishop of Mackenzie River, Canada, since 1913, has relinquished his diocese owing to the ill-health of his wife.

Mrs. Lucas's breakdown in health is due to the trying life of the earlier days in the frozen north. Dr. Lucas, known to his brother clerics as the "Bishop of the North Pole," has a diocese covering 600,000 square miles, with only about 6,000 people, almost entirely Indians and Esquimaux. On one of his visits to London he gave some interesting particulars of his great "parish," remarking that

The diocese includes the North Pole, but I don't think an endeavour will be made to establish a mission there until we know our way about those regions better.

The cathedral church is at Fort Simpson, and to catch a train we have to start six and a half weeks in advance. The nearest railway station is a thousand miles away.

The transformation worked in our Esquimaux has been profound. Formerly they used to be dirty, dishonest, and untrustworthy. Now, before attending church every week, they insist on having a bath, no matter how low the temperature may be. They will meet snow in their huts to do it.
