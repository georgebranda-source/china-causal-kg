## CHINA'S FAUX PAS

### Hong Kong, Monday, Nov. 8, 1926

China has committed another grievous error. The Government functioning in Peking has taken the ill-advised and high-handed step of abrogating the Treaty of friendship, commerce, navigation, and jurisdiction between China and Belgium signed on November 2, 1865. As Belgium alone had the right to propose modification or abrogation, and as China declined to agree "to Belgium's request to refer the question to The Hague, it can readily be understood that foreign diplomats regard China's action as a distinct and serious breach of Treaties. Not only so, but China has deliberately flaunted the League of Nations under whose covenant The Hague Court was instituted and of the protocol of which China is a signatory. Thus, by a stroke, China repeats the now infamous "scrap of paper" episode of 1914 that led to the Great War. China, furthermore, has shown her intention of dealing with "unequal" Treaties by simply abrogating them at her own sweet will. Obviously such can never be tolerated by the foreign Powers who have contracted Treaties with China.

China, it may be recalled, made a terrific fuss over the question of securing a permanent seat on the Council of the League of Nations; and she still reserves the right to revive her claim whenever a vacancy occurs. China has been dinning into the ears of the whole world her grievances against the so-called "unequal" Treaties, and has lost no opportunity of presenting her case for their abolition. And still, whilst no stable Central Government exists and whilst the country is torn asunder by civil war from North to South, the Government—or semblance of Government, rather—functioning in Peking calmly and deliberately abrogates the Treaty with Belgium. This automatically abolishes Belgium's extraterritorial rights and places Belgian subjects in China.

in the same position as Austrians;

Germans, and Russians.

What China can do "to-day in respect of the Belgian Treaty she is quite capable of doing tomorrow in regard to her Treaties with the other Powers." Thereinlies the most serious aspect of the business. It is bad enough for one Power to have a Treaty wiped out of existence by a mere stroke of the pen. It is a grave matter to see the League of Nations openly flaunted. But the position becomes a thousandfold worse by the knowledge that China may repeat the mischief in regard to her Treaties with other Powers as soon as the opportunity arises.

It cannot be gainsaid that China has previously gained a fair amount of sympathy in regard to her claims for the abolition of the so-called "unequal" Treaties. There has been manifested a readiness on the part of the more important of the foreign Powers to review the existing Treaties—as soon as China takes the most imperative step of putting her house in order and shows that the time is actively ripe for new conditions in her foreign relations. Now it is to be feared that China has not only alienated a great deal of that sympathy, but has brought down a hornet's nest about her own ears. China has sincere friends among the Powers (including Great Britain) and all of these must deplore any action that tends to widen the gap between China and the outside world. There is, on the part of China, a lamentable absence of the "Locarno Spirit" of which so much has been heard of late. It is hoped that the latest blunder will be retrieved before real mission is done to the Chinese cause itself.

### REPLY TO "FORMER PATIENT."

#### OUR G. C. H

(To the Editor of the "China Mall") Sir,—In your issue of yesterday evening, your correspondent, "Former Patient," who criticised the Government's scheme of rebuilding the G.C.H., betrayed much ignorance and a perverted sense of right and wrong. I should like to take him to task on three points only.

1. He says that "by reason of its location in a densely populated Chinese district," the present site is unsuitable for a big hospital.

Any one who has any experience of most of the large, wonderfully equipped and well staffed London hospitals, will know that they are situated in districts no better if not actually worse than that of the G. C. H. I can vividly remember, while working in a large and well-known hospital in London, the smell of fermenting hop came up from the open windows. To one who, drinks beer, this may not be regarded as an objection, but it was certainly an unpleasant and “indelectable odour.” And that, too, was a hospital for Europeans, including the middle class people. But perhaps no one in London has the brain waves or the nerves of some of our Hong Kong writers to suggest removing the hospital to more ideal sites? For people in Hong Kong who love ideal sites, there are the "Peak, the Victoria," the Kowloon and other hospitals, and they need not go to the G. C. H. if they do not choose to.

II. Your correspondent also says that the present site of the G.C.H. is inconvenient "for the bulk of those who may be called upon to use it." In other words, the guiding principles should be the greatest good for the greatest number, which is a most laudable idea. But if so, does your correspondent know that the "greater number" of those who use the G.C.H. are certainly not Europeans? For these and others who are well-to-do, the present site may be inconvenient, but for the less well-to-do, the poor and needy —(and their name is Legion, in Hong Kong)—it is not, after all, so very inconvenient as made out by some people. To suggest that the neighbouring hospitals are adequate to replace the G.C.H. is to express ignorance of the urgent need for increase of hospital accommodation in this district.

III. Your correspondent also says that “either the Government should devote the Hospital exclusively to Chinese, in which case such extensive rebuilding would not be necessary,” etc.

I have noticed for some time the irresponsible and inconsiderate way in which some British people air their views in public. Some of these are well known public men and even leader writers who ought to know better.

For those who seem to share the opinion of your correspondent, "Former Patient," I would ask, is it any argument or excuse at all to say that merely because a hospital is exclusively for the Chinese, therefore any extensive rebuilding is unnecessary.

The revenue of this Colony is largely from Chinese; the trade is nearly all with Chinese; the people here are mostly Chinese. Is it not astoundingly courageous of your correspondent and others of his kidney, who earn their daily bread here, to state such views publicly?

For the lordly taipans and such like, there are developments in the Colony the benefits of which they largely enjoy, and must they grudge even the little that is spent for the poor and needy, the sick and the maimed — even though they are Chinese? The consciences of British people must have been terribly impaired by this climate if such views are allowed to pass unchallenged. I have had most pleasant memories of noblemindedness of British people in their own country, and I am sure, in the Press in Britain, a view like that of your correspondent, would have been received with a storm of protests and righteous indignation from other British people. It is a sign of a small mind to let prejudice displace reason and conscience, and in any case, no discrimination should be made in the treatment of those in suffering. The Chinese people have as much right as, if not more than, any others in having a decent, up-to-date hospital.

Let your correspondent and others like him, remember and practise this admonition, of which they must have often heard in their homeland——

“Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren (even though Chinese) ye have done it unto me,” etc.

Enclosing my card, and thanking you for the space.

Yours, etc.

## EPISCOPAL APPELLATIONS

(To the Editor of the "China Mall") Sir,—In the course of a letter published in this evening's issue of your esteemed journal, a correspondent refers to the "Lord Bishop of Victoria." With the greatest respect to the very eminent prelate at present occupying the see of Victoria, Hong Kong, the title is incorrect.

Only twenty-four bishops of the Anglican heirarchy are entitled to the prefix "Lord," namely those who have seats in the House of Lords. The Bishops of Durham, London and Winchester are always members of the Upper House, the remainder of the bishops of Home dioceses, with the exception of the Bishop of Sodor and Man, enter the House when vacancies occur, according to their seniority.

#### THE A.D.C

ATURDAY NEXT, THE FIRST NIGHT.

Reference has already been made to the fact that the A.D.C. is "giving four separate plays every night of their season which opens on Saturday next, and that the same four plays are staged on each occasion. The plays chosen present a very wide variety, ranging from a delightful Comedy by A. A. Milne to a piece of pure drama by John Galsworthy.

The curtain-raiser is a farce, pure and simple, that serves as the introduction to the Galsworthy drama. Most people have either seen or read Galsworthy who is prolific in both novels and plays. There is in fact, a new play of his being acted in London at the present time, and though the play that the A.D.C. is staging next Sunday is one of his shorter and earlier ones, it is essentially Galsworthian in all that pertains to this particular genius. In it, as so often before, what he does so well is to give a panorama of dexterously touched episodes, balanced so as to convey an impression of an impartiality that does not, of course, exist in art—in any selection by one creative mind out of the mass of possibilities. The thing has to be arranged, the lights thrown here, and not there, after careful deliberation. All that Mr. Galsworthy does, at his best, is to conceal from the audience that hand of the mechanician. The result of this masterly impartiality is that the audience is kept in doubt regarding the final decision that the principal character—a rising and already well-known Barrister—will take until the moment before the fall of the curtain.

On his decision depends the life of an unknown man, an outcast, unjustly condemned to death for a murder which he did not commit—but which the brother of the Barrister did—and weighing against this life, in the barrister's eyes, of doubtful value—is the honour of his dead brother with which he is inextricably joined his own honour and his whole future career.

## ALL OVER ONE CENT

## CHINESE WOMAN GETS A DUCKING

## AN ASSAULT CHARGED

It has been said that a Chinese never makes a good witness in a Court of Law. This may or may not be a "fact, but the patience of Bench and Bar was taxed to its utmost in Mr. R. E. Lindsell's Court at the Central Magistracy on Saturday when all the eloquence of the experienced Court interpreter failed to persuade a Chinese witness to confine herself to the facts of the case and the learned Magistrate, in sheer exasperation, ejaculated "Don't be an idiot. I have not come here to listen to a lot of nonsense."

The case before the Court was a summons for assault and involved an alleged attempt to extract an additional cent from a Chinese pig-wash collector at the Hong Kong terminus of the Eastern Ferry Company's Yaumati ferry service. Mr. H. J. Armstrong appeared on behalf of the complainant, Ip. Ho, a widow, and Mr. J. M. d'Almada. Remedios represented the accused, Man Lam, a ticket collector employed by the Eastern Ferry Company. Rescued by Friends

Outlining the case for the complainant, Mr. Armatrong said that his client was a pig-wash collector. On October 6 at 8:30 a.m. she went to the wharf carrying two buckets of "pig-wash and a smaller bucket containing scorched rice." She purchased a ticket for six cents and another ticket for her two buckets of pig-wasir costing two cents. She passed through the barrier and was about to board the ferry when a collector demanded another cent for her small bucket. She refused to pay the additional sum demanded and an altercation followed, accused striking her twice and ultimately pushing her into the water. She was rescued by friends with the aid of a pole. The regulations permitted passengers to carry personal belongings and his case was that the bucket of rice was such. The woman, however, borrowed an extra cent from a friend.

His Worship: If she paid, why did the collector detain her?

Mr. Armstrong: That is one of the points in dispute. I may say that I am not instructed by the complainant. I appear upon instructions from the Kowloon City Guild.

Mr. Remedios: I must object to that statement. I think it is only made with a view to prejudicing Your Worship against the accused. His Worship: As a matter of procedure, I do not think I can allow it.

Mr. Armstrong withdrew the re-mark.

Complainant then went into the witness-box and corroborated her counsel's opening statement.

A number of eye-witnesses were called and His Worship adjourned the summons, saying that he would hear the defence to-day.

## TO-DAY'S HEARING

At the resumed hearing this morning, Mr. d'Almada Remedios called a Chinese Police constable who was on duty in the neighbourhood at the time and who witnessed the altercation. A ticket collector was also called and, after cross-examination by Mr. Armstrong, His Worship decided that he did not require further evidence. "Frankly, Mr. Armstrong," said the Magistrate, "I do not think that the defendant deliberately pushed the plaintiff into the water. The evidence of the constable is corroborated. Whether the woman paid the extra cent or not, I have not to decide. I find no case for a charge of assault."

Mr. Armstrong: Would Your Worship consider binding over the parties to keep the peace?

Mr. Remedios objected to the application, submitting that the Magistrate having dismissed the charge against the defendant, there was no reason why the latter should be bound over.

His Worship sustained the objection, adding, “The defendant was only doing his duty. The complainant brought this trouble on herself.” It is her own fault. The charge is dismissed.
