## JOINT MEETING

London, Aug. 19.

The conference between the miners and the coal owners—the first for two months—was held at the offices of the Mining Association in London.

Industrial quarters beforehand were mildly optimistic. They drew encouragement from the fact that the Federation had decided to approach the owners direct.

The officials of the Federation had previously conferred with the Trades Unions Congress Committee on the subject of further financial assistance for the miners. The conference lasted an hour and a half. It is understood that both sides adhered to their old positions, the coal owners insisting on the eight-hours' day, and the settlement of wages according to the "economic position of the industry," and district agreements: the miners insisting on a seven-hours' day and a national agreement, refusing "to budge on the question of wages until they had definite guarantees regarding the reorganisation of the industry. FAT MAN'S DEATH

How chair coolies who, by demanding a higher fare, indirectly caused the death of a fat man during the heat wave in Canton, is related in a vernacular report.

The victim lives near the North Gate and had a daily to the West Bund. It has precluded from riding in a riches by his stoutness. Ordinarily he took a chair, and paid $1 for the journey. On Sunday the bearers wanted $2, pointing to his bulk and the oppressive heat. A bargain was not struck when the would-be passenger offered $1,60 and he decided to walk to his destination.

## HARBOUR SURVEY

## WHY IT SHOULD PRECED DREDGING

"ENQUIRER" REPLIES.

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"]

Sir,—I was interested in the reply to my original letter on harbour sitting, or rather in the Government view, as gathered on enquiry by one of your representatives. The records of tides and certain depths which he was informed had been taken since the issue of the Fitzmaurice report were not, as he was given to believe, part of the scheme of taking a general survey of the harbour, as recommended by Sir William. I have some knowledge of the subject and I assert that the only records taken were those in connection with works put in hand, records which would have to be taken as they were a vital part of such work.

Why have a survey at all? Why not get on with the dredging that requires to be done straight away? These are questions which rise to the mind of those unacquainted with this sort of work. But dredging done without proper data may—and probably would—be waste of time and money. A record on the lines advocated by the Fitzwilliam report would show where dredging would be attended by best results and might obviate the necessity of dredging certain places if the report showed it possible to direct certain currents to scour out such places by means of proper channelling and maintain them permanently clear without incurring further expense.

This might be possible in the case of the shoal off Kowloon Clock Tower and certain other places where silting has been in progress for a number of years. There are two shoals, however, which might be removed at once without any great difficulty by means of dredging and which need no harbour survey to indicate the way. These are the two off North Point—"Belcher's Ridge" and "Ramblers Shoal." The cost of removing these would be comparatively small and the operation could be performed within a week or so. At present

they constitute a great danger to shipping as any one who observes the manoeuvres ships have to adopt in passing between them can well understand. It would be a comparatively simple operation to remove them and make the whole fairway navigable at this point.

At present, although the carrying of pilots is not compulsory by law it is practically imperative in the interests of ship-owners with vessels entering the harbour that they should engage pilots. Such a consideration makes all the difference sometimes as to whether shipping owners direct their vessels to enter Hongkong harbour or proceed past it on their voyage. If it is only a question of fuelling or replenishing water supplies, the cost of a pilot may—and often does—determine them in favour of omitting Hongkong whereas if entry of the harbour without a pilot was a safe procedure they would direct the ship to enter here and Hongkong would get the benefit of certain harbour dues which it has to do without in many cases at present.

It is in the immediate—and infinitely more in the future—interests of Hongkong that a survey and improvements to the harbour are put in hand at the earliest possible moment.

Yours, etc.

Hongkong, August 19.
