## THE DEVIL BIRD

BAFFLING MYSTERY OF CEVLON

CHE PLANTER'S STORY.

The following are extracts from a paper read by Dr. Spittel at a meeting of the Coylon Natural History Society:

Always fascinating and alluring is the unknown. " The Devil Bird is on the many mysteries of our Island. Like the Singing Fish of the " Batticalou lagoon, it has continued to battle us. Long may they both continue to do so.

The Devil, Bird owes its nume not to its appearance which, if I know it, is lundsome; but to its voice, which is eerie and appalling, and leened only in wild places in the silence of night.

The human mind has a leaning to the supernatural; and one may pardon the extravagantly fanciful descriptions that have characterised the reports of most of those who have had the good fortune to hear this bird. Here, for instance, is the story as told by an old-time plunter.

"It was just before the breaking of the big monsoon. "The night was warm and close. I had been dining at a friend's and was riding home quietly and soberly along the bridle-path, in and out among the deep ravines. Suddenly there was a cry; then another still more piercing, and my pony, a brisk, black, little Pegu, corked his ears and bolted. He flew, and in the twist of a rivine he had me off like a shot. There I'lav on my back, kicking and struggling and crying blue murder. And all the time shriek followed shriek, curdling my very marrow; and the mountains shrieked in concertly until my cheek pulled and I began to pray.

It is the human note in these series that is their appalling feature; the one that of a lost soul, the other that of a person being garotted. What wonder the villagers in the localities the bird accustomed to hunt, hold it in holy terror, and its cry as of ill omen. So much so that if it be heard when a person is seriously ill, his relations will not bother much about him, but leave him to his fate, saying intervention is useless. They will run away from the sound, if it be heard close and stay hushed indoors.

There are scores of people in Ceylon who have shot the Devil Bird while it cried: only they are not the people who write about such things. I myself know half a dozen of them and the descriptions of every one were in agreement.

Only four days ago I asked a man who had spent a great part of his life in the forests whether he knew what the Devil Bird was like.

"It is an Engle with a crested head," he promptly replied.

"How do you know?" I asked. "I shot one while it was "hooting" and ogling on a large dead tree one moonlight night by my camp," he said. "I not only shot it, but ate its flesh to prov to my terror-stricken coolies, who ran away when they first heard the cry, that it was no demion but a bird." That is the kind of evidence I have repeatedly elicited from reliable informants.

With the foreigners in China—and the British and Japanese particularly—already experiencing great trouble in regard to the Shanghai strike and the general stoppage of shipping, the Bolshevists at Canton were not slow in utilising the opportunity of striking another blow at Great Britain through, the medium of the great port of Hongkong. It will be remembered that on June 12 the Yunnanese troops who had been in charge of Canton for so long were overwhelmingly routed by the Bolshevist forces, under the wing and guidance of Soviet emissaries, and that as a result of that victory a horrible revolting massacre of the defeated Yunnanese took place in Canton. It is well known that the Seamen's Union (of Chinese) which has a branch in Shanghai has its headquarters at Canton, and that the leaders of that Union are frankly and avowedly Bolshevist-inclined. It is perhaps hardly to be wondered at, therefore, that when the Bolsheviks assumed full and unfittered control of the Kwangtung capital they should take the first opportunity of adding to the trouble in those parts by engineering a general strike of all kinds of workers in Hongkong. The machinery for such a move was, apparently, all ready, and the strike commenced to take serious effect in Hongkong on June 19/20, and quickly embraced all of the essential and non-essential services affecting the Colony, including the most important of all, shipping. In a short few days the commerce of the port was practically tied up; all manner of Chinese employees, from workers in Government departments to houseboys, dockyard workers and seamen walked out, or, rather, went out under the duress of intimidation; and at the end of the month Hongkong, as Shanghai, was carrying on as best it could—and a very good best, be it understood—with foreigners (including Americans and other nationalities), doing their utmost and taking part in all manner of services, protectional, governmental and commercial, in order to keep things going. And so the situation is at the end of June: Strikers in all departments of activity in the Colony, trade disorganised and every foreigner doing his best to help to carry on. The Hongkong strike is wholly one where the forces of intimidation have been supreme. It is certain that not one per cent. of the men out have wanted to go on strike, or that they have any serious grievance against their employers, or the Hongkong Government, or the British people generally. It is simply a move in the gigantic Bolshevist scheme to get China under domination, to ruin foreign trade with the country and to abrogate foreign influence.
