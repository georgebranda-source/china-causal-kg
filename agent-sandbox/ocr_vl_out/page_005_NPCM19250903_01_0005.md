## THURSDAY, SEPTEMBER 3, 1925

## NOT A DULL CITY

## SIR ROBERT HO TUNG'S ADVICE

The thing that strikes me most forcibly each time I visit London is the amount of traffic, and the hurrying crowds of people intent upon business or pleasure, and the splendid manner in which those crowds and vehicles are regulated by the ever-courteous police stationed at all crossings, making it quite safe to wend one's way where one lists if only one follows the traffic regulations, writes Sir Robert Ho Tung in the "London Evening News."

Year by year I notice changes and improvements in this great city; some old landmarks swept away to be replaced by wider streets and splendid buildings.

If London Men Wore Silk.

Englishmen's clothes are as sombre as some of the grey old buildings which I love to see again and enjoy visiting. Perhaps if your men adopted a Chinese dress of flowered silk it might help to brighten the appearance of streets — but, notwithstanding, I do not find London a dull city. It is in my opinion a serious, a busy one.

Some streets and houses look gloomy, but others are gay and pretty, especially at this time of the year.

You must not change London very much, or else visitors like myself and my wife and daughter, who enjoy coming here so much, would not be pleased!

But to return to our subject, the everlasting, much discussed one of clothes. I think Chinese garments are more picturesque, warmer in winter, cooler in summer, easier to put on, and very comfortable for the wearer. They have one disadvantage, they make more luggage, for so many suits are required for changes of climate.

A Chinese wears satin clothes on all formal occasions. viz.:—a blue robe reaching to the ankles, and a black riding jacket which is quite short.

In the day time, and for tea parties, etc., our robes and riding jackets are made of any bright coloured silk, like English ladies' summer gowns. We rarely wear overcoats, but if the weather is very cold we have a lining of fur or cottonwool padding under our robes.

I do not see why Englishmen should not wear brighter colours such as we use, although in my humble opinion no European costume is more becoming than the frock coat worn with light trousers, and also the light grey frock suit with hat to match. I am sorry it is no longer fashionable to wear a black frock coat; the morning coat which has taken its place is a poor substitute. Skirts too long. Then too short.

English evening dress, with the white bow tie and waistcoat, looks beautiful, but the dinner jacket is in some respects better than the tail coat.

As I am asked to express my humble opinion freely, I think the tails are a little bit clumsy, and the pockets bulge in an ugly fashion if anything is put in them.

Ladies' dresses are so grand and graceful I cannot help admiring them. Sometimes I think the skirts are a little too short, and I wonder if the ladies' calves are not cold!

In China short dresses would be more economical, because we pay for clothes in accordance with the amount of material that is in them. So I asked a friend if short dresses are cheaper, and he told me "No."

Perhaps the ladies like them short?

But I think they put up with them because they are the fashion

And if the shorter dresses are not cheaper for the ladies who buy them they must be cheaper for the dressmakers. I remember when European dresses reached to the ground: then they were too long and I pitied the poor ladies having to hold them up. It must have been fatiguing.

London streets are very well kept and clean, in spite of the great amount of traffic there. But some of them are not wide enough and many houses might be more artistic; though houses may be beautiful within, they are often ugly outside.

TRUCK CRASHES INTO GARDEN WALL.



A motor truck owned by a Chinese transporting firm crashed into the side wall of the Botanical Gardens facing Garden Road yesterday morning, both the vehicle and the wall sustaining damage.

A Chinese who was seated beside the driver stated to the Police that the driver changed his gears on ascending the slope past Kennedy Road. The truck then ran backward, uprooted a gas lamp post, and was brought to a sudden halt by striking the wall.

The occupants of the truck escaped injury except for slight bruises sustained by a cookie woman.

A nine-year old Chinese boy was the victim of a fatal accident at a Lalchikok quarry yesterday while he was seated at the foot of a bank.

Finger-Counting After a Handshake

London people seem happy and healthy and often gay. I like the custom of raising the hat in salutation and the shaking of hands—though sometimes my hand is squeezed so hard that I count my fingers afterwards. In China we bow in salutation with the hands clasped, but in the Treaty Ports Chinese are beginning to shake hands too.

I like English food—it is whole-some and hygienic. But I think you—could—reasonably dispense with afternoon tea except for social gatherings, because three meals should be ample.

Also, you take your tea exceptionally strong, and that is not good for the nerves. Three or four times I have drunk tea prepared by my English staff and I felt giddy after it.

In China we take tea as a drink, not as a food. We drink it very weak when we feel thirsty. We never drink cold water. Our tea is made without milk or sugar, and is paler than amber.

English people are industrious and the working-class do a lot in their working hours. They like jokes and seldom quarrel one with another. I think they are satisfied with London as it is—and so, on the whole, am I.

In conclusion I must say how much I admire and greatly appreciate the unfailing courtesy shown to me, a visitor, by all classes with whom I have come in contact in this wonderful and great city.

# WALKED THE PLANK? FATE OF A TOWN.

THE WINGFUNG'S CAPTAIN.

Reading like an episode of walking the plank from pirate craft a hundred years ago is a report in the "Wah Tsz Yat Po" concerning the flagship of the Canton Government.

Two messages were received yesterday by the vernacular paper stating that the Russian commander had been thrown over the ship's side into "the middle of the sea." Considerable interest has been manifested in the unusual action of the vessel.

The Wing Fung is called a cruiser. One of several of its type, it is supposed to have migrated to Canton from Peking when the late Dr. Sun Yat-sen came south to establish opposition to Yuan Shi-kai's monarchy. Admiral Ching Pik-kwong, whose statue now faces down the Canton River on Dutch Folly Island, took the "First Northern Cruiser Squadron" down with him. All have since gone back with the exception of the Wing Fung. This boat has been re-named the Chung San in honour of Dr. Sun whose other name—best known to the Chinese—was Sun Chung-san.

Recently Russian officers were placed in command of 'Canton's fleet'. As reported at the time resentment was alleged. It is now reported that a mutiny has taken place and that the warship has sailed for Hainan Island which is in the hands of General Tang Punyan, a pro-Chan leader.

When sighted the Wing Fung was flying neither the Bolshevik ensign nor her commander's pennant. However, this may have been only a blind as the Wing Fung has previously been on expeditions to the south of the province, on one occasion taking back the s.s. Hok Canton as a prize for alleged smuggling. She may be on one of those errands now or her movements may be in connection with the more recent political developments. Another point—reported in yesterday's "China Mail"—has been the pronounced naval activity in Canton harbour this week, both day and night.

## GOLF CHAMPIONS

## PERTINENT FACTS ABOUT THE BEST

Now that the world's two chief golfing events—the British and the United States Open Championships—have been decided, it is just as well, despite the fact that both Championship Cups now reside in America, that golfers should recognise that they have been won by men born in this country and trained on British links. Americans may derive as much satisfaction as they please out of the fact that James Barnes and W. Macfarlane have developed their respective games during the past fifteen years or so on American courses, and that for national purposes they are United States citizens, but they have to admit, however grudgingly, that to England and Scotland is the greater gratification, writes the golf correspondent of "The Observer" in mail week.

In the case of Barnes it is a triumph for Cornwall, and in that of Macfarlane a victory for Aberdeenshire. This is a reversal of last year's results, when both championships were won by pure home-bred Americans — Hagen, the British, and Mr. Bobby Jones, the American. In view of the constant talk about the decadence of British golf, it is not inappropriate that the public should be reminded of the facts. Personally, I am just as delighted at the success of Barnes and Macfarlane as the Americans appear to be, so we can cry quits, at any rate as far as this year is concerned.
