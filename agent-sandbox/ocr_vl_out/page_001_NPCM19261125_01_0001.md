## MURDER TRIAL

Gruesome" Exhibits In Court.

A DAGGER MARK?

Shoulder Bone Shown to

Cross-examination as the resumed hearing to-day of the Western Market alleged murder case centred round a depression in a shoulder bone of the deceased.

The bone in question, with two others, was produced in court by Dr. E. P. Minett, Government, bacteriologist, who had detached them from the body subsequent to an exhumation carried out on Tuesday.

Dr. Minett, in evidence, stated that the body was in an advanced state of decomposition when exhumed and recognition by features was impossible. The bones were imbedded in a semi-tibial mass of decomposed material and appeared to be those of an adult male.

The body had been identified, said Dr. Minett, by the string used in the mortary for sowing up bodies and witness had then proceeded to detach the three bones in question. In the large bone of the upper (left) arm there was an depression measuring half an inch long, 3/8 of an inch across at its widest part and about the same measurement in depth. The soft inner part of the bone was exposed.

Mr. A. Dyer Ball (for the Crown): Do you connect that in any way with the dagger found on the accused?

Dr. Minett: It might have been caused by a weapon similar to the dagger.

Asked if he had anything in his mind as to the situation now of the dagger in question. Dr. Minett said that his impression was that if the depression in the bone was caused by the striking of a dagger," he would have expected the depression in the bone to be cleaner, to have smoother edges, if the point of the dagger had not been turned.

Asked if a point of a dagger would be turned by meeting a bone, Dr. Minet said he thought it would have to be very soft metal.

In reply to other questions, witness said that the wound in question could have been caused by the dagger in its present-turned state.

Cross-examined by Mr. H. S. Fitzroy (for the defence) witness said that he did not know anything about steel.

Mr. Fitzroy: Could the depression have been caused by an instrument with a sharp point?

Mr. Fitzroy: "Can you say if it is possible for the infliction of a severe wound on the shoulder blade, such as the prisoner bears marks of, to cause loss of memory?

Witness (after examining the large scar on the prisoner's shoulder): I cannot say. It might or it might not.

Witness added that the scar stopped short an inch and a half from the spinal column.

The examination of further witnesses is proceeding.

In view of suggestions both in Hong Kong and Shanghai that armed expeditions be sent by the Clinton Government to stamp out piracy, it is interesting to note that a brigade of Nationalist troops have again been to the fore in an intensive campaign against outlaws.

Russian on the Staff.

[From Our Own Correspondent.]

Päkkik. Konmoons Nov. 23.

Brigadier Chu King-tong of the 60th Brigade has won further laurels in his efforts against bandits.

Recently I reported his transfer from this district to Heungshan where he also did good work. On his coming back, he immediately told his men to Lai'Ngok, which has an area of about 50 square lias and comprises about 20 villages.

[Note: a life is Chinese linear measurement, equal to "about a third of a mile".]

By reason of its proximity to Kongmoon proper, Lai Ngok has been a terror for law-abiding people.

Nearly all the inhabitants are pirates, kinsfolk of pirates, receivers, or are otherwise associated with the nefarious calling.

It was the first time that the den was broken into.

Brizadier Chu was accompanied by his Russian adviser, who recently arrived from Canton. Both went into action.

Stubborn resistance was offered. Snipers hiding in the fields, or pretending to be farmers, picked off the advancing soldierry, who suffered over twenty casualties in the fray.

Anybody unable to prove his or her bona fides was summarily dethoth with. Houses were set afire in rows, and the work of devastation was done so completely that every one of the Lai Ngok community fled.

After that a systematic search was made and steps taken to prevent the pitates and their relatives getting back.

News of the successful repulse of an attempted piratien attack in the West River yesterday had reached the Colony by wireless from the armed launch which beat off the pirates.

It appears that the tow-launch "Mun. Tong," together with two lighters bearing a cargo of wood, was on the way to Hong Kong from Wuchow when "pirates opened fire near Kubo and called on the vessels to stop."

"The armed convoy launch, one of the many which are engaged" on anti-piracy work in accompanying "tows" and doing patrol work, replied to the fire very effectively and the pirates decamped.

## "One Man Wounded"

The wireless message states that "one man on board was wounded and sent to 'Kongmoon.' It is not stated whether the man was a member of the Chinese crew but it is presumed that this must be the case.

The “Mun Tong” is due to arrive in Hong Kong later in the day.

## The Die-Hards

London: Nov. 24.

The South Wales and Northumberland, Durham and Cumberland are proving the two most die-hard coalfields in the matter of the settlement.

While agreement is under way in most of the other districts, the Northumberland miners' group has rejected the owners' tentative terms. A complete deadlock has been reached in South Wales.

It is noteworthy that these are not only the largest colliery groups, but show the lowest proportion of defections.

Production Rapidly Increasing. London Nov. 24.

That the rapidly increasing production of coal is now over 2,000,000 tons a week was mentioned by Colonel Lane Fox in the House of Commons when announcing that practically all restrictions on the movements and employment of coal were being withdrawn almost immediately, except those governing export.—Reuter.
