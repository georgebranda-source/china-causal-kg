## TRADE RESUMING

## ADDITIONAL STEAMERS TO CANTON WITH MERCHANDISE

Further definite steps have been taken to resume traffic with Canton, following the settlement of the boycott.

Reports, on the whole, are not unfavourable:

In spite of the atmosphere of obscurity—as to their policy in future—created by the Strike Committee and the Kuomintang, belief is gaining ground that's a revival of trade will speedily follow the calling off of the boycott.

Actual figures show no immediate improvement. Merchants attribute this to Chinese characteristics and to the necessity of one transaction going through so many hands before completion.

Only from Swatow is there a report that pre-settlement conditions prevail. The pickets have left for (Canton but the local) strike executive is still intimidating.

## Spirit of Hope

Elsewhere there is nothing to contradict the spirit of hopefulness that has set in:

Discussion has been on three lines, viz., (1) whether both communications and trade will be fully restored; (2) whether if Canton does not collect the temporary surtaxes the boycott will be enforced again, and (3) if encouragement will be given to anti-British agitators to continue a trading boycott while communications are left unmolested.

Should the settlement be allowed to take its course, it is estimated that it will be a week at least before cargo, hitherto forbidden, is moving freely again.

Waiting for Dealers.

Take imported manufactures, for instance. It is not the foreign hong that ships cargo either on consignment or on own account to Canton for disposal. To send up for storage in foreign godowns would not be much use.

Dealers in Hong Kong—who hold stocks of some sort—have either to ship up old lots, or take delivery from importers and send the goods to connections in Canton who, in turn, place them on the market.

Until instructions come down from Canton dealers, the local native merchant cannot make things move. And the man in Canton prefers, to wait a few days before making a decision.

The Pioneers.

It is the same with exports. Inquiries come here from abroad. The European exporter turns to the Chinese dealer, who gets his supplies from another dealer in Canton. The Canton dealer collects his produce from up-country. When the produce finds its way into Canton again and dealers take the first step, the machinery of export will be in motion.

Briefly, the above is the situation to-day. Sampan folk in Canlon are beginning to carry foreigners, and Chinese to Shameen or the boats. This is done openly and not as a gamble in the dark as hitherto. Junkmen have also signified their willingness—in view of the sampans having set an example—to follow suit. Cargo coolies and shop fokis will, it is felt, also handle merchandise—as a few have been doing during the last few months.

A minority movement is on foot, however, to engineer "no intercourse" with the British.

Opposition Organisation.

There is no evidence whatsoever of intimidation at shops nor is there any agitation on foot.

An organisation has been formed to "differentiate cargo." It is supposed to embrace students and labourers but its extent is a mystery so far. They have appointed a committee of investigation to examine merchandise. Neither names nor regulations have been announced.

Risk and possibility of trading loss have also deterred merchants from making any appreciable shipment either way. There must be a demand for goods before cargo moves, said a leading merchant to the "China Mail".

Then there is considerable speculation as to the outcome of Canton's surtaxes. No report has come through that collection has started nor that the Nationalist organisation has been set in operation. Matters will become clearer when the order goes forth for shippers to pay the levy but Hong Kong firms are consoling themselves with the fact that the Custom status is the concern of several Powers and not this Colony.



On the proposed new taxes, the "Toho News Agency" reports:

The Canton Government has detailed its officials to the Customs House to collect surtaxes, but foreign Consuls here have not yet held their meeting in this respect.

## LULL SETS IN

## Canton's Modern War Methods

## (1) 

KIUKIANG LINE STILL SAFE.

Shock Troops and Artillery Fail

## in Uttensive

Cantonese efforts to take Kiu kiang having failed up till the present, a lull has set in on th Kiangsi front.

Definite news has at last come through that the northern Allies still hold the whole of the Kiukiang-Nanchang Railway running from the north of Kiangsi to the centre of the province.

An onslaught by the right (southerly) wing of the Nationalist forces, with Nanchang as the objective, was stemmed. Marshal Sun Chuan-fang claims that he has pushed back this part of the southern army towards Kao-on, which is to the southwest of Nanchang.

Another Drive Coming.

Apparently the centre and northern flank of the Cantonese are waiting for this southern flank to rest before making another attempt.

The northerners have not, it seems, followed up, this success by a general counter-attack, being content to hold their line and prevent the Cantonese from breaking through.

Both sides are still bringing troops up and indications are that the Nationalists will soon commence another drive on that part of the line between Te-an and Nanchang.

Advantage of Offensive. Hostilities in Kiangsi so far have had the effect of bringing the southerners much nearer to the lower Yangtse valley than when Sun Chuanfang's five provinces were first dragged into the war a month or so ago.

This drive on Kiukiang, Te-an and "Nanchang has brought the Cantonese closer to their goal, and has also relieved all pressure on other parts of the Kiangsi-Hunan front, where Sun Chuan-fang once threatened to cut the line to Canton.

The northern push along the banks of the Yangtse, towards Wuchang, has also come to nought, the Allied detachments.in that direction having been withdrawn to the home fires around Kiukiang.

From very reliable sources it is learned that the fighting around Lushan (heights outside Kiukiang) was particularly stubborn. Brigades of southern infantry were brought up as shock troops, being replaced by others as soon as their first force of energy was spent.

On a small scale, a preliminary bombardment was delivered by hill and field pieces, said to be manned by Russian gunners on the Cantonese side.

Concentration points outside Kiukiang and along the railway were systematically "searched" by Cantonese artillery to prevent reinforcements coming out to the northerners.

Machine-Gun Barrage.

That the Alines were able to save Lushan—which is regarded as the key, on land, to Klukiang—is attributed to the carefully planned system of trenches and defence around the heights, putting them in a favourable position to repel attacks.

A machine-gun barrage is also believed to have supported the infantry rushes by southerners on Lushan but this phase of modern warfare was not exploited to its utmost—else there may have been a different tale to tell.

In the interval, Sun Chuanfang is mobilising men for the front to combat the extra strength at the disposal of the Nationalists, released through the capitulation of Wuchang.

## SWEEPSTAKE ECHO

## -

MR. "FRANK HAYTOR" SUED BY PRINTER.

ALLEGED BALANCE OWING.

## 一 

There was an echo in the Summary Court this morning of the sweepstake on the St. Leger which was organised locally and banned by the police.

The Yue Shing printing establishment, of Queen's Road, Central, claimed from Frank Haytor (or Mr. E. Broadberry) $569.15 as the balance for goods sold, work done and material supplied.

Mr. Leo d'Almada was for plaintiffs and Mr. L. D. Turner for the defendant.

Mr. d'Almada said that the plaintiff firm was an old established one and had been engaged in printing here for over 40 years. The defendant was on the staff of William Powell Ltd. and had also interested himself in local turf meetings where he had acted as bookmaker.

Some time in June, continued Mr. d'Almada, Mr. Broadberry, together with Mr. Wheeldon, of the P.W.D., conceived the idea of starting a sweepstake for the purposes of benefiting the Hong Kong Benevolent Society and the Society of St. Vincent de Paul and in connection with the scheme. Mr. Wheeldon approached the manager of the plaintiff firm. The race on which the sweepstake was to be run was the English St. Leger on September 18, 1926.

## 50 ,000 Tickets

After finding out the approximate cost, Mr. Wheeldon returned later with a draft of the books it was proposed to issue on which draft terms were arranged. According to the plaintiff, the arrangement was that he should print 50,000 tickets for which he was to receive $750. The defendant wanted the books as soon as possible as it was proposed to circulate them in Shanghai and other places as well as in Hong Kong. The defendant and Mr. Wheeldon made suggestions on the first proof submitted and these suggestions were put into effect, plaintiff proceeding with the printing. According to the arrangement, the defendant or Mr. Wheeldon was to go to the printing establishment as they wanted the tickets.

It was very unfortunate, continued Mr. d'Almada, that in the tourise of the running of the sweepstike the police intervened and frustrated further progress, stopping defendant from proceeding with the sale. Defendant was subsequently brought before Mr. Nihill and formally fined $25. He promptly told the plaintiffs that he would not require the tickets but they had already been printed.

The defence, Mr. d'Almada understood, was that the arrangement was not in accordance with his client's suggestion but that delivery was only to be made as the tickets were required, plaintiffs to be paid $75 for each batch of 5,000. Defendant had paid $225 for those he had taken delivery of and paid into court a smaller amount for other expenses such as rubber stamps, etc.

## Verbal Agreement

Asked by His Honour (Mr. Justice, Wood) if this was so, Mr. Turner replied that that was the point at issue. He further stated that the agreement between Mr. Wheeldon and the plaintiffs was a verbal one.

Mr. d'Almada: My clients sympathise with the defendant in that he was proceeding with an undoubtedly laudable object.

His Honour, upon consulting the book of tickets which stated that the first prize was to be $40,000, the second, $15,000 and the third, $7,000, remarked that he saw that the laudable object was only to benefit to the extent of 10 per cent. Mr. Turner commented that he believed that this was the usual amount in such sweepstakes.

After hearing the evidence of Mr. Wheeldon and Mr. Broadberry, also the manager of the plaintiff firm, His Honour gave judgment for plaintiffs for $44.15 only (the amount paid into court).

Costs up to the time of payment into court were in favour of plaintiffs and after that date in favour of defendant.
