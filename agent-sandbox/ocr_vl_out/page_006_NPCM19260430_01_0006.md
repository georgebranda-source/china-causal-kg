## LOCAL CHRISTIAN EDUCATION

To say that missionaries are the cause of all the trouble in China—or that they are primarily responsible for the outbreak of anti-foreignism in China,—is a serious statement. If an extremist—one in favour of reform by revolution—made the statement, we might well dismiss the matter as not worth a moment's consideration. Similarly if a Russian Bolshevik or a Communist Chinese made it, not much regard need be paid to it. But the statement comes from a Briton—one entitled to be heard; one wielding a formidable pen and prepared to justify his statements. Leaving the larger issue to one side, as beyond intimate knowledge, except where direct contact has been made with missions and missionaries in China—does the statement hold water locally? There will be no question that it does not. Whilst there is a measure of medical missionary work being carried on in the Colony, and a large measure of evangelistic work in the villages of the New Territories, missionary effort is principally centred in education: Few know little or anything about Christian education of a primary character carried on in different parts of the Colony. It is of course not unhonoured, but it is certainly unsung. Day by day, teaching in the vernacular is carried on by devoted Chinese teachers under the supervision of foreign Christian missionaries. The larger schools are more in the public gaze by reason of the class of education taught and the influence they have on the lives of hundreds of Chinese of both sexes. The strike may have found school-boys in these institutions tinged with the spirit of nationalism and inclined to assert what we may easily describe as a feeling of patriotism. To ascribe any ill-effects from such manifestations to missionaries would be as unjust as it would be absurd. All the institutions we have in mind are staffed, as a general rule, by enthusiasts in a cause who devote their lives to educate and influence. The boards or committees invariably include Chinese who appreciate what is being done, and appreciate it in a remarkable and practical manner by giving and raising large sums for buildings and endowments. No school of this character is a closed preserva. It is open to the light of inspection and where Government grants are given.

## TRAFFIC CHARGE

KOWLOON INCIDENT.

An accident in which four ricsha coolies were struck by a motor bus and injured at Gas colone Road had a sequel at the Kowloon Magistracy yesterday when the bus driver was charged with dangerous driving and having inefficient brakes.

One of the coolies giving evidence said that the bus must have been travelling like a race horse. Asked whether he would return to the hospital after giving evidence, another coolie said he intended seeing a Chinese doctor, as he had no faith in foreign medicine.

The case was adjourned.

## SHALLOW LIES

PARTNER TO DELIBERATE

FRAUD.

CHINESE CONVICTED

At the Central Magistracy yesterday, Mr. E. Davidson called further evidence for the prosecution in a charge against a managing partner of conspiring to defraud the Him Yick firm of $8,200. It was stated at previous hearings that when depositors applied for the return of their money the accused put them off with a remark that the affairs of the firm had not been adjusted.

Mr. J. T. Prior, for the defence, said that another man was instructed to make the payments, but it was not known whether he had done so, as he had not been seen since the case began. There was nothing to show that the promissory notes were forgeries and he thought his client had been easily imposed upon.

The Magistrate (Mr. Lindsell) pointed out that accused had obviously lied in the witness box, to which Mr. Prior said that the lies were extremely shallow and he did not think the man was lying by art.

Accused was found guilty of a deliberate fraud and sentenced to three months' hard labour.

CREW SAVED FROM BLAZING SHIP

SHIRCO

The "Penang" which is a vessel of 870 tons gross, and was built as far back as 1881, runs between Singapore, Fort Swettenham and Port Dickson. Yesterday she left Singapore at four o'clock with a general cargo and thirteen deck passengers. She had a crew of about fifty Malays and Chinese, and there were 2,500 tins of kerosine in her after hold.

One of the most thrilling stories in local shipping history was enacted in the Malacen Straits, about 23 miles from Singapore, last night, says the "Straits Times" of April 23, when the Straits Steamship Company's "Penang" caught fire and was a total loss, the crew being saved by several ships which stood by.

At about seven p.m., when the ship was nine miles north of the Sultan Shoal light, the alarm of fire was raised, and flames shot up from the fore hold. The fire-hoses were immediately got to work, the boats slung out, and all other precautions taken, but it was quite impossible to check the flames. It is believed that the fire started among some straw, and its intensity was due to the fact that some Chinese sambu caught alight. It was evident in ten minutes that there was no chance of extinguishing the fire, and the Chinese, who had taken boat stations, let the boats away. The tackle became tangled, and the European officers had to cut the lines to free the boats. Had it not been for the fact that men were clining to lines and tackle in the water around the "Penang" Captain Ure would have attempted to beach the ship and he did in fact try to do so, but had to stop the engines to save the men in the water. The anchor was then dropped.

After a short time it became impossible to stay on the ship. The hant and fumes were overpowering, and the fumes were blown aft by the wind, so that it was impossible to get forward at all. The Chinese coolies had already left the ship, and since they had taken the boats the Malay crew and European officers were left to save themselves as best they could. Rafts and everything that would float were made use of, but some of the Malays had to swim until they were picked up.

Captain Ure and his officers told a "Straits Times" representative this morning that all the Malays behaved splendidly and especially John, the Malay boatswain who did a great deal of gallant work. The young second engineer and the Chinese steward were overcome by the gaps and fainted, and the Malays picked them up and saw that they got into the boats and throughout the Malays behaved in a manner worthy of that seafaring traditions.

Officer), and Mr. Anderson (chief engineer) remained on the ship until everyone else had left and they left the ship themselves about forty-five minutes after the fire broke out. In another fifteen minutes the ship was ablaze from stem to stern, the flames reaching to the musthead. Frequent explosions were heard, as the flames reached the kerosine and the drums of fuel-oil which were also in the holds.

The captain and chief engineer donned lifebelts and slid down into the sea by a rope from the stern. How the mate, who was on the bridge aft, got away is not yet known, but he was picked up safely. The captain and engineer, who supported themselves on a gangway, drifted four miles and were in the water over an hour before they were picked up. There was a strong current, and the men on rafts or swimming without support were carried along by it, but fortunately a small Batu Pahat coaster named the "Meena" also drifted with the current, and saved them. There was a fresh wind and sea, but fortunately, having regard to the danger from sharks, there was no moon.

Fortunately there was no lack of assistance, several vessels having left Singapore just after the "Perang." The "Meena" came up almost at once, and did most valuable rescue work. Her Malay captain had a boat out, and also picked men up on his own craft. The Straits Steamship vessel "Calypso" and the Dutch steamer "Van Cloon" bound outwards from Singapore for Batavia, also stood by. The Asiatic Petroleum Company, whose officials saw the blaze from Pulau Bukom, sent out the tug "Hercules," the Harbour Board sent a tug, and Captain Chalmers, the Senior Boarding Officer, went out in a launch. The blaze was clearly visible from the top of Mount Faber.

As far as can be ascertained no lives were lost. The two boats of the "Penang" were towed back to Singapore by the "Hercules," which also brought back the captain, the chief engineer, and the second engineer (Mr. Murchie). The chief officer went to Muar on the "Meena," and several others to Malacca on the "Calypso," but everyone appears to have been accounted for.

Officers Lose Everything.

The captain, officers, and crew lost all their belongings, and a considerable amount of money in the case of the officers. The first news of the disaster was received at Singapore through a wireless message from the "Van Cloon."

The fire lasted until 5:30 this morning, when the "Penang" sank, her disappearance into seventeen fathoms of water being witnessed by Mr. P. S. A. Georgeson, superintending engineer of the Strafits Steamship Company, and Captain H. A. Weldon, marine superintendent of the company, who were on board the Harbour Board tug.

The "Penang" was an iron screw steamer of 807 tons gross and was built at Leith in 1881. She was 201 feet long, had a breadth of 27 feet and a depth of 15 feet. She was engineered by Richardson of Hartlepool.
