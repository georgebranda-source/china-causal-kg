## DECAY OF KU KLUX KLAN

It may be rather difficult for English readers to form a mental picture of the Ku Klux Klan which was organised in the Southern United States four years ago and spread to nearly every section of this country, writes Mr. Foster, proprietor of The Housman Chronicle, in The Times. A brief account of its operations, and its gradual decline may, therefore, be worth while. For the entire period of its existence I have been in the midst of the fight to enlighten the people on the menace that confronted them, and on the intolerance that followed in the wake of the organisation and its efforts to spread the insidious doctrines of recital hate and religious bigotry. These may seem harsh words to apply to a secret society that many men thought would do good, and in which some good men enlisted. I have used every form of the written word to expose and ridicule its work. A few more, therefore, will hardly add anything to the venom that has already been showered upon me and the news-paper which I represent.

### ' INVISIDLE EMMARE."

The representatives of the "Invisible Empire" have already poured upon me the vials of their wrath. They have often threatened me with death; they have used every method of boycott, intimidation and character assassination; all because I realised the evils of secret and masked order that sought to dictate men's religious beliefs and to direct the political and moral conduct of those not identified with what they brazenly called an empire. That was what they organised in a free Republic—an empire with a throne, a kingdom with an emperor, a secret power dictating to an irony of obedient subjects and inflicting punishment upon those who dared to disobey its midnates, whether inside or outside the Order.

It was started by one William J. Simmons, a visionary, who was imbued with harmless ideas. He wanted a great brotherhood of white men who could resist the encroachments of the foreign element, and would purify and extol the race, especially the natives of the country. He forgot that America is new that all our ancestors were from foreign shores, only a century or so removed and that it was these men who had made the United States possible and powerful. He forgot that freedom of religion is part of our constitutional guarantee and that racial hatred could not be maintained in a country which owed its very existence to immigration. His idea made little progress until one Clarke seized upon it for a "get-rich quick," campaign. It was Clarke and his coterie of high-powered salesmen who perfected the organisation that made Atlanta, Georgia, its headquarters, and from that point established various realms, appointing men to positions of supposed honour, giving them a certain territory in which to get money for the Order and always paying liberal commissions.

## DIVIDING THE SPONS

It was very simple. The more members secured, the more money for the Atlanta headquarters and the more for the agents. The division of the spoils was very liberal. Everybody had a share in the initiation fee and yearly dues; but from $2.00 to $4.00 of every $10.00 collected must go to headquarters. What did they sell for $10.00? The right to put on a costume of white, resembling a nightgown, and to cloak one's face and head under a hood, with eyes glaring through the slits. They would meet in some out-of-the-way hill, or in some distant pasture. There were incantations and a semi-religious ceremony. They were "the chosen" of the white race. To each member was given the right to say upon his neighbour and to inflict punishment without consulting the law or the constabulary.

Every form of hate was preached. If you lived in a community where there were negroes, you were told of the duty of the white man to protect himself against the black. Hatred of the negroes was fomented where the fomenting meant more members and more dollars. If you lived in a community which included whites of the Roman Catholic faith, you were told that the Pope was his country's most deadly enemy and that the United States must be saved by Ku Klux Proteants. In California it was hatred of the Japanese that brought in new members. In the factory and mining districts it was the supposed menace of the ignorant foreigners that provided incentive to membership. Whatever hate could be engendered, the Order grew and flourished.

## THE "TERRORS"

At first it was looked upon by intelligent Americans as something so ludicrous that it was bound soon to pass away. Good men often visited my office in the early days to protest against our denunciation of the Order, "You are merely advertising it," they said. As the Order grew it became more bold. From meetings in secret, behind closed doors, it became militant and aggressive. It began to appoint "Terrors," as they were called, to whom was assigned the duty of inflicting punishment. If someone outside the Order was reported to be living an immoral life, the "Terrors" attired themselves in hideous costumes and invaded his home or waylaid him in some dark street at night. They would always go in a body of 12 or more and seize their victim when no one was near. He would be taken far away, stripped naked, and tarred and feathered. Then the poor, frightened creature would be placed in a motor-car, stirred back to the town and thrown out—a pitiful, shit king object for his friends and neighbours to gaze upon Sometimes there would be hot brinding or cruel mutilation. Several times women were thus seized and ill-treated. If a man resisted, he was sometimes shot to death. When the hideousness of the Order's became known, we sirove harder than ever to arouse the people to the danger of it all. At times it seemed difficult to make the best citizens and the law officers take action. A wave of fear spread, over many counties. Men and women whispered—they hared not act.



But the Houston Chronicle never stopped its crusade. We exposed every criminal act, and we obtained the names of the men who attended Ku Klux Klan meetings. We called upon our Governor to take prompt steps to stop this lawlessness. In this county we fortunately had a fearless head of the Criminal District Court and a brave Federal judge. They joined us in the effort on behalf of law and order. Some of the leaders were arrested. A few were brought before the Bar of justice. Some convictions were secured here and elsewhere. In one year a dozen of the offenders were sent to the penitentiary. That stopped the maruading parties. They were cowards at heart; and as soon as the prospect of penitentiary sentence opened before them they ceased to hunt the victims of their hate and intolerance. Some of the culprits confessed and gave information about their comrades. When that phase of the great game ended, they tried a new method of securing power. They entered politics. "We must elect our own officials," was the cry. For a year or so they succeeded in many sections, for they represented a well-organised minority competing secretly with the unorganised and seemingly indifferent majority.

## PUBLIC REPUDIATION

But it was shown to what extremes they would go if they had this power—that no man would be safe if the Klan controlled the offices. During the past year the people of Texas were thoroughly aroused to the danger that confronted them. County after county repudiated the Klan candidates. Our Attorney-General and our Governor were elected on a platform that denounced the Klan and its methods. One plan we adopted in Houston to curb them was to report every secret meeting that was held. We used one of their members to report for us. He was a spy among spies. It was war, and it was necessary to know what the enemy was plotting and planning. We gave a detailed account of the secret meetings. The name of every man who attended, if he were known to more than 100 people, was published. Every action was recorded. There was no abuse in these reports; simply a true and fair record of the actual happenings. That method of reporting attracted national attention. The Atlanta headquarters sent their ablest secret service agent to discover the Klansman who was spying. If he had been found, his life would have been the forfeit. He exposed them, not for hire, but as a duty, because he realised the inquiry of the Order.

They watched my office and my home night and day. Vengeance was threatened. For two years I was forced to keep an armed guard outside my office and around my home. They spread the lowest and vilest reports about me and the paper I represented in the hope of stopping our exposure of their methods. They even secured some workers in our mechanical departments as members, in order to keep a closer watch on our operations. One day the "Exalted Cyclops," the local head of the Klan, visited me. At first he begged me to cease our efforts. He admitted that we were injuring his Order—that new members were hard to secure and that old members were dropping out; that practically every man of any prominence refused to attend the meetings for fear that the names would be published. I refused to listen to his pleas. Then he began to threaten and finally, he left the office in a rage. Now that it is practically all over I look back on the past four years with some amusement, although there was tragedy in it. It all seems like child's play—that grown men would take such an Order seriously.
