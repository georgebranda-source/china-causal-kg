## COAL WAR

## FINAL RAY OF HOPE IN THE COMMONS

General Strike Formally Proclaimed at Midnight.

(1)

In the House of Commons an animated discussion took place on the coal crisis.

The Chancellor of the Exchequer significantly hinted that the Government was prepared to resume the negotiations if the challenge of a general strike was withdrawn.

Mr. J. H. Thomas asked if this meant that the employers notices would likewise be withdrawn, but he did not press for an immediate answer.

A meeting was arranged subsequently between the member of the Cabinet and the Trades Union Congress.

It was announced at 11.30 p.m. that the negotiations had failed. A general strike was proclaimed at midnight.

Notwithstanding numerous conversations between the members of the Cubine and the Trade Unionists in the House of Commons it was announced at 11:30 p.m. that the negotiations had failed.

A general strike was proclaimed at midnight.

STATE OF EMERGENCY.

The House of Commons opened with the customary questions. The tenseness of the atmosphere was apparent from the cheering, counter-cheering, and laughter as the various party leaders entered.

Colonel Ashley announced that the Ministry of Transport had arranged for a service of motor cars to convey M.P.'s to and from Parliament.

Mr. S. Baldwin, Prime Minister, at the end of questions, presented a message from His Majesty the King declaring a state of emergency. He moved that a humble address be presented to His Majesty thanking him for the message.

## Majority 200

### Mr. Baldwin's Statement

Com. the Rt. Hon. B. M. Eyres-Monsell (Conservative, Evesham), moved the adjournment of the House, whereupon Mr. Baldwin, who received an ovation from the Ministerinlists, made a statement. He declared that the occasion was one of the gravest on which the House had ever met. He reviewed the difficulties of the coal industry, which were largely due to its own extraordinary machinery for the adjustment of wages. That machinery required radical overhauling, which would be one of the first things that must be tackled when the atmosphere was calmer. The owners and miners must find, or have found for them, some way of managing their own business independently of a Government intervention just like other great industries.

The Risk He Ran

Mr. Baldwin dwelt on the risk he ran in negotiating up to the last minute under the threat of a general strike. (Ministerial cheers).

The situation had changed materially on Sunday. Certain overt acts in anticipation of a general strike and involving interference with the freedom of the Press, occurred between 2 and 3:30 o'clock on Sunday (Labourite laughter).

That made the continuance of the negotiations impossible. The Government found itself challenged with an alternative Government.

Despite Power.

He (Mr. Baldwin) did not believe that there had been a thorough-going consultation with the rank and file before this despotic power was put into the hands of a small Executive in London. (Ministerial cheers)

This was a gross travesty of every democratic principle. The Labour leaders did not seem to realise that they were threatening the basis of ordered Government

and going nearer to proclaiming civil war than we had been for centuries.

The only light hearts in the country to-day were those which either envied or hated us because they saw our whole democratic freedom entering on a course which, if successful, could be the only substitute for tyranny.

It was not wages but the very freedom of our Constitution that was impelled. Everything for which he had worked and cared for during the past two years had been smashed to bits, but that did not shake his faith or the courage of his aims. (Ministerial cheers).

## LABOUR VIEW

#### "Not a Revolution" Shays, M.P. Thomas

Mr. J. H. Thomas (Labourite, Derby) said that whatever might be the result of the strike anybody was a blind idiot who did not see that the economic facts of the coal position would remain. He did not believe that two per cent. of the population would support a revolution but people involved in the strike might be driven to circumstances that might have all the effects of a revolution with entirely different objects.

"This is not a revolution, but a plain economic industrial dispute."

Parliament could force an honourable settlement, and he would make a contribution to that where ever the opportunity offered.

## AN APPEAL

Mr. D. Lloyd George (Liberal), Carnarvon), begged the Government be make one more effort for peace. He said that the members of the Trade Unions Congress who ordered the strike were as little revolutionary as anyone there, and the Government could continue the negotiations without any loss of its rights or position.

He proposed that the miners be asked to open the negotiations and discuss wages, but not to pledge themselves to a reduction in advance.

Will Government Make One More Effort?

He added that in the event of the resumption of the negotiations the House would agree to a limited continuation of the subsidy.

## NO HOT HEADS

## CHANGE

See Reconstruction Programme

Mr. J. Ramsay MacDonald (Labourite, Aberavon) solemnly asked the members of the House in their hour of trial to raise themselves to a great triumph. He declared that they were faced with a situation the development and end of which none could visualise.

If the miners were going to accept a reduction of wages on the programme of reconstruction they must see the programme of reconstruction first.

He respected the Constitution as much as the Conservatives, and whatever their differences they ought not to enter on the dispute with hot hands, but with a determination to do justice.

## EMIGRANT'S ARMS

## BROUGHT FOR HIS OWN PROTECTION

POLICE COURT CASES

A Chinese emigrant was charged before Mr. R. E. Lindsell at the Central Magistracy this morning with the unlawful possession of three revolvers and 929 rounds of ammunition.

Tendering a plen of guilty on behalf of the accused. Mr. J. T. Prior asked for a lenient sentence. He said the man was a butcher in America and returned by the s.s. "President Taft" on his way to the country: While in America, he had heard of the number of armed robberies committed in China and brought the weapons for his own protection. So far as he (Mr. Prior) was aware, the accused had not been in Hongkong for 30 years and he had been away from China for 15 years.

Sergeant McDonald said that inquiries made from reliable persons have been satisfactory to the police and he did not wish to press the charge.

His Worship imposed a fine of $500 and ordered the confiscation of the arms.

## Cyclist Nuisance

The nuisance caused by push cyclists in crowded thoroughfares was mentioned when a coolie stood charged with pushing another coolie off the machine at Queen's Road West, thereby causing bodily injuries.

Evidence was not called as it was stated there was nothing to show that he committed the deed. He was accordingly discharged.

Alleged to be property stolen from a show case at Queen's Road East, a young couple were charged with receiving a part of the jewellery. Mr. M. W. Lo, defending, pleaded not guilty.

Hearing was adjourned until noon on Saturday, bail being allowed on $1,000 for the man and $2,000 for the woman.

Joss sticks wrapped in bundles and rolled in a blanket brought bad luck to the owner, a cook on the s.s. "Kinshan." Being too heavy for what they looked, the searchers cut the bundles open and found 14 taels of illicit opium hidden in the middle.

The old excuse that he was asked by another man to take the goods ashore did not satisfy His Worship, who fined him $1,500 or six months' hard labour.

### “A FIGMENT.”

COUNSEL ON TAM LAY'S "IMAGINATION."

"WONDERFUL STORIES."

The case for the defence of the second prisoner in the Wing. On Bank case opened this morning Mr. C. G. Alabaster describing the evidence "of Tam Lay, a crown witness as a "figment of the imagination" and as "wonderful stories" the evidence of Chung Sam, another Crown witness who stated that the second prisoner had confessed to him complicity in the alleged fraud.

Although the two prisoners appeared jointly, said counsel, it was the duty of His Lordship and the jury to enquire, severally into the evidence against them. The defence of the second prisoner was totally different from that of the first.

The first charge against him was that in conjunction with two other men he conspired to defraud the Wing. On Bank of $50,000 by means of forged books; that he did it in Hongkong (that was essential to the charge) and that he did it on divers dates not specified between March 1, 1923, and February 1, 1926. The prisoner's case was that he did not. It was not part of prisoner's case to say whether one or both books were either genuine or forged, continued counsel. He says he did not conspire in the making of those books either between those dates or at all.

The second charge against prisoner was that he (again in this Colony) in conjunction with another man, uttered, i.e., presented for payment, the book "Emma" on May 3 and thereby got what was known as the first withdrawal. His case was that he did not have anything to do with it. It was again no part of his case as to whether or not the books were forged.

The 3rd charge concerned the alleged withdrawals a day later. Prisoner denied that he had anything to do with the two later withdrawals.

The fourth count was that on August 14, 1923, he endeavoured in conjunction with the first prisoner to demand by means of the Industrial and Commercial Bank, the sum of $50,000 from the Wing On by virtue of the other book which the Crown alleged was forged in part ("Nina.").

It was not a part of prisoner's case whether the label of "Nina" was genuine or forged. He had nothing to do with it.

“Uncorroborated Evidence.”

The charges dated as far back as March 1, 1923 and some of the evidence dated back to October or November, 1922 and practically all the evidence brought with relation to the second prisoner consisted of the uncorroborated evidence of a man named Tam Lay who had stated that he joined the service of the second prisoner in October November, 1922 and remained there in connection with printing work in a shop in Canton until June 8, 1923.

During that period certain things which Tam Lay and Tam Lay alone deposed to were said to have occurred. The defence to that was very simple. Tam Lay was not employed at the second prisoner's shop in Canton and never had been, as he had stated, said counsel.

Such a statement did not depend alone on the evidence of the second prisoner who was to be put into the box, but would be corroborated. Tam Lay had stated that the first prisoner and another man had a printing business in Tai Shan Street, Canton, called the Chung Wah, and that it was a printing business from the time he joined it in 1922 and throughout the period in question.
