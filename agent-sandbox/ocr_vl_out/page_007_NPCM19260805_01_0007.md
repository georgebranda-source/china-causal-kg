## RUPEE FIXED

MIXED RECEPTION IN INDIA.

SOME DISAFFOINTMENT

 $ ^{a} $ The Curbency Commission port stabilising the rupee at 1s, 6d gold adversely affected the Bombay Stock Exchange. Particularly Imperial Banks (fully paid shares), which dropped 90 rupees but subsequently recovered slightly.

The report has had, if mixed reception by the Press. The Nationist Bombay Chronicle denounces it as "defrauding" India.

On the other hand the Times of India says that the report is excellent, but the report's proposal to create a Reserve Bank in India is criticised.

In Calcutta the European mercantile community welcomes the stabilisation of the rupee at 18.6d. On the other hand the leading Indian business men, interviewed by Reuter, consider this to be the most disappointing feature of the report. Reuter.

## EGYPT'S COTTON

PROPOSAL TO LIMIT AREA OF CULTIVATION.

BOLL WEEVIL RA VAGES

The Ministry of Agriculture, in a communique, states that the area devoted to the growing "of Sakellaridis" cotton is nine per cent. less than last year. The expansion of flower is inferior in most provinces in Lower Egypt to that of last year. The ravages of the Boll Weevil are more extensive and more shrubs have withered.

The inspectors have unanimously decided that it is necessary to promulgate a "law limiting the area for the cultivation of cotton to one-third of the present area."—Reuter.

## POLICE DISGUISED

22 DESPERADOES ARRESTED I. CLEVER COUP.

BANDIT GANG CAPTURED

Disguised as firemen but hiding bulletproof vests and automatic pistols under their firemen's attire, a party of Shanghai Municipal Police from Louza Station under Detective-Inspektor Conduit on July 26 pulled off one of the cleverest coups recorded in local police history.

"Distracting attention in this way and also by means of a fake fire, the police raided a house in Chogo-foong Road and captured 73 notorious bandits wanted for numerous crimes committed chiefly in Chinese territory, and not a single shot was fired. Other raids carried out in connection with this one resulted in the arrest of 22 members of the gang—of Zaoshing origin—and the release of a boy who was kidnapped at Hangzhou and held for ransom by the gang in Shanghai."

Terrorised For Months.

For months this gang has terrorised Shanghai, and although the police by consistent work arrested most of the members, sufficient escaped arrest to maintain the gang's reputation. Following up information, the Municipal police aided by Chapel detectives raided a house in Chapel on Sunday and arrested two women. Later in the day, another Chinese, visiting the house, was arrested.

This man furnished information which led to an unsuccessful raid on a house in Bubbling Well and then to the raid in Chaofoong Road, wherein the resources of the Municipal Fire Department were recruited.

Preliminary reconnoitering showed that the house next to the bandit lair was empty. With the landlord's consent, a clever plot was carried out. A bogus fire was staged in the yard, oily rags soaked in naphthalene being set on fire.

Other arrangements for fighting the supposed fire were also undertaken by the police and as soon as the alarm was given an old pump manned by a dozen foreign and Chinese detectives dressed as firemen rushed up to the house. Sure enough, dense volumes of smoke arose from the burning rags, and among the first people to turn out to extinguish the fire were some of the bandit gang, including the leader, who rushed out with a bucket of water.

With cinema like realism and a certain amount of professional skill, the pseudo-firemen got out the hose and jets and tackled the fire, at the same time taking the bandits by surprise. According to plain, the house was surrounded and the disguised detectives, who penetrated the bandits' hair, suddenly threw down the hose and produced pistols.

## Effect Miraculous

The effect was miraculous. The Landit gang were taken so completely by surprise that they had no time to resist. Pursuing the advantage, the detectives searched the house and arrested 13 men and women, as well as releasing the captured Hangzhou boy. Not a shot was fired, although the detectives were ready for any emergency. The leader of the gang was most abushed of all. The only thing he could do was to protest feebly that he didn't cause the fire.

Other raids brought the total of prisoners to 22, and they will be taken before the Mixed Court and, in all probability, handed over to the Chinese authorities for most of their crimes were committed outside the Settlement.

A search of the house did not reveal a single pistol, neither were any guns found on the arrested men.

## CHENGTU AFFAIRS

ATTACKS ON WOMEN BY

MARAUDERS

WRONG MAN STOOD TRIAL

### Chengtu, Sze., July 10

Although the murderer of Mrs. W. E. Sibley was almost undoubtedly isinner that is such an easy plea that almost any and every excess can be assigned to insanity. For instance, the day following that tragedy one of the young ladies of another Mission was riding in a riesha and was suddenly grasped by the throat by a student and dragged out of the vehicle. By the help of a servant and the riesha ran she was released from his grip and she jumped into her riesha. But again he set upon her, grasped her lightly by the throat and again pulled her out. She was with great difficulty released with torn clothes and the signs of marks and bleeding on her neck.

Evidently the fellow means serious business and regretted that he had no weapon with him with which to kill. He was arrested, and after inquiries by the Consell date was fixed for trial, but this was postponed. When it was held three Consuls were present as well as other foreigners. The trial is described as a complete farce. No attempt was made to get at the facts. Moreover, it seems almost certain that the perpetrator of the deed has been released and another victim, a confirmed opiuei, not who could not live long, was substituted for the original. This individual was selfbeped for eighteen months imprisonment but he is not likely to live so long under the conditions of the "modern" prison as seen in this part of China.

Mission School Girl Killed.

Another recent incident was the attacking of a young Chinese girl in the streets in broad, daylight, it is said that she was mistaken for a Mission school student, but this is uncertain. Nevertheless she was attacked and is said to have died subsequently. This is just in line with all too frequent murders of Chinese in this city, together with kidnapping of children.

Recently a tenant on the Union University property was very seriously beaten by soldiers because he had given some evidence concerning treatment by other soldiers. Instances might be multiplied indefinitely of the justice (?) meted out to the Chinese, most of whom are defenceless.

There is no love lost between the two great chiefs who at present occupy this city and control this section of the province. Recently one of them admitted to his army a large group of robbers and attempted to bring them into the city. The other great chief decided that since he holds the position of "robber-cleaning chieftain" he should deal with these robbers. Accordingly, as they were about to enter the north gate of the city to occupy one of the camps, he seized a number of their leaders who were subsequently hastened out of this life according to prevailing fashion. The rest of the bunch, who had not yet arrived at the gate, took to their heels and escaped. This action has increased the bad feeling prevailing between the two chiefs.

One result is the persistent announcement that there is in preparation a new political line-up in the province which will likely soon eventuate in a new group sitting in the seats of the mighty in this city. Such are the "kaleidoscopic combinations—and permutations" of the "political wheel in this enigmatic and distressed country. What next? is a legitimate question always on the lips of folk. The issues are certainly confused, so confused that they cannot be intelligently followed. So it is impossible to state what changes may occur in this section of China during the coming few months.

The forces of darkness are abroad, and it is impossible to forecast what may happen, next. Quite apart from the disturbing and confusing conditions, there are other uncertain factors entering into the general situation which cause a great deal of uncertainty and even anxiety on the part of both Chinese and foreigners. I refer chiefly to the persistent reports of the activities of certain secret societies whose existence and plottings are causing the powers that temporarily be considerable disquietude.

## Uncertain Factors

This is illustrated. In the disinclination of these authorities to consent that foreigners should go to the summer resorts for their usual recreation and recuperation. The Consuls have had rather anxious times trying to decide whether consent should be given. Anonymous letters have been sent to some of them threatening dire results to venturesome foreigners. Whether to take these and other indications at face value is the question.

## Union Université

Quite recently the Commencement Exercises of the West China Union University were held on the spacious grounds of that institution. The past year has been a very successful and a very happy one for the university. While other Missionary colleges and other institutions in other parts of China have had unhappy experiences the past was one of the most successful in the history of the W.C.U.U. Very happy relations between students and staff have obtained throughout the entire year. The students have been loyal, and that is saying a great deal during these troubles times in China.

Students registered very freely last September and have remained with the university "all the" year without any indication of ill-will and without causing any unpleasantness of any kind. There were not "even" as many, unusual cases of discipline as formerly. A larger number of students received either diplomas or degrees in the various grades and faculties than in any previous year.

Another interesting and gratifying fact is that the many students who have attended this institution and have gone out to teach, preach

affairs of life, are for the most part making good" and are demonstrating Toyulty to the university and to the foreigners who have had a share in their education. These students have not yet been infected with the spirit prevailing, in some of the other educational institutions in the city.

The Mission hospitals have for the greater part of the past year been full or nearly no most of the time and the patients come as freely and as trustingly as ever. Indeed these hospitals are almost certain to break all previous statistical records by a big margin. This seems to indicate that the demonstration of ill-feeling towards foreigners is not very widespread, but is confined to a relatively few victims of some sinister agitation.

Certainly the right-thinking people of this city in great numbers have expressed their deep chagrin and shame over the unfortunate murder of Mrs. Sibley. Quite a number of better class Chinese friends who are not at all related to any Christian church were brave enough and kind enough to come to foreign. compounds on the occasion of the murder to express their profound regrets and sympathy. It is such manifestations as these that enable foreigners to live and to work hopefully in these far interior parts of China.—"N.C. Daily News"

## SANITATION

PLAGUE OUTBREAK CAUSES GREAT ALARM.

The discovery of cases of plague n. Yokohama and Osaka and of suspected causes in the suburbs of Tokyo has caused alarm amongst the general public.

The press is devoting a good proportion of its space to arousing citizens to the necessity of proper sanitation and to awakening them from their laissez faire attitude. Japan is yet unfortunately afflicted with contagious diseases that could be easily eradicated, provided proper sanitation measures are resorted to.

While the nation has made giant strides in many directions, sanctions, however, has been woefully neglected. Open sewers, into which are dumped indiscriminately all manned of refuse, from street sweepings to night-soil carts, form a festering sore in Japan's principal cities. The outer moat of Tokyo is really a veritable cess-pool, especially when the heat of the summer causes the stagnant vegetation and other offal in it to fester and rot. The disgusting smells of decomposing off-scourrings that "assail one's nostrils tell a dangerous tale of potential epidemics, but the olfactory nerves of the Japanese, or their sense of civic pride, seem to be terribly at fault. Najune to, be terribly at fault. No one policeman will lazily watch, from the shelter of his police-box, a coolie drugging a cart full of dross and dump it into a disused well, the moat (which is most preferred) or any large-sized drain.

It is only when a major epidemic threatens that there is a certain amount of essays written and orations delivered on the supreme necessity of sanitation in crowded cities. In Yokohama, now that the hot weather is definitely on, one can discern a distinct colour generally. Be found in grave yards where the dead have not been interred deep enough.

The cities of Japan are nearly defenceless against infectious diseases, and organised bodies, such as the Health Department, seem to be doubtful as to their ability to eradicate these diseases. One great drawback is that cities here are badly built. They really were never conceived with any definite scheme in view, but like Topsy in "Uncle Tom's Cabin" "just growed." According to investigations just made by the Home Office, there are no less than fourteen slum districts in the six principal cities of Japan. The population of these slum districts is estimated at about 100,000 and these districts house nearly every variety of contagious disease.

Commenting on the disgraceful lack of sanitation in Japan, the “Osaka Asahi,” a paper with a circulation that embraces nearly a million readers, declares editorially:

"The sanitary organisation" in cities of Japan is lamentably backward. Our cities are glaringly backward in sanitary measures to prevent disease. The drainage system is imperfect and citizens suffer from difficulties in the disposition of filth. Some of our countrymen are not better than the natives of the South Sea islands in this respect. The existence of un-wholesome districts and dirty houses huddled together is a shame to the country, and if things, be left as they are, the national health will deteriorate. Citizens are in terror every time the hot weather comes around. Such a defect in their life should be radically uprooted."—"N.C. Daily News."
