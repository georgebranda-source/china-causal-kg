## MIDDLESEX WIN

LIGHT BLUES DO WELL AT THE OVAL.

J. B. Hobbs has scored another two individual centuries in a first class match. He now has 122 centuries to his credit as against 110 at the end of last season. Four of the last twelve were made in Australia. This year, he made five centuries in seven matches and then slacked off a bit. Earlier in the week, he played another three-figure innings and in the latest results to hand, he is given as having made two centuries against Cambridge University at the Oval. Dr. W. G. Grace has the highest number of centuries to the credit of any batsman in first-class cricket, namely, 126. Hobbs, therefore, requires 4 to tie and 5 to establish a new record. Judging by the way he is going, he may accomplish the feat this season.

In spite of Hobbs's many runs, Surrey lost, the Light Blues pulling off a glorious victory by six wickets. - Surrey were 165 runs ahead on the first innings but did not enforce the follow-on and declared their second innings closed with 7 wickets down. - Cambridge needed 426 to win and made 427 for the loss of 4 wickets.

In the county championship, Middlesex gave further proof of their batting strength by overwhelming Kent at Tonbridge.

Yorkshire continue their winning sequence and appear as if they will win the championship for the fourth time in succession as they are far ahead of their nearest rivals. Lancashire drop two more points through Essex putting up determined resistance. Essex have proved themselves the Cinderellas of the competition this year. Sussex are coming into their own again. ## DAVIS CUP

COMMENT ON CURRENT PROSPECTS.

## SILVER JUBILITE

In the original conditions for the Davis Cup there was a clause which stipulated that if at any time five years should elapse without a challenge the trophy should be returned to the donor. The superstitions might see in that provision, which was framed in 1900, a prophetic reference to the world war. In point of fact, though the prosperous development of the Davis Cup has long since revised the first charter, the trophy did remain in America during the war. Australia had won it back at Forest Hills in August, 1914, after McLoughlin had beaten both Brookes and Wilding, but neither victor nor vanquished at that time wished to expose so precious a symbol to, the risk of a voyage across the seas.

This year the Davis Cup celebrates its silver anniversary, and, by fortune's happy chance, there were twenty-five different countries competing for its possession. That is a record for the international championship; it is also a proof that nomination can now afford to organise its lawn tennis on lines of "splendid isolation." It must think in terms of continents; it is not enough to think in terms of countries.

Compared with results to date, his predictions have come out right. In part he says:

I am not going to prophesy what will happen in the European zone. France must start as the favourite by virtue of her handsome victory last year. She won every tie without the loss of a match, although England won a match at Eastbourne after the result was declared. France cannot call upon Cochet this year; she may not feel his absence with Borotna and Lacoste in the van and Brignon and Ferét just behind. Still her second round tie against Italy is no certainty, for Morpurgo has beaten Borotra recently as last July, amid the doubles has an element of speculation about it. But, assuming a French victory, as I think we may, France ought to reach the final again. I am disposed to think that the winner of the Noordwijk tie will ultimately oppose France in that round. The Indian team is well balanced, but scarcely aggressive enough; the Swiss are aggressive enough, but not well balanced. Neither Austria nor Ireland can hope to go far this year. The absence of Spain is explained on the ground that she has entered in the American zone, in which, by a curious coincidence, she has drawn Cuba.

The real "rub" of the Davis Cup will take place this year, as last, in America. If history repeats itself, France will meet Australia at Boston in the inter-zone round on September 3, 4, and 5. The winner of this tie will challenge America at Philadelphia on September 10, 11, and 12. In other words, for France to win the Davis Cup for the first time, Rorotra and Tacoste must first beat Anderson and Patterson and then Tilden and Johnston. The task is not beyond their imagination nor their courage; it remains to be seen whether it is beyond their strokes.

SUCCESSFUL EFFORT AT THE UNIVERSITY.

## TODAY

From three p.m. to ten p.m. are very comprehensive hours, but the whole time would well be spent at the bazaar at the University, inspecting stalls stocked with a choice variety of goods and enjoying the excellent variety of games and competitions.

That, at any rate, was apparently the opinion of those attending the initial day's proceedings yesterday which were opened by Sir Claud Severn who reminded his hearers that the proceeds were to go to the Building Fund of St. Stephen's College. A site had been granted at Stanley on condition that a certain amount was raised in a given period. A large amount had been raised but a lot still remained to be secured. In formally declaring the bazaar open Sir Claud Severn congratulated all who were instrumental in decorating and arranging the various stalls and anticipated that the function would realise quite a large sum for the building fund.

The Principal of the College, the Rev. W. H. Hewitt, responded on behalf of the students and Mr. Li Chor-chi, an old boy and secretary of the Bazaar Committee of the College, presented Sir Claud with a Chinese engraving on glass as a memento of the occasion.

Sir Henry Pollock presided over the opening ceremony, among others present being Lady Pollock, Mr. E. B. C. Hornell and Mr. W. L. Pattenden.

Quite a brisk business was done at the stalls, most of which were under the superintendence of separate College forms but there is much yet left for intending purchasers this afternoon. The articles are useful and ornamental suitable for use in the home or for curios to be sent to friends.

On the tennis courts are competitions of all kinds and a sliding shoot, but most popular of all are shooting ranges with miniature rifles, in connection with all of which prizes are to be won. In convenient corners are to be found drink and refreshment stalls and sit down teas may be obtained at any time. This and all arrangements for refreshments are in the hands of ladies from St. Stephen's Girls' School who were also responsible for the provision of the good sold.

The services of talented amateur performers had been obtained for a concert held in the evening together with a cinema entertainment which proved a great success. Evening performances are also promised for to-night.

#### V. R. C

## LAST YEAR'S GOOD WORK

## FINANCES STABILISED

The progress made as regarded the swimming activities of the Club and the use of the library, also the success which attended the holding of the Boxing tournament, were the subject of congratulatory comments by Sir Claud Severn, the Chairman, at yesterday's annual general meeting of the Victoria Recreation Club.

The Chairman said that he hoped the ensuing year would see still further development, particularly as regarded rowing which had not been taken up so enthusiastically. It was, however, a great source of satisfaction that their veteran, Mr. Cooke, had won the harbour race and considering that they had beaten the Japanese Olympic representatives in the team race they might say with reason that they had swimmers in the Club almost up to Olympic standard.

The death of three members (Mr. W. G. Dowse, Mr. A. A. Claxton and Mr. R. Hutchinson) was referred to with regret in the course of the meeting.

The financial position was stated to be satisfactory considering that they were in a period of transition regarding it. A sum of $236 had had to be written off as not recoverable from the No 2 Boy but the danger of a recurrence was obviated as all the boys were now under security.

On the proposition of Mr. P. W. Ramsay it was agreed that it should be left to the annual meeting to vote the honorary secretary and honorary treasurer a yearly honorarium instead of monthly amounts being voted.

Mr. R. C. Mitchell (honorary secretary) and Mr. J. Smith (honorary treasurer) were thanked for their services by the Chairman and both were re-elected, the former on a ballot as a result of which he received 19 votes and Mr. J. Stewart 12.

Sir Claud Severn was re-elected Chairman and returning thanks said that it was probably the last year which he would be able to do not that position.

The General Committee was elected as follows: Messrs. A. A. Alves, J. Stewart, G. J. Cooke, W. Logan, P. W. Ramsay, W. S. Bailey, J. Lyon, A. Silva-Netto and D. Lyon.

## PRISON FOR MOTORIST

## CONVICTED OF WANTON DRIVING

MAGISTRATES SERIOUS VIEW

Convicted of driving wantonly and furiously outside Repulse Bay Hotel, Mr. Howard T. Werschkul, a young American flour merchant, was yesterday sentenced by Mr. S. B. B. McElderry at the Central Magistracy to two month's hard labour.

On June 6, a car driven by the defendant knocked down and seriously injured an Indian watchman in the employ of the Hotels Company.

After Mr. Laitle, one of the occupants of the car, had given evidence at the continued hearing yesterday afternoon, Mr. M. H. Turner who appeared for the defence, addressed the Bench and said that the sum and substance of the contentions of the prosecution was that the defendant had driven round the entrance to the Hotel for a joy ride and then went round again. He would submit that the evidence was not definite and that the car had been up the delivery path.

The Magistrate interposed that he was satisfied that this was what actually had taken place.

O) the question of speed, Mr. Turner commented that it was impossible to drive at more than 15 miles an hour along the drive passing the hotel after turning round the corner from the delivery entrance. Regarding the striking of the Indian watchman by the car, Mr. Turner said that before His Worship could believe the defendant saw the Indian he would have to come to the conclusion that he deliberately told the occupants lies when he felt the bump and when they asked what had happened.

Finally, Mr. Turner said that he agreed that defendant should not have gone past the Hotel at the speed he did but he could not agree that the driving of the car was wanton and furious. On the question of compensation his client was willing to pay.

In passing sentence the Magistrate said it was necessary for him to take a serious view of any such case that came before him. He could not do less than pass that sentence. He agreed that the defendant did not see the Indian and said that he was taking into consideration all the points raised by Mr. Turner.

The prosecution throughout was conducted by Inspector Kent.

The reocinding of the old prison rules and the substitution of new ones is announced in the current number of the Government Gazette.

The laws relating to a prisoner under warrant or order for execution provide that he shall, immediately on his arrival in the prison or after sentence, be thoroughly searched, and any articles which it is considered dangerous or inexpedient for him to retain in his possession shall be taken from him.

Every prisoner under sentence of death shall be confined in a cell apart from all other prisoners, and shall be placed by day and by night under the constant charge and observation of a prison officer.

The cell or room in which a prisoner condemned to death is placed shall be previously examined by the Assistant Superintendent who shall satisfy himself of its fitness and record the result of his examination in his Journal.

## CORRESPONDENCE

## FOOTPATH BOANDAL

(Tó tho Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—I am more than pleased to see in your valuable paper, a criticism of the disgraceful mined of some of our public footpaths. The particular footpath I have in mind, is the one from Causeway Bay, to the British Victoria Schools. In spite of complaints to Sanitary Board Department officials, Police officers, and a letter to the press, this road still remains what it has been for the past three years, i.e.: — a deathrap for children, and a disgrace to the Colony.

Anyway we will hope that your campaign against the misuse of footpaths will improve itself on the "powers that be." and that an in-quest on a European child is not necessary before the sidewalks of Caroline Road are made fit to walk

During the past two or three weeks, the P.W.D. have added their little quotas to the miscellaneous collection of "junk" which ornaments the footpath, by stacking on the path, two tar boilers a heap of gravel, and a collection of pickles and shovels. I notice this week, that the P.W.D. are busy playing "put and take" again, with the loose earth and sand which they have heaped up on what was originally a footpath opposite the Victoria Schools. Every heavy downpour of rain washes the sand on to the road; the next day cools arrive and carry it back again to the side.
