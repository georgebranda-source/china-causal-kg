## AT GENEVA

DEPARTURE OF BRITISH DELEGATION.

PRIVATE CONVERSATIONS

Rugby, March 6.

Sir Austen Chamberlain, Lord Cecil, and the "other principal members of the British delegation to the League of Nations left London this morning, and it is understood that the French delegation will join them this evening in Paris.

Delegations from other Capitals are already reported to have set out for Geneva, and the representatives of the principal European Powers on the League Council will be there in ample time to participate to-morrow in the private conversations suggested by Sir Austen Chamberlain as a means of removing misunderstandings prior to the formal meeting of the Council on Monday.

Chamberlain and Briand.

Ruzhy March 7

Siri Austen Chamberlain, head of the British delegation to Geneva, was met on his arrival in Paris by Monsieur Briand and Lord Chewe (British Ambassador).

M. Briand and Sir A. Chamberlain immediately drove to the British Embassy, where they conversed for half-an-hour.

Sir A. Chamberlain, after dining at the Embassy, proceeded on his journey to Geneva, with his delegation, members of the French and some other delegations proceeding by the same train.

It is considered unlikely that M. Briand will be able to remain in Geneva for the meeting of the Council of the League to-morrow owing to the disturbed political situation in France caused by the resignation of his Cabinet following the defeat in the Chamber early yesterday morning.

Satisfaction is, however, expressed here that the French crisis has not prevented M. Briand from proceeding to Geneva and participating in the highly important informal meeting there to-day between the leading signatories of the Locarno Treaty.

The belief is strongly held that these conversations may clear the difficulties from the way of achieving the primary object for which this week's session of the League of Nations has been convened, namely, the admission of Germany to the League with a permanent seat on the Council.

## FIRST COMMUNIQUE

A communique issued after the meeting stated that no difficulty was experienced in making clear their reciprocal positions, and adding that this first exchange of views will be followed by French conversations.

Prior to the League's meeting representatives of the countries which met at Locarno to negotiate the Rhineland Pact met to exchange views on the questions on the agenda.

Free, Full and Frank.

Sir A. Chamberlain subsequently stated: "We had a free, full, and frank talk as at Locarno. We covered the whole ground but did not find immediately a solution of all our troubles. Nothing could be decided to-day as we could be bound by nobody but ourselves. Doubtless before we next meet for a further exchange of views we shall find some method of settling our troubles, but we have not done so yet. We have cleared up some misapprehensions and suspicions." He added that they were trying to make the entry of Germany into the League absolutely non-controversial. If, by waiting a day or two, they could assure this result, surely it was better to wait.

Geneva, March 7.

Reuter learns from a German source that a difficulty in the private conversations this afternoon arose from the fact that the German delegation would not countenance an enlargement of the Council before Germany was given a permanent seat, but "certain other delegations" insisted that Spain should be allotted a permanent seat simultaneously with Germany.

M. Briand left for Paris tonight but it is hoped that he will return on Tuesday.

## INNOCENT PARTIES

## 2 

TENANT WHO SUB-LET WITH-OUT RIGHT.

AND REASON.

"This seems to be one of those cases in which the decision will probably be right and the reasons probably wrong." said Mr. Justice Woodi, in a Summary Court case this morning.

The matter upon which he was called to decide was the right of a tenant who had gone into premises under an agreement with the previous tenant whereas that previous tenant was under agreement with the landlord not to assign the premises without the landlord's permission.

The landlord claimed possession of the tenement in question, No. 19. Gilman Bazaar.

Mr. D. McCallum, for the new tenants, admitted that under common law the landlord was entitled to possession as there had been a breach of the agreement of the original tenant. It was clear, however, that the landlord had no objection to the present tenants which could hold water. Under the Ordinance he could not get more money in rent. The present tenants were innocent parties and their only faith had been that they had not made the fullest enquiries as to the type of tenancy. It must be remembered that they were Chinese dealing with British law. He claimed that under the Ordinance, the new tenants were entitled to the discretionary protection of the court.

Mr. M. M. Watson, for the landlord, claimed that they had no right to protection at all. They were “trespassers” in that they had never been accepted as tenants by the landlord and as the landlord had asserted his rights by the issue of the writ their claim even to be statutory tenants would fail from that time forward. As regarded the claim that the landlord could not get more money in rent, it was right to be told not if the premises remained as one tenancy but it might be his wish to make it into two.

His Lordship indicated that an order for possession would have to be made but the terms of the stay he would consider.

## WANCHAI MURDER

## DUMPING DEAD BODY IN THE STREET

CHINESE GET FREE

The arrest of a Chinese alleged to be the murderer of a coolie at Wanchai resulted in the charge against two others being amended to one of moving the dead body without permission.

Defendants, who admitted that they dumped the dead body in the street, were bound over by Mr. R. E. Lindsell at the Central Magistracy this morning on personal bonds to come up for judgment when called upon within six months.

## MYSTERY SHIP

"DURBAN" GOES OUT AT MIDNIGHT.

THE PIRACY SCARE.

Immediately on receipt of a message that an irregularity in shipping might have been due to piratical activities, the local Naval authorities sent out H.M.S. "Durban" about midnight on Saturday to search for a "mystery ship."

Waglan lighthouse is one of the landmarks showing the way into Hongkong harbour. Vessels not necessarily making for the port but passing close by are also sighted. On Saturday night Waglan reported that a passing ship had failed to give the usual signal. Whether this meant that the boat failed to respond or ignored the lighthouse, is not at present known.

Although nothing has so far developed, it was just as well that precautions were taken. The Harbour Office was communicated with, and then the Naval authorities. There was a possibility that the ship had fallen into evil hands, or that something was amiss. H.M.S. "Durban" put out, with police officers on board, as well as Mr. L. H. V. Booth (at present acting assistant Harbour Master, and formerly assistant Director of Criminal Intelligence.)

When a “China Mail” reporter made inquiries this morning it was gathered that the cruiser had not returned. Outside of the harbour he had examined steamers but none had come under actual suspicion.

No ship had been posted, missing at the Harbour Office. There have been previous instances of ships passing through Hongkong waters—though not coming into harbour—and evading light dues by not giving the name to Waglen. A ship, in any part of the Colony's waters, should pay two cents per registered ton, as light dues. This time there may have been no such desire at all. Unless there are further developments, it should be concluded that the omission was unintentional.

## TARIFF CURSE

STIRRING PROTEST BY A BANKER

TRADE LEAGUE WANTED.

## Rugby, March 7

Mr. Walter Leaf, President of the International Chamber of Commerce and Chairman of the Westminster Bank, made another striking protest yesterday against the evil effects of tariffs and trade barriers on the economic situation of Europe.

Addressing the Council of the International Chamber of Commerce he summarised reports received from various National Committees, and drew from them the general conclusion that what was needed was a European Trade League. Everywhere there were complaints concerning the difficulty of finding markets for manufactures. The capacity for production was there, and it was generally much larger than in pre-war times, but products were stagnating because they were refused, or at least hampered by foreign tariffs and trade barriers. Hence unemployment, the stagnation of industry, and a lamentable waste of potential human energy. The whole standard of living was lowered by the artificial restrictions on human efficiency.

A European Trade League would have open markets, but at least the same scale is those of the United States, and would thus be able to compete in production of equal terms with that vast area of Free Trade Intercourse.

## DRUNK ON DREGS

FRENCHMAN STRUCK WITH A CLOG.

ALBERT CAFE INCIDENT.

With a black mark over his left eye, M. Albert Guetat, proprietor of Albert Curie, gave evidence before M. R. E. Lindsell at the Central Magistracy this morning in a charge of assault against a Chinese kitchen coolie.

Complainant said that the defendant had lately been a nuisance in the establishment and on Saturday evening got drunk by swallowing the grega left in the glasses of the customers. Defendant refused to work or to go away and returned the following morning for his wages. Defendant had to be pushed out of the back door into the lane and it was alleged that he struck the complainant in the eye with a wooden clog.

Continuing, the Frenchman said that he retaliated with his first and knocked the man down. He next picked the man up, pushed him along the lane, and handed him over to a constable.

Defendant admitted that he had the clog in his hand, but denied he struck the proprietor. He claimed to have been assaulted with a stick.

## BROADCASTING

### RAISING STANDARD OF PERFORMANCES. NEED FOR ABLE MEN

(British Wireless Service)

The report of the Broadcasting Committee set up under the Chairmanship of Lord Crawford was issued last night.

It's proposes that the whole equipment and staff of the British Broadcasting Company be transferred at the end of this year to a new public Corporation, the British Broadcasting Commission. It is proposed that this corporation should be made up of five or seven Commissioners nominated by the Crown, possibly including the Director of the superseded Company, all salaried, and the report lays great emphasis on the need for securing men of first-class capacity for these appointments.

Advisory Committees should, it is recommended, be freely appointed, and the report urges that the Commissioners should be given much larger liberty than an ordinary Government Department enjoys.

The Committee pay a high tribute to the work of the British Broadcasting Company, but believe that the stage has been reached when a monopoly of this character should be in public hands.

The Committee's recommendations state that every effort should be made to raise the standard of style and performance in every phase of broadcasting, and particularly in music; that a moderate amount of controversial matter should be broadcast, provided the material is of high quality and distributed with scrupulous fairness, and that the discretion of the Commissioners in this connection should be unheld.

Licences should be granted to blind persons free of charge.
