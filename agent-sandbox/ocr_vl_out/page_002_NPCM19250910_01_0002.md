CAPITAL AND THE EAST.

## -

Mr. Smillie, M.P., speaking at a Cumberland L.L.P. rally at Keswick on August 3, said he sincerely hoped that a Labour Government would never again take office without power. He did all he could to prevent the Party doing so last time.

They were told they should be Constitutionalists, and not go outside the Constitution or be rebels, but when they came into their own and the country returned to the House of Commons sufficient men and women to form a Labour Government with a majority of 20 or 30, and they began to touch the real issues, to take the first step, the taking over of the hand, and began to nationalise mines and railways, the talk about Constitutionalism would fall through, and the other side would throw Constitutionalism to the winds in favour of raising the Army against them. He believed in trying to get some of the palliatives--improved old-age pensions, widows' pensions, protection for life and limb, and a minimum wage--as long as capitalism continued, but they wanted to wipe out capitalism.

A prominent mineowner had said that so long as British coal had to complete with coal produced in other countries, it would have to be produced under similar conditions to those under which it was produced in other countries. That was the owners' idea with regard to coal in the mointime, but it applied to every other commodity produced in the country. In India coal was being developed with British money, for some amount of which was invested by British coal owners, and there were and children were working under the conditions they were working under in Britain 80, 90, or 100 years ago. The little children were carried into the Indian mines, and they and their mothers worked for twopence and the price a day, and for ten hours a day, and aground. India might be a competitor with British coal some day, as it was further developed by British-money and ealdowners' money, and the outlook for the British miner, his wife and children, was that they would have to bring themselves down to the conditions under which coal could be produced elsewhere, to the standard of good labour. That was the outlook under capitalism, not merely in coal-mining, but also in every other industry. Capital had lost all nationality, and went wherever it could go the cheapest labour to harness to its machine, and it was now beginning to exploit the East.

## Other Credit Inclinations

This inclination on the part of Germany to give credit to Russian customers is also seen in the case of German exports to Russia. According to the Fremdenblatt, practically all the goods bought in Germany for Russia either through the medium of the Commercial Mission in Berlin or by the agents of the various Soviet States trusts and syndicates are purchased on the credit system. The German exporting firms are as a rule content with a cash payment of 50 per cent or in some cases of only 25 per cent of the sum due, the remainder being payable on short term credits. The reality is that the German export trade in Russia rises and falls according to the willingness of the seller to with for his money. It is estimated that on an average goods to the value of 2,000,000 gold marks are as present being exported each working day from Germany.

Investors who are on the lookout for a trustee investment giving a clear return of 5 per cent, might do worse than study the offer made by the City of Belfast. Sums of £50 and upwards may be invested either in 5 per cent, stock, redeemable in 145, or in 5 per cent. mortgages for shorter periods, special provisions for earlier repayment of which constitute a valuable protection against depreciation. ## CHINA MISSIONS

FACTORS LEADING TO PRESENT UNREST

## (i)

ARCHIDEACON BARNETT'S COMMENT.

No-one is better qualified to write on the present and past situation in South China as it affects missionary work than the late Archidencon, the Ven. E. J. Barnett, who devoted over 20 years of his life to such service. The extent to which mission work is affected by recent disorders in China has already been referred to, also the decision of missionary bodies to hand over supervision, temporarily at any rate, to Chinese, and it is interesting to note that Archidecon Barnett has contributed to the August number of the "Church Missionary Outlook" an illuminating article on the factors leading up to the present unrest.

The author of Church Notes and News in the "Telegraph" comments as follows on this article:—

Archdeacon Burnett takes the view that the present serious crisis is more deep-seated than the Boxer outbreak, and more dangerous, because there is recognised reasonableness in certain of the demands. The chaotic condition of robber-ridden China, under so many independent military rulers, doubtless favours the anti-foreign agitator. But the root cause, he points out, is a hideous sense of humiliation which, if it is to be removed, demands (so they are taught) desperate measures. Neither economic nor industrial conditions alone would have produced the present upheaval. It is an attempt to wipe out what is regarded as an intolerable national reproach. The means being used to that end are improper and illegal, but they are prompted by a new and sinister influence which has of late worked its way into Chinese politics.

## BAD LEADERSH

After relating many examples of hostility, the archdeacon writes: "The foregoing facts are chosen from present-day life to illustrate the changed conditions under which the Church's work is now being carried on. It must not be concluded, however, that the people as a whole have adopted an antagonistic attitude. Churches are fuller than ever, and the demand for the Scriptures and for teaching is at high-water mark. Neither should it be assumed that the motives of those who oppose themselves are evil only. Ignorance and bad leadership will account for much."

## account for much

In judging the Chinese today the archdeacon warns us not to forget that they can boast an historic past which is millennium older than that of the Western peoples whom they regard as their oppressors. They are conscious of capabilities and intellectual powers which will well compare with those of others. It is therefore mental agony to the later educated generation to realise that their great country, with all its immeasurable possibilities, hags so far behind Japan and the Western peoples in their forward march. For their backward position they blame (not altogether justly) the Great Powers, who in the past have imposed upon them trenties which they now demand shall be revised.

The archdeacon concludes his review of the position by saying that it is not all evil is certain, for the motive is one which all fair-minded Christians must respect. During the past decade we ourselves learned anew what patriotism means. Patriotism is now the dynamic of Chinese discontent. Without belittling the students' serious mistakes, it is through the exercise of Christian virtues—love, sympathy, patience—that we, with unfailing prayer, can best set forward God's own remedy for China's present troubles.

Writing from Shaolisingfu, Chekingu, under date June 30, the Rev. T. Grant ends an account of local conditions by saying: "Our Chinese Christian fellow-workers and friends remain friendly to us; though taking the Chinese point of view in the Shanghai affair. I think they are beginning to see our point of view more clearly now, as the truth comes out. I believe that none of the Chinese newspapers has published, true accounts, with all the facts, of the troubles, and, consequently, a great many lies have been believed by the Chinese, much to the detriment of the good names of the foreign sojourners in the country."
