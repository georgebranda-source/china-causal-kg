## THE AUSSIES

CAN THEY BE BEATEN?

TEMPERAMENT THE MOST IMPORTANT POINT:

"Temperament is the thing that tells in Test matches. It is not how a man stands in the averages, but how he shapes on big occasions, that should guide the electors of an England eleven." This is the considered opinion of Mr Arthur Gilligan, England's cricket captain, expressed by him to the "Sunday Express."

"Spoforth," added Mr Gilligan, "is reported to have said that he thought the Australians would win in 1926, as he considered they had more nerve. He is right in a way, though I think our fellows put up a pretty stout fight. But the Australians have a wonderful fighting temperament, and I think the most important point for us to study is temperament.

“Never mind how a man stands in the averages—pick him for what he has done on big occasions. Watch him carefully in a difficult match, such as Gentlemen v. Players. You will so often find that a man who does well is smaller matches is finished on a big day. If a match were called Mr. Somebody's team v: Australia instead of England v. Australia, many a cricketer would not feel so overwaved by the importance of the event.

Temperament, though largely a matter of natural character, can be developed to a great degree.

THE LATE TOUR.

Discussing the recent tour, Mr. Gilligan said: "I never noticed any instances of funking by our men; I thought we did remarkably well. But our battling was not so good as that of the Australians. They were good right down to No. II. If we had seven wickets down out last three were more likely to put on ten than a hundred.

"Our bowling was quite as good as theirs, and our fielding definitely superior—as the Australians themselves admitted. I, as a bowler, do not like the eight-ball over. Batsmen don't object to it; for instance, Jack Hobbs is all in favour of it, while Tate is dead against it. Australia is really a batsman's paradise, and the eight-ball over makes life hard for the bowler. The bowler should, in my opinion, be helped, not hindered."

It the summer of 1920 is dry, we shall have a hard struggle with the Australians. Should it be a damp one, England stands a very good chance. The Australians, it will be remembered, made a poor show in the fourth Test match during our recent tour, when the wicket was wet; but on a dry wicket they take full marks.

## THE DIAGNOSIS

CRICKET STORY.

For a moment on first waking I was conscious only of a vague discomfort. Something was wrong, but it was not until I attempted to move that I realised how ill I was, writes T. Hodgkinson.

Then however, the ache apparent in every limb and the fact that even breathing was a process not unfraught with pain made it clear that the malady was no triding one. And I confided the fact to Phyllis without delay.

Who wants his breakfast in bed? she asked heartlessly, misled by the brave smile I had assumed.

"Not me," I replied, and noting how illness had weakened my grammar as well as destroyed my appetite, she, too, began to grow concerned.

"Aching all over," she said

"Sounds like flu. I'd better take your temperature."

Ever since I bit the end off a perfectly new thermometer and for days, owing to the amount of glass I had swallowed, left her, in doubt as to whether to order red or black for her spring costume Phyllis only takes my temperature in extreme cases. That, presumably, was the real reason for her indignation when she discovered that I was quite normal.

You're as well as I am," she declared. "You'll feel much better once you're up."

“I shall have to feel much better before I'm up,” I told her. “I think we ought to have other advice. Haven't you got another thermometer?”

She hadn't, and so great was her faith in the old one that it was only with the greatest difficulty that I persuaded her to send for the doctor.

Tell me the truth," I begged him. "I can bear anything better than suspense." And thus implored, he did so.

"You ought to know better," he said, severely, "than to send for me just because you're a little stiff after your first cricket practice."
