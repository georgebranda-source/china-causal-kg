## CANNON DISCOVERY

IRON AND BRONZE WEAPONS. BURIED IN SAND.

"On the island of Gili Genting," the entrance to the bay of Soemeng, writes a Madura correspondent to one of the local

mapers, a treasure lies partly buried in the shape of iron and bronze: annon, numbering from 200 to 00.

A Dutch artillery officer is now on the island superintending the work of digging out the cannon which have sunk into the sandy soil.

These guns were brought to the island by a British fleet in command of the then Governor-General, Sir Stamford Raffles, for the bombardment of Soemenep and the subjugation of the Sultan of that island.

Before operations were begun, however, the decree respecting the return of Java and the other islands to the Dutch was made. The fleet then sailed away, leaving the cannon behind.

An official controversy prevailed for a while respecting the ownership of the cannon, the Dutch claim being finally validated.

The cannon will be transported to Java and broken up, the metal representing a considerable value.

## SCENES AT FUNERAL

A crowd of 300 angry wome chased a bereaved husband through the cemetery at Willesden, an pelted him with clods of earth.

He was Albert Putteney, whose wife, Mrs. Ada Kathleen Petchley, aged 27, and her two-year-old girl were found dead in a gas-filled room. At the inquest it was stated that the husband had left them two days previously without any money, and the jury asked the Coroner to ensure the husband.

Women tried to attack Petchley as he entered the mourning coach on leaving the house, but he was protected by the undertaker's men. At the cemetery the crowd booied and shouted and threw turf at Petchley, who was on the verge of collapse.

He walked from the carriage supported by his mother. Owing to the shouting of women the service was nearly inaudible, and at the end Petchley had to run from the cemetery, pursued by the crowd of women. He managed to get back to the coach, which was driven rapidly away.

## CHRISTIANITY

CHINESE ARE FAILING TO ACCEPT.

Shanghai, China, Sept. 28.—The success of the Christian cause in China is not what most optimists have expected in the past few years. With the recent anti-foreign agitation and anti-Christian movement the Chinese have ceased to accept Christianity.

This is the uncompromising verdict of Rev. Arthur H. Smith, octogenarian, who spent 54 years in China and who is the author of numerous volumes on missionary work in the Orient. His views are supported by not a few of the leading secretaries now in the field. Those Americans, who have dug deep in their purses to convert Chinese are doubtless surprised but the bald statement of fact is probably true. Especially in those centres where foreign commercial men have been boycotted, has Christianity been barely able to hold its own.

The solution of the problem has been searched for by those at home in China—the enigma to date is unsolved.

As counter-irritant to student and atheist propaganda, leaders of the Christian movement have hit upon the plan of making the Oriental convert carry on. With this programme in full operation, leaders believe that the Church will come into its own again.—"United Press."
