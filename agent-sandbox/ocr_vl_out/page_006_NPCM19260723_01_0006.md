## LOCAL COMPANIES

Were all the reports of local Company meetings this year to be published seriatim in one issue of this newspaper they would make sorry reading. They would certainly give the lie direct to those who insinuate that Hongkong has not been so greatly affected by the strike and boycott as local folk make out. Likewise they would reveal a state bordering almost on financial panic—in, a lesser degree, "cold" feetitis"—in the case of certain Companies in which a great deal of local money is invested.

On perusing some of these reports — Directors' reports and Press reports of shareholders' meetings—the conclusion must be reached that foresight and forethought have been sadly lacking. No one conceived in 1923 and 1924, for instance, in certain cases we could specify (but refrain, for obvious reasons), of the Colony being called upon to undergo such a difficult period as has been occasioned since June of last year. There was, prior to the latter date, an air almost of sheer recklessness. "Company affairs were conducted with a total disregard for the inevitable law of averages as regards lean and fat years. Shareholders, for whom Directors were trustees, were kept totally in the dark, even duly audited accounts failing to reveal that, in the case of loans, for instance, securities in guarantees were worthless or practically non-existent. The manner in which certain concerns were conducted and directed could not but lead to disintegration, if not disruption and liquidation, when the day of reckoning dawned at last.

Cynics declare that there is no such thing as commercial morality—and less in public companies than in private ventures. Some of the disclosures made during the past twelve months almost persuade them to range ourselves on the side of the cynics. There has been a display of incompetence that would not be tolerated in the handling of one's personal affairs. There has been a degree of inefficiency in financial administration that engenders surprise why a certain type of person ever finds a place on a

Board of Directors entrusted with the care of publicly invested money. With few exceptions, for which those with money to invest must be truly grateful, the chapter of local Company control in one short year is one of which to be absolutely ashamed.

It is clear that there are still some Augean stables still to be cleaned. Many concerns can still weather the storm by dirt of hard work and economical administration. Shareholders as a rule are liable to look on the dark side when, instead of profits and dividends, they are confronted by deficits and evidences of inertia and ineptitude on the part of those whom they have duly elected to Boards of Directors. With a spirit of goodwill as between Directors and shareholders and as between debtors and creditors, there are still some limited liability companies that can be re-eved from their present inglorious position. By putting a "stout heart to a stey brae" the year of Company history following the settlement of the boycott could easily be transformed for the better and all concerned be the better for the past and present furnace of trial, difficulty and doubt.

## PORT SANITATION

SOURCE OF SUPPLIES OF WATER.

The annual meeting of the Association of Port Sanitary Authorities was held on May 28 in London.

Dr. W. Allen Daley (Hull) moved a resolution recommending that it should be made compulsory for the master, or officer in charge of a vessel to keep a register or make a full and proper entry in his log showing the date and source of all water supplies taken on board, and that a copy should be forwarded to the Ministry of Health. He said he did not think there would be any doubt in the minds of those present of the desirability of having full and exact information as to the source of the water supply on board a ship. In the port of Hull they never had any serious difficulty in obtaining such information, but it was always verbal, and they felt that on future occasions if they were investigating cases of typhoid, dysentery or even cholera they ought to have something more than a verbal statement concerning the source of supply. It was really, he felt, a matter for international legislation, but that was a lengthy business, and they considered that something definite should be done in the meantime. After a brief discussion the resolution was adopted.

A resolution was brought forward from the River Tees Authority for consideration expressing the opinion of the Authority that the time was opportune to make venerable disease a notifiable disease and that representations to this effect should be made to the Ministry of Health, Members of Parliament, Port Sanitary Authorities, etc. The resolution was lost by nine votes to eight.

## Bathing Facilities:

Turning to the question of bathing facilities on this side of the harbour, the Committee received a very careful report from its subcommittee, most of whose recommendations were adopted. It was decided to write to the Government in order to lay before it the following proposals:

1. The improvement of the principal bathing beach at Castle Peak by the construction of a good pathway from the road to the shore, and a parking space for cars beside the road. The provision of a pipe to convey fresh water from the nullah to the line of matsheds, a raft with diving steps and a supply of life-saving appliances.

2. The opening up of the smaller bay to the east of the pier at Castle Peak and the erection of a matshed, or other structure, for the use of the public, at a small fee per cubicle having accommodation for men and women.

3. The conversion of the portion of Tai Wan Bay between the Waverly Terrace of the Docks and the Cement Works into a public bathing beach, clearing away some of the rough stones, old matsheds and other obstructions and erecting a matshed (or other building) with cubicles for the use of the public at a suitable fee. The new road skirting the Dock property will make the bench easily accessible and available for a bus service in the bathing season.

Since the above recommendations were passed by the Committee the recent storm has involved the Government in great and unexpected expenditure, and it may, seen inopportuneto put forward any proposals requiring the outlay of money. But many of the recommendations especially in connection with the bus service do not touch the Treasury and some of the more expensive work suggested can be left till next bathing season.

## NEW SHIPS

## DETAILS OF SIX FINE VESSELS

A feature of the shipping trade is the particularly noteworthy vessels which are being launched. Prominent among these is the "Shropshire," a twin-screw passenger and cargo motor-vessel, which the Fairfield Shipbuilding and Engineering Co., of Glasgow, have just launched for the Bibby Line. The "Shropshire," which has an overall length of 502 ft., will engage in the Eastern trade.

Another launch is that of the "Idomeneus," a motor-vessel, also for the Eastern trade, her owners being Messrs. Alfred Holt and Co., and her builders Messrs. Workman, Clark and Co., Belfast. This vessel, whose length is 476 ft., has a load displacement exceeding 15,000 tons, and will have a speed of about fifteen knots.

There is also another motor-vessel, the "Silverash," launched by Messrs. Joseph L. Thompson and Sons, Sunderland, to the order of the Silver Line, Ltd. This vessel is to trade between the United States and the Far East, the voyage extending over a passage of about 27,000 miles. The vessel's main dimensions are 442 ft.; by 58 ft. 3½ in., by 38 ft. 6 in., and her speed will be approximately 13 knots.
