On Sunday a large number of troops under the command of officers came to the numerous pig merchants in the north city and demanded that they be permitted to buy some animals. The outcome was that they carried off in carts and ricshas nearly three thousand animals.

All the merchants had to show for the holiday stock were military notes worth about eighteen cents on the dollar. Thus a pig worth $20, brought the owner about $3.60 in actual value of military notes.

Apparently some soldiers had been left out of the Sunday raid. A group of these turned up on Monday morning at the Ssu Pai Lou markets. At one shop they demanded pork but were told there was none. They then began a search and finding the coveted meat, they threw the entire stock into the dirt and mud and practically wrecked the shop.

The suggestion was made following this wholesale pillering of the markets where hundreds if not thousands of men are employed, that a good chance to teach the looting soldiers a lesson was lost. Most of the workers in these pig markets have long poles used for carrying the animals while others have hooked iron spears. Had they come out in a body with these weapons, a few dead soldiers might have left a lasting taste in the mouth of their fellow robbers.

A "Swiss Landru," an electrician, aged twenty-seven, of respectable family, who admits cynically that he murdered two young women in order to gnin possession of their suvings, and who is suspected of doing away with a number of others, is keeping the Swiss police continuously occupied.

## Widow Mystery

The body of a widow, aged forty, Frida Gazemann, was found in a wood near Zurich a month ago. A revolver lay beside her, and two wounds in her head were at first believed to have been self-inflicted. Investigations showed, however, that she had been cheerful up to within a few days of her death, when she told her friends she was going away to be married.

No motive for suicide or a crime could be discovered, but when the police received an anonymous letter to say that the writer had been swinbled by Kaufmann, who had tried to obtain her savings, and that he constantly advertised for a wife, his room in Zurich was searched. A trunk containing a woman's lingerie and house-linen embroidered with "M. C." was found there.

The strangled body of an unknown girl wearing similar underclothing with the same monogram had been found only a few days previously on a lonely country road. Kaufmann swore that he had bought the trunkful of linen as a speculation. Another discovery was a large quantity of love letters, neatly filed and annotated.

Kaufmann was arrested and forced to admit that he had been practising as a matrimonial deceiver for many years. He confessed that he had killed Frau Gazemann, and when shown proof of his having drawn £250 from the savings bank account of Marie Cadenas, he admitted he had murdered the girl whose trousseau had been found in his room.

The police have issued a notice asking Swiss girls to communicate with them if they have had experience of his dealings, and a large mass of correspondence revealing the young man's misdeeds has resulted. # AMERICA ON THE STRIKE

From the beginning of the general strike American public opinion has been strongly on the side of the British Government. Thanks to the voluminous Press correspondence from London, there has at no time been any confusion here about the real issue. Indeed, it can be said confidently that because of the limited publication of newspapers in Great Britain the average American has been better informed about the strike throughout its course, to date than the average person in Great Britain.

The Times' New York correspondent writing at the time of the Strike, says:—

## An Indefensible Attack

To American eyes—and this means the eyes of all ranks of society, by no means excluding organised Labour—the general strike was an indefensible attack on orderly government. American Labour—leaving out of account a few Socialist and Communist organisations negligible in numbers and influence.

—feels that the British unions made a strategical mistake—to call it nothing else—in attempting to coerce the State and in disregarding the sanctity of contract. For all its sympathy with the confiners, sympathy shared by most other ranks of American life, it is determined not to undermine its own strong bargaining position and its solidarity by extending help to those who, it seems, have, rashly put themselves out of court. All Americans have been united in the belief that the British Government must win the struggle to save Great Britain from the greatest disaster in her history.

## America's Best Customer:

Americans see American trade and industry a loser to a considerable extent by the dislocation of industry in the home of the best customer of the United States. Some orders will come to America which would otherwise have gone to England; some, indeed, for coal; have already been placed in America; but these will be no more than a small offset to the falling-off of American export trade to Great Britain which is expected. Last year Great Britain alone bought from the United States $1,031,877,000 of the $4,909,000,000 worth of goods the United States sent abroad, and paid for them in part by sending to America $412,316,000 worth of goods—not quite one-tenth of the imports into the United States. Great Britain was far and awny America's best customer; Canada, the second best, taking goods to the value of only about three-fifths of Great Britain's purchases. The largest single item in British purchases from the United States was cotton.

Americans, according to their temperaments, are grieved or exasperated by what they consider to be the futility of the general strike, because it was felt here that up to the time it broke out Great Britain, by following the only right road, was making sure, if slow, headway out of her troubles. There was the strongest possible sympathy in the United States with the efforts of the Prime Minister, which were considered to be as effective as the extraordinary difficulties of the situation would permit.

The attempt of the strikers to suppress the newspapers was felt here to be the crowning folly of their enterprise—a strong indictment by the strikers of the justness of their own cause. That single action probably did more than anything else to convince the Americann public that the unions were conscious of the inherent weakness of their position.
