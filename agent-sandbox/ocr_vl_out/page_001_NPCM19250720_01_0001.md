## GALLANT RESCUE

PO HING FONG EPIC

## Marvellous Escape From Terrible Death

With all hope abandoned of finding a single soul alive after those already rescued in the Po Hing Fong house collapse disaster of Friday morning, the work of digging out the unfortunate victims was proceeded with practically without a break until the afternoon of Saturday, when a further seven dead bodies were added to the toll since the morning.

In the midst of work in a central portion of the demolished row of houses, a police officer who was one of the working parties, heard a voice calling out from beneath the wreckage. After making a brief halt, during which he satisfied himself that the voice was there, he summoned others to the spot. Some planking was hacked through, and gradually the way was cleared for a closer approach to the person who was entombed below. From questions put by the rescue party it was ascertained that the entombed person was a girl. She stated that she was thirteen years old, and could not move as she had one leg caught in the wreckage. Her mother, she asserted, was in the kitchen, and she begged to be supplied with some food and drink.

## Found Alive

It was not until three hours later, after a considerable amount of wreckage had been removed, that a small aperture was made in the direction from which the voice was proceeding. The girl was eventually located in a lying down posture about three feet away from the aperture. Long before the discovery was made the girl asserted that she could see light from the place where she lay. These were the overhead lights and the electric torches which were connected up with the main specially for the purpose and which proved so useful. By reaching in through the aperture and directing the torches in that direction, the girl was handed a bottle of lemonade and some biscuits. The bottle was later returned by the girl, who thanked her rescuers, and said she was feeling much better. Questioned as to whether she was hurt, she stated that she was uninjured except for her leg which was caught somewhere and prevented her moving.

A considerable amount of timber, bits of furniture and other wreckage, sticking out at all angles, lay above the place where the girl lay, and this in turn supported tons of brickwork. There was a further bank of brickwork at a sloping angle and floor tiles on the right of the supporting woodwork, covered over by a bed of floor planks. It was decided to lighten the weight above by removing a part of the rubble. The bed of planking proved useful as a chute and several tons of material were removed in a short space of time. All hands rendered assistance, the Northern members of the Fire Brigade especially doing yeoman service and frequently-receiving-commendation from their chief.

## Gruesome Discovery

In the course of removing the material, a dead body was discovered below the top heap. The body was behind a brickwork boulder with very little earth concealing it from view. This was removed by the stretcher bearers.

The work of clearing the interior bank of bricks and tiles, amongst which were bed posts and iron bars and piping from the building was next done, and after that shoring up with timber was done to retain the mass of woodwork which served as a floor and prevented tons of material above from crashing down into the place where the girl lay.

## Another Dead Body

A considerable amount of woodwork had to be hacked and sawn through before access could be gained to the girl by making an opening a foot high on the same level, and more shoring up was done. This was accomplished at about 1 a.m. In the meantime the girl had gone to sleep after an injection of morphia administered by Dr. S. C. Ho.

During the work of removing the timber and shattered remnants of household furniture, another body of a dead man was found in a doubled up position almost in front and below the aperture which was made at first for communicating with the girl. The man had been crushed to

death by falling between some heavy planks, and the broken part of a chair still adhering to his body indicated that, he had been seated when the crash occurred.

Mr. R. B. Cargin, an officer of H.M.A.S. Brisbane, who volunteered his services and worked incessantly in the earlier operations since his arrival at 9 p.m., entered the opening with Mr. H. Braga. It was found that the girl was lying on her back with the left leg pinioned between falling spars. Above her was the mass of woodwork standing on a heavy glass door, partly shattered. This woodwork left a cavity in which the girl was found. On the left side of her were some skirting-boards running parallel with her body, and these, backed by other beams, "effectually arrested further progress of the sliding mass of brickwork at the rear. But for the skirting-boards and the timber behind it, the girl would have been buried by the brickwork. She was lying in a space eighteen inches high, with a clear space of six inches between her and the glass door and woodwork above.

After inspecting the position of the girl, Mr. Cargin came outside and did some more shoring up of the heavy beams above the girl so that work could be carried on with a fair amount of safety both for the rescuers and the rescued.

Getting back to his position: Mr. Cargin and Mr. Braga handed out bits of broken glass, stones, a broken blackwood stool which was directly under the girl, and other obstructing prices of wood extending outside were removed, some having to be sawn away from outside and dragged out under directions from the workers inside. Mr. Barga supported the girl and assisted Mr. Cargin in his attempt to extricate her leg. This proved a difficult matter as a spar had fallen across just above the left knee, pinning it down, whilst her left hand was also entangled. Another two beams had fallen crosswise over a point below the knee. On top of the soft part of the foot had fallen a thick broken porcelain jar and some bricks, which were got out after much difficulty as there were other bits of wood wedging them down. Having extricated the foot, an attempt was made to remove the spars on the other pptions of the leg. With the narrowness of the opening, it was not possible to remove these with any other tools except a chisel. The spars were eventually cut away after laborious work extending over a long period and the leg was removed clear.

#### "Struck.By Lightning."

It may be mentioned that the girl recovered from the effects of the injection as soon as the workers entered the opening and she was touched. Her cries when she was in pain during the process of attempting to move something here or there guided Mr. Cargin to proceed slowly and cautiously. At other times she indulged in a free flow of words in the course of which she stated that she thought she had been struck by lightning when she found herself imprisoned. She asked for tea to drink, as the lemonade made her sick, but this was ignored by Dr. Ho. Next she wanted an assurance that besides medicine she was going to be supplied with good food as soon as she was taken out. A promise that a pot of porridge was being cooked for her kept her happy. During the period of imprisonment, which lasted 42 hours, the girl had retained her senses, and one thing she was certain of was that she had some money in her pocket. When Mr. Cargin found it necessary to tear away the clothes as they were caught in the spars, she remembered her money and expressed ears for its safety.

A Further Casualty

While the work was proceeding, the girl touched an object directly under her right leg at the same time drawing the attention of Mr. Cargin in that direction.

object proved to be the upper part of a woman's head, apparently the one referred to by the girl as her mother. The head was crushed face down and the remainder of the body was not visible, being buried by the mountain of wreckage above.

The order from the workers to draw up the stretcher indicated that the rescue was complete, the time being 2.45 a.m. The girl was immediately taken to the Tung Wah Hospital close by under the care of Dr. Ho. She appeared to be exhausted and pale after her long period of imprisonment without any food, and remained silent after reaching the open air.

## Heroes Congratulated

With his technical knowledge of the situation which confronted Mr. Cargin on Saturday, there is no doubt that he proved himself the hero of the hour. That the girl was extricated from her difficult position with no further injuries than those caused by the wreckage which pinned her down was sufficient evidence that Mr. Cargin handled the situation with caution and skill. Mr. Brags deserves no little commendation for the valuable assistance rendered to Mr. Cargin in a situation which called for great endurance from both of them.

Mr. Cargin and Mr. Braga had been working in a lying down posture, which was the only position allowed by the opening, for an hour and a half, and they never once stepped out or relaxed their efforts. Their position was attended with great danger and they were glad to reach the safety of the open, although soaked to the skin, covered with mud, bruised and exhausted with unceasing toil inside the death chamber. As soon as the girl had been got away, Mr. D. Burlingham (Deputy Superintendent of Police) hastened to congratulate Mr. Cargin and Mr. Braga on their gallant feat. Congratulations followed from Mr. H. T. Brooks (Superintendent of the Fire Brigade), and the other workers.

Others for special mention in the rescue work are Mr. H. T. Brooks, who had worked practically without a break since Friday morning; Mr. D. Burlingham who was always there when "his official duties permitted him; the Rev. G. T. Waldegrave, Mr. C. E. Thomas of the Prisons Department, Captain Bloxham, Mr. L. H. V. Brooth, Mr. James Key of the Radio Telegraph Office, and many others who assisted, but whose names have not been ascertained. Dr. S. C. Ho never left his charge for a moment and his presence proved an inspiration to the workers. The "stalwarts" of the Fire Brigade, especially the Northerners, worked in a creditable manner and considered no undertaking difficult to accomplish when a life was at stake.

## The Hasued Girl

From enquiries made at the Tung Wat Hospital this morning, it was learned that the injuries sustained by the girl to her left leg and arm were not of a serious nature, being in the nature of bruises, and that she would be completely recovered in the course of a few days. The girl was not hurt in other parts of the body, and she will recover from her injuries without any permanent scars.

## Latest Death Roll

A larger number than usual were engaged in clearing the debris yesterday and last night, and many more bodies were recovered. The total number of burial orders issued by the Tung Wah Hospital until this morning was 49. Last evening there was one body which was located at the base of a big mound, but this cannot be removed until the whole of the wreckage above has been cleared. The extent of the wreckage above the place where the Chinese girl was rescued alive early yesterday morning, can be imagined, when it was found that up to this morning the body of the woman who had only a part of her head visible in the pit had not been removed. The number of deaths so far as certain exceeds 50.

The bodies of Mr. Chau Su-id and the mothers of his household are still being retained at the Tung Wah Hospital and they will be transferred to the Repository next week. Interment will take place at a later date.

## STRIKE NEWS

## FEW STRIKERS RETURN VIA

POSITION LITTLE CHANGED.

The local strike position remains in a position of more or less deadlock in so far as strikers in Canton are concerned, although workers continue to dribble back who have never left the Colony but merely awaited developments.

Still more have applied for their old positions at the Naval Yard, but they have, as warned before they left, to start again in their respective jobs. Many of them have lost their chance of reemployment as they are now called upon to pass a medical examination before they can reenter the service, and in the cases of the older men particularly this has meant rejection.

On enquiry at the Naval Yard concerning a rumour that the Naval Yard contemplated discharging all employees and closing down for a few weeks, the "China Mail" was informed that it was without foundation.

Although it is difficult to say whether strikers are walking in large numbers from intermediate stations on the Kowloon-Canton railway in order to avoid being turned back at Sumchun, it is known that more have re-entered the Colony in this way. Still more are taking ship from Canton to Macao, where they are allowed to land providing it is for the purpose only of transhipping. By this means many have returned to Hongkong, as three boats still maintain communication between Hongkong and Macao.

## Police Raid

Another raid for the purpose of ridding the Colony of idlers was carried out by the Police at Hunghom this morning. Troops also assisted and a cordon was drawn round an area embracing Bulkeley Street and Wuhn Street.

Houses were entered by the Police and as a result 62 persons were taken to the Police Station, where they are detained pending enquiries.

## Volunteers at Work

A noticeable feature of yesterday's operations on the site of the demolished houses was the presence of members of the Engineer Section of the Hongkong Volunteer Defence Corps. They were seen strenuously working with shovels on the top of the slopes, getting large quantities of earth away to the coolies handling baskets at the foot of zinc chutes fastened down the slopes. The Volunteers were on duty even early this morning, and their presence on the scene has resulted in the height of the mounds being considerably lessened in a remarkably short space of time. Invaluable Ambulance Service.

The Indian and Chinese sections of the St. John Ambulance Brigade, under their respective Sergeants in charge, have rendered invaluable services in conveying casualties and dead to hospital. The men have been working for long spells, and have always shown a marked sense of their duty. From what was seen of the members of the Indian section particularly, they have never hesitated even when it came to handling the bodies. Mr. E. Ralphs (Assistant Commissioner) exercises constant supervision by visiting the scene.

Members of the Police, Sanitary and other departments are also working very hard, and with the large number of workers it would be difficult to pick any out for individual mention. ## LOCAL LANDLORDS

## A NEW ASSOCIATION FORMED:

STRIKE PROBLEM3.

If the strike has served no other purpose it has, incidentally, seen the Hongkong Landlords' Association became an established fact.

tion became an established fact.

This morning's "Tsun Wan Yat Po" announces that a meeting of about a hundred Chinese landlords and property brokers was held at the Hang Fa Lau Restaurant, just below Boddies Market yesterday afternoon, when APS Pong Walting, a well-known houseowner, was elected temporary chairman.

Speakers who addressed the meeting which, by the way, started very late, pointed out that tenants were protected by an association, other classes of the community either looked after themselves or were looked after, and share-brokers were cared for by the Government. Accordingly, the speakers reasoned, it was high time for the landlords to act. Such organisation as the one which had sponsored the meeting was no more than a club. More than this was required—hence the association for which a rather inappropriate name was found.

Among the questions discussed was that of the tiding over of loans on property, reduced rates of hypothecation, etc., during the present stress.

## RICE FOR STRIKERS

## CANTON OUTLOOK NOW WORST

In spite of boasts that North China rice would be imported into Canton by way of direct shipment from Shanghai, prices of the staple food are shooting up, writes the "China Mail" correspondent. That there is a pronuncement of the event from the proposal of the Strike Executive to place an embargo on wine (Shamshu) brewing. Although a fairly large quantity of rice is used in this manner, the quality is poor and the percentage very small. If even the Samshu rice is needed to prevent a famine things must be fairly serious.

Unrest is growing in the strikers' camp now numbering about 40,000. Within the last few days a very large number has departed by tow-boat for up-country points, so it is stated, to escape being enrolled in the Labourers' Corps. Had it not been for the pickets and the lack of direct transport to Canton, the number would have been reduced even further, concludes the correspondent.

## LADY SEVERN'S LOSS

DEATH OF HER MOTHER AT HOME

It is with sincere regret that the “China Mail” records the death of Mrs. T. Lowndes Bullock, mother of Lady Severn, at Woodlawn, Oxford, yesterday.

The late Mrs. Bullock was well known to many Hongkong residents having stayed here for some months in 1922.

On March 21 this year, Lady Severn left for Home in response to a cablegram which gave rise to concern as to her mother's condition and the sad news of her death has just been cabled to the Colony.

Mrs. Bullock, it is understood, was born at Peking. She was the wife of the late Professor T. L. Bullock who, it is believed, was once in the Consular Service, and subsequently Professor of Chinese at Oxford University.

Of a retiring and charming disposition Mrs Bullock made many friends during her stay here.
