### Hongkong, Thursday; May. 6, 1926

## NO ADVANCE

From the voluminous reports of the events of the second day of the general strike at Home, it will be seen that no advance has been made toward a resumption of the negotiations which so unhappily broke down at the last moment.

The only verdict possible on the whole miserable business is that volunteered by an American newspaper, which describes it as a tragedy. That it undoubtedly is. The only ray of hope comes from the House of Commons, where all Parties in the debate on the emergency regulations appeared most anxious to assist in the search for a formula on the basis of which the negotiations could be resumed. Even a typical Labourite emphasized the need for resumption, lest the action of some hot-heads might precipitate a catastrophe. Moreover, some light on the situation was thrown by the Labour member for Derby, Mr. J. H. Thomas, who was one of the Negotiating Committee of the Trades Union Congress, who revealed that the Labourites had a formula prepared for presenta

tion to the Prime Minister when the negotiations were so precipitately broken off. That disclosure has created quite a stir in political circles, and there is bound to be an urgent demand for popular curiosity as to the nature of the formula to be satisfied.

ment, through the Earl of Birkenhead, in the House of Lords, has firmly ruled that there can be no possible question of resuming the negotiations so long as the threat of the general strike holds the field. In this decision the Government will assuredly be most strongly supported by the mass of public opinion. That threat of a general strike was a deplorable error of judgment on the part of the Labourites at the most critical moment of the negotiations which at one stage promised so well. As Mr. Lloyd George said in effect in the House of Commons yesterday that the general strike has obscured the merits of the miners' case. He might have gone further and declared that the miners' case has been swamped altogether so far as public interest both within and without the United Kingdom, is concerned. The Trade Unionists have ranged themselves against the whole nation, and they cannot complain if by so doing they have jeopardised the miners' case and alienated the sympathies of thousands. No great heed may be paid to the reports of disturbances in various parts of the country. It was only to be expected that in the first stage of a general strike many of the unruly elements would be let loose and imagine that by lawless acts against tramcars and omnihouses they have made of themselves veritable heroes. They will soon find that respect for the law is essential in their own interests, for the forces of the Crown are more than sufficient to cope with all the hotheads and intimidators, as was proved last year even in the small Colony of Hongkong. Summed up, it may safely be declared that although nothing tangible is reported in the direction of a truce, the position is not yet irretrievable.

The "Daily Mail" appeared in the afternoon in the shape of a penny photographed typewritten news sheet printed on both sides. It includes Monday's leading article: "For King and Country," the refusal of the workers to print which led to the non-publication of Monday's "Daily Mail."

There is an improvement in Glasgow to-day in the tramway situation. It is announced that 114 tramcars are running. There is also a slight improvement in the railway services.

## A NEW INTEREST

## Profittering News Boys

Listless groups of strikers and street corner loafers in London get a new interest in life with the appearance of news boys, who have found a lucrative substitute for newspapers by selling single-sheet racing and cricket results, for which a "profiteering charge of threepence" is made.

The "Evening Standard" was on sale in the West-end to-night, the proprietors devoting the receipts to charity.

A few sporadic cases of rioting have so far occurred but have been speedily handled by the administration of batons which the Police evidently consider more efficacious than a prosecution for the type of individual involved.

## SEDITIONS!

## Jall for Company Director

A prosecution—the first under the Emergency Powers Act—was held in Manchester, William Stoker, described as a company director, being sentenced to two months' imprisonment in the second division for an "act calculated to cause disaffection amongst the Forces and civilians."

It was stated that Stoker's high-powered car was standing outside the Socialist Hall at Openshaw ready to convey to Glasgow 1,600 copies of the Workers' daily, which, the prosecution alleged, Stoker knew contained seditious matter. Notice of an appeal was given.

The latest disturbances occurred in Albert Square, Manchester, where the Police dispersed a large demonstration of unemployed, also in West London and South-East London, where attempts were made to prevent buses running. The Police restored order. They have the situation well in hand everywhere, though heavy calls are made on their alertness to prevent the crowds who were dispersed forming again elsewhere.

Protection Assured.

An official notice has been issued to the printing trade, bespeaking the intention of the Government to insist on the inclusion in any settlement of a clause preventing Trade Unions victimising any man remaining at work or resuming work during the dispute, and adding that men doing their duty to the country in the present crisis will not be left unprotected from subsequent reprisals.

## IRISH ACTION

Powers to the Governor:

The Northern Ireland Parliament has passed a Government Bill empowering the Governor, if an emergency arises, to proclaim a state of emergency and regulate the supply and distribution of the necessities of life.

### IN THE COMMONS. Debate on the Emergency Powers

In the House of Commons, Sir W. Joynson Hick, Secretary of State for Home Affairs, moving the confirmation of the Emergency Power Regulations, quoted the declarations made by Mr. Ramsay MacDonald in 1924 in support of these, which were a very fair statement of the present Government's position.

He explained that the regulations against incitement to mutiny and disaffection enabled summary action to be taken instead of a long trial. The Police were authorised to arrest without a warrant.

Another regulation enabled a search to be made of any building suspected of printing or issuing a document calculated to cause mutiny or disaffection among the Crown Forces, Police, or civilians, or to impede or restrict measures for water, fuel, light, and other public necessities.

This regulation gave him great power, but he said quite definitely that in the interests of the life of the nation these powers were at present necessary.
