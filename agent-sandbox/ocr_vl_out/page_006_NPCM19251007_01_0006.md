In his recent remarkable outburst M. Chicherin, the Soviet Commissar for Foreign Affairs does not seek to hide his chagrin at the successful outcome of the negotiations for the holding of a conference to discuss the European Security Pact. It was well for the maintenance of that spirit of goodwill which has marked the earlier conversations at Locarno that the publication of Chicherin's speech was followed by a prompt and strong denial on the part of Herr Stregemann that Germany was seeking an Eastern orientation. Although German's chief delegate dissociates himself, so "hotly" (in the words of the cable) from the suggestion that Germany seeks to bring pressure to bear on the French and British delegates by using the possibility of a Germano Bolshevik alliance as a lover by which to obtain special treatment, it may be that a useful purpose will be served by Chicherin's reminder of an alternative to a satisfactory settlement. It must be borne in mind

that Germany has already a trade agreement with the Soviets, and although it has proved of little or no use to Germany, an extension of its provisions might quite conceivably be considered by Germany as the lesser of two evils in a choice between it and crippling undertakings with the Allies. The Treaty of Rapallo between the Soviets and Germany, the provisions of which Chicherin so grandiloquently promises to extend to matters military in the event of the Locarno Conference not concluding favourably for Germany, was concluded during the Genoa Conference of 1922 at a time when German business men believed that economic and political penetration of Russia was possible. But several German firms are said to have burnt their fingers as a result of it. Whether the prospective advantages for Germany in a Bolshevised Asia in the event of such an agreement as Chicherin holds forth being accepted would prove less mythical than the trade “advantages” of the Treaty of Rapello is extremely problematical but the decision of Germany to risk it must be considered as a distinct possibility if the hands of the more moderate of the delegates at Locarno are forced by the attitude of a section to impose demands solely with an eye on the sanctions which may be enforced in the event of non-compliance. It would be jingoistic to predict an early division of the world into two camps fighting on a European-Asiatic front if Germany should decide to throw her lot in with a Bolshevised Asia, but it is obviously the ruin of Western civilisation that Chicherin and his myrmidons have set before themselves as an ideal, and they are sparing neither themselves nor their own country in their efforts to bring such a state of affairs to pass. The actual decision as to whether she will throw in her lot with Europe against the forces of Bolshevism rests, of course, with Germany but her attitude may be largely determined by the policies of France and Britain towards her. The complete accord which Mr. Austen Chamberlain and Mr. England arrived at and the conciliatory tone of the French notes

calling of the Locarno Conference may well be regarded as indicating France's pacific intentions, and if France and Germany agree on the details of the proposed arbitration treaties between Germany and her neighbours, practical and general agreement may well be in sight. It is sincerely to be hoped that the goodwill which has animated the delegates in the preliminary discussions is an augury of final success in this effort to unravel the tangle of European affairs.

derived from the refusal of the Chinese to take part in the judicial enquiry into the shooting at Shanghai on May 30 is the evident sincerity, or determination of the Peking Government. They know their own mind and their contentions, in all the circumstances, are reasonable. There has been an inexcusable delay; first in deciding on a judicial enquiry, and secondly in appointing members to the judicial board. There may be reasons for the latter: the slowness in matters of modern diplomacy; the absence of Britain's representative at Peking with no one left in charge in a superior position, adequately to represent the nation. This latter has been the cause of pointed strictures at Home, and no good will be done in blinking facts, and in ignoring the harm done by those facts. The conceding of these points does not for a moment mean approving the action taken by the Chinese. The outbreak of what we may term hostilities against the British, was due to the unfortunate fact that a British police officer at Shanghai felt it necessary to give the order to shoot which resulted in the deaths of numbers of Chinese. What has followed needs no repetition. Mob law, boycott, looting, and striking need strong arguments to justify them. Nothing has arisen to justify what has taken place in these respects. An enquiry has been held and evidence taken and recorded. "Most of the requisite evidence has become unobtainable or has disappeared." On this we must qualify our admission of "evident sincerity" on on the part of the Peking Government. It suggests to the impartial mind that one party, having made outrageous statements and accusations, is now willing to let matters remain in a state of passivity, so that on proper occasions the innuendos and indictments of the hot days of June and July can be trotted out and used as "evidence" of foreign methods. The action of foreign Governments who have sent judicial representatives will be awaited with some interest. A rebuff and an insult have been tendered. It is impossible to think that in spite of the Peking Government's Note, the enquiry will not be held. It should be held, and it must be held.

## FINDING THE NORTH COUNTRY

Is there any blue half so pure, and deep, and tender as that of the large cranesbill, the Geranium pratense of the botanists? When Bell saw the beautiful, rich-coloured blossoms in the tall hedge-rows, she declared we were already in the North Country, and must needs descend from the phacton to gather some of the wild flowers; and lo! all around there was such a profusion that she stood bewildered before them. Everywhere about were the white stars of the stitchwort glimmering among the green of the goose-grass. The clear red blossoms of the champion shone here and there; and the viscid petals of the ragged robin glimmered a bright crimson as they struggled through the thorny branches of the hawthorn. Here, too was the beautiful harebell—the real ‘bluebell’ of Scotland’—with its slender stem and its pellucid colour; and there was its bigger and coarser relative, the great hedge campanula, with its massive bells of azure, and its succulent stalk. There were yellow masses of'snapdragon' and an abundance of white and pink roses sweetening the air; and all the thousand wonders of a luxuriant vegetation. The delight of Bell in these wild flowers was extravagant, and when the lieutenant had gathered for her every variety of hue that he could see; she chose a few of the blossoms and twisted them, with a laugh of pleasure, into the breeze masses of her hair.

If it was not really, the North Country which Bell so longed to enter, it was on the confines of it, and already many premonitory signs were visible. These tall hedges rows, with their profusion of wild flowers, were a wonder: We crossed dark-brown streams, the picturesque banks of which were smothered in every sort of bush and herb and plant. At last, a breath of the morning air brings us a strange, new scent, that is far more grateful than that of any wreath of flowers; and at the same moment both Bell and Tita call out — 'Oh, there is the peat-smoke at last!'.

Peat-smoke it is, and presently we come upon the cottages which are sending abroad this fragrance into the air. They are hidden down in a dell by the side of a small river, and they are surrounded by low and thick elder trees. Bell is driving. She will not even stop to look at this picturesque little nook; it is but an outpost and the promised land is nigh.
