## AULD ROBIN GRAY"

CENTENARY OF LADY

ANNE BARNARD

A hundred years ago (on May 6) died Lady Anne Bargard, the authoress of the Scottish ballad, "Auld Robin Gray." She was a daughter of James Lindsay, fifth Earl of Balcarres, and was born on December 8, 1750. When she was forty-three she married Mr. Andrew Barnard, a son of the Bishop of Limerick, and accompanied her husband to South Africa in 1793, when he was appointed secretary to the colony of the Cape of Good Hope under Lord Macartney. Returning to England in 1802, she resided in London until her death on May 6, 1825, at the age of seventy-five.

Lady Anne Lindsay was a vivacious young woman of twenty-one when she composed her famous ballad in the ancestral hall of the Lindsay, old Balcarres House, near Colinsburgh, in the East of Fife. The ballad was written to suit an ancient Scottish melody of which Lady Anne was very fond; but soon after its publication a new air for it was composed by an English clergyman, the Rev. Mr. Leoves, rector of Wrington, Somersetshire: It is to the English tune that the ballad has been mostly sung.

The secret of the authorship was kept for more than half a century. Lady Anne revealed it only in 1823 in a letter to Sir Walter Scott. In the year of Lady Anne's death, Sir Walter Scott edited the ballad for the Bannatyne Club, with several additions, which, however, are poor. Lady Anne herself had written two additions to the original version.

Lady Anne wrote other poems, none of which are remembered, and some of her prose is preserve. In the pages of "The Lives of the Lindsay's."

There were four other children at home. Ellen had been going to school every day and the children had their meals at a relative's house.

The Coroner (Mr. Oddie): Can you explain why this girl should drown herself?—No, except worry. There was something else for

her to worry about, wasn't there?—I smacked her because she had only a few shillings left out of £3 or £4 I had given her. She had paid the rent and 12s. 6d. for the children's food.

It was not the only time you have hit her?—Never before that. That is not my information. You have thrashed her frequently and without sufficient cause?—No.

Did you ever threaten to kill her?—No.

Didn't you flog her on Thursday [the day before she disappeared]? —No.

I am told that you used to knock your wife about. What do you say to that?—That is a direct lie.

Jackson said that he only hit the girl with his flat hand on the face.

The Coroner: Why did you do that? She had already got enough trouble, having lost her mother and being left at the age of thirteen in charge of the household. Wasn't it a brutal thing to do? She is dead now, you know, and that is the cause of it. That will do.
