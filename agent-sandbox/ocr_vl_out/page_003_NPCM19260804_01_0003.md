## SWATOW & BOYCOTT

EXTREMISTS TRYING TO WRECK THE NEGOTIATIONS.

CHINESE MERCHANTS

HOPEFUL

The Swatow correspondent of the "N. C. Nally News" writes:

The outcome of the conference on the settlement of the boycott, now being held at Canton, is naturally the subject of much speculation in these days. Optimism as to the result seems to be much more pronounced among the Chinese than among the foreign community, which seems impressed with the truth of the beatitude pronounced on those who expect nothing. The Chinese merchants are said to be already making preparations for the resumption of business.

The strict censorship which is being exercised on the press at Canton while negotiations are going on is certainly not in force here; and there is a curious propaganda on foot which seems anything but conducive to a settlement. There appeared some days ago the manifesto of the latest addition to the number of the countless "hui" (societies) with which the place is plagued, this one having as its aim in life the extermination of rebels and brigands, and the maintenance of public peace, and its particular function being to search out rebels, reactionaries, and other enemies of the Knomintang and hand them over to the authorities.

Violent Attack on Hongkong

Its manifesto contained a violent attack upon Hongkong as being the fount and origin of all the ills that affiet the province; it asserted that it was the policy of the Hongkong Government to keep the province in a chronic state of unrest, in order to weaken the "attack upon the north," and that for this purpose they were not only subsidising Wu Pei-fu to the tune of millions of dollars, but also financing the rebels and barbits all over the province.

This account of the outpouring of wealth from Hongkong hardly agreed, by the way, with the statement made at the beginning of the document that Hongkong had already been reduced by the boycott to the condition of a "desert island." It is difficult to believe that there is not official support somewhere to this sort of thing, and it makes one wonder where loyalty to the Canton government comes in. The British Consul has rightly protested against the publication of such stuff at the very time when the conference is in session. It would be a different matter if it were a free expression of opinion; but the newspapers, as well as all the numerous "hui" are of course mere organs of the officials, or at least of the Kuomintang. The crowning tout of humour is added by the loud protest of the leading "hui" against this attempt to suppress public opinion!

## NAVAL & MILITARY

Once again the cruiser Yarndoth is being got ready for a trooping trip to the Far East, a voyage of 10,000 miles. She will bring out ratings for the Mediterranean Fleet, East Indies, Squadron, and the China Squadron, as well as relief crews for the sloops Foxglove, Hollyhock, Bluebell and Magnolia, the depot ship (for submarines) Titania and Ambrose, and the mine-sweeper Marazion, which does duty with the 4th Submarine Flotilla. The Yarmouth is a marvellous old cruiser, and holds the long-distance steaming record of the Navy or any other Navy, she having got well into her second 100,000 miles voyaging on trooping trips since the war closed. She was built in 1912. By the London and Glasgow Shipbuilding Co., and was on the China Station, under the command of Captain Henry L. Cochrane, when the late war opened. She participated in the chase of the German cruiser Emden in Chinese and Indian waters; served in the Mediterranean in 1915, and with the light cruiser squadrons of the Grand Fleet the remainder of the war, and on the African Station, 1919-20. For the last four years she has been chiefly employed in trooping trips. "The Yarnouth will leave England in September next for the trip to the Far East."

## HONAN CHILDREN AMPUTATED UNDER GRINDSTONES

BRIGANDS' SHOCKING INHUMANITY.

A letter from a city in Eastern Honan tells of the most terrible inhumanity by brigands, and giving details of brutality which it might be thought no Human beings were capable of. For certain reasons it seems necessary to withhold from publication the name of the place and of the narrator, but these are well attested and can be given upon inquiry from proper quarters. The writer says:

Less than two weeks ago there was awful slaughter in a walled town eighteen li from Sihwa. The towns people had dug "trenches" on the east side of the town to prevent the begiands from attacking without warning. The trap was discovered, whereupon the brigand bands joined up, surrounded the town and did their deadly work. Children's hands and feet were ground off under the wheat grindstone, others were slashed with knives, had kerusene poured over them, and were burned to death. Some sixty or seventy were thrown into wells and heavy stone rolling were thrown in afterwards to press them down. Numbers were burned to death in the town because the houses were set on fire and there was no means of escape from within the walls. Those who found their way out were shot down or stabbed in the wheat field adjacent to the town. Two or three days ago we heard that people from the city and nearer villages had gone "out to bury the dead" and found them in such a state of decomposition, and so numerous, that they just heaped a little earth over them and came away.
