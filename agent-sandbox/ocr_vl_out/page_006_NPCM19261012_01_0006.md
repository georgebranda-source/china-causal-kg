## CORRESPONDENCE

## PLAINER ENGLISH

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"]

Sir,—Sir Oliver Lodge's plen for the use of plainer English by scientists is at least tiltely. A number of the papers read before the British Association were more than the public could digest, and though the journalists who transmitted them were to blame, they were not entirely to blame. All the same, it is not to be fancied that the writer or lecturer on, say, biology is just clumsy when he uses long words of spurious Greek. It is quite likely that he regards simplified science for the million as a sheer abomination. His obscurity may be calculated and defensive. He may think it better not to be understood at all than to be misunderstood copiously.

Or, if he is another person he may use terms, as Butler accused Darwin of using them, because they provide him with ways of retreat in case of necessity.

The scientist who nails his colours to the mast is rarely the scientist who in ten or twenty years will be quoted with approval. Experience of the past teaches the advantage of abundant light and shade, but particularly of shade. One thing, however, is certain. Those who like Sir Oliver Lodge to-day, or Huxley in the last century, discard jargon have faith in themselves and in their works:

Yours, etc.

## AMERICA AND RUBBER

(To the Editor of the "China Mail".)

Sir,—The announcement that the Firestone rubber interests have "definitely abandoned the Philippines as a prospective field for the production of raw rubber will not come as a surprise to those who know anything about the subject. One's chief surprise, if any, is why the "American people have been contented to listen to all the nonsense that has been said concerning the production of rubber on a large scale in the Philippines.

Is it likely that the American investor—and the average American investor is no fool—will sink vast sums of money into rubber plantations when he has seen how badly the British investor has been hit by the slump price "of the raw commodity? One is inclined to go so far as to express the opinion that the majority of American users of crude rubber have not the slightest intention of risking a cent of their money in a tropical agricultural enterprise which they know has just passed through desperately hard times and which still has many difficult problems to face, not the least of which is an epidemic of disease which, at any moment, may arise and wipe out rubber trees by the square mile.

## STATE OF SIEGE

## FOREIGNERS' PLEIGHT IN CHUNGKING

AFTER WANHSIEN AFFAIR

Chungking, the last British treaty port up the Yangtse, has suffered from intense anti-British feeling in the neighbourhood due to the Wanhsien affair, the tension being such that all women and children have been evacuated and for a time (after the Wanhsien affair) there was a possibility of the men being taken off also.

According to a message from Chungking, published in the "Central China Post," an organised attack on the Mackenzie compound, where the majority of British residents were at the time domiciled, had been organised by various bands of T'uan from sur-

rounding districts who combined in anti-British demonstrations, the object being to drive away all remaining foreigners for the purpose of completely looting it. The arrival of the U.S.S. "Monocacy" is said to have led to the abandonment of the plan.

The Chungking correspondent of the “Central China Post,” writing under date of September 20, states:—

Night Watches

The British men on the compound have "formed themselves into a British Defence Corps under an elected" Commandant who works in conjunction with the American S.N.O. We are keeping night watches with a view to anticipating any attacks from outside, and intend to hold on as long as it is possible. Last night, the compound was surrounded by about 200 of the T'uan, and we are reliably informed these men considered the possibility of making an attack. These people seem to have returned to their hills, and we hope they will not return here. It is generally thought the T'uan have definitely abandoned that part of their programme which referred to this compound since it was noticeable that the American gunboat was taking an active interest in matters.

Officials in Sympathy.

City military and civil officials have informed the American Consul that they are in sympathy with the present anti-British agitation but will, they say, do their best to preserve law and order. This merely means that they will not definitely act against us—more than that is unlikely, and we need not expect any help from them. The most that has been done up to now by responsible officials on our behalf was the despatch of two military officers from the city "to preserve order." As a matter of fact, these men were themselves afraid of the local people, and asked to be allowed to stay inside the compound, which request was refused. From this, a fairly correct estimate of the situation as far as the officials intend to interest themselves can be made.

All office staffs are absent from British offices, and it is problematical when they will be able to return, hence, for the time being, it is quite impossible for us to handle cargo if sent up by outside steamers. The present position is that we are practically besieged inside this compound, and while we are able to smuggle out an occasional telegram, or get hold of mail, nothing like ordinary business, is possible for the present. The local Telegraph Office folk are making difficulties by refusing to accept telegrams except those accompanied by cash, and the Telephone people to-day called to collect September subscriptions under veiled threats to cut us off. I have not seen the Compradore nor any of his people for more than a week now.

## OBITUARY

#### MAJ.GEN. SIR WILLIAM GASCOIGNE

Major-General Sir William Julius Gascolgne, K.C.M.G., who was G.O.C. at Hongkong in 1898, an I for a time administered the Government of the Colony, died at Bosecombe on September 9 at the age of 82. General Gascolgne was the son of the late General Ernest Frederick Gascolgne, and was nearly 19 when he joined the Scots Fusilier Guards as an ensign and lieutenant. In 1870—the year of the Riel rebellion—he was in Canada for a few months on particular service. Ten years later he was appointed to the command of the School of Instruction in London for the Auxiliary Forces, which appointment he was holding in July, 1882, when he received orders to embark for Egypt with the Brigade of Guards under the command of the Duke of Connaught. On August 25 he took part in the action of Mahuta and was present at the capture of Tel-el-Kehir on September 13. In 1885 Gascolgne accompanied Sir Charles Wilson in the steamer "Bordein" for the relief of Khartum, when he proved a cool and capable officer during an anxious and trying time. The "Bordein" was often under heavy fire from enemy forces on the banks of the Nile. He was promoted to the rank of lieutenant-colonel in 1887 and became major-general in 1895, when he went to Canada to command the Militia forces. His exit important post was to command the troops at Hong Kong in 1898, and sent the Royal Welsh Fusiliers north during the Boxer uprising. During the absence of Sir Henry Blake, he administered the Government of the Colony, and his services to the community during that time, which included the passing over for the free use of the inhabitants of King's Park, Kowloon, are perpetuated by the naming of one of the peninsula's leading thoroughfares after him, namely, Gascolgne Road. In recognition of his valuable services during a long course of years he was given the C.M.G. in 1899, and in the following year was promoted K.C.M.G. He retired in 1904. In 1875 he married Helen, daughter of the late Martin Tucker Smith, M.P., and widow of the Hon. Arthur E. Egerton (he died in 1866), son of the first Earl of Elvestmere.
