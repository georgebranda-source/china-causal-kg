## ONE OBJECTIVE

## To Obtain Acceptance of Commission's Report

(British Wireless Service.)

## Rugby, Muy N

The debity on the war crisis was opened in the House of Commons this afternoon by Mr. S. Baldwin. Prime Minister in a speech dwelling upon the many difficulties besetting the coal industry which, he said, had been considerably increased for many years past by the organisation of the industry itself and its intricate machinery for wages adjustments. The coal trade had not been wholly happy or settled since the war, and the difficulties which were already showing symptoms about four years ago, were only masked and delayed by the French occupation of the Ruhr which, whilst, it lasted, gave what was really a fetitious value to coal. When the Ruhr occupation was removed the world's supply increased so fast that the world's prices tumbled fast.

## Position Summarised

After reviewing the terms of the report of the Coal Commission the Prime Minister said that the single attempt of the Government had been to obtain the assent of both parties to the recommendations of the report with a view to placing the industry on an efficient and self-governing basis.

He related the course of the negotiations, and said that last Friday night the position was that the miners' representatives were not prepared to consider any immediate reduction of wages or an increase of hours, which meant that the industry could not be carried on without a continuation of the subsidy. The Government could not agree to continue paying indefinitely wages to miners and profits to mine owners at the expense of the taxpayers. The negotiations broke down on Friday night. On Saturday the General Council of the Trades Union Congress expressed its willingness to discuss matters with him, and he invited its committee to come and see him that night. He did so in spite of the threat of a general strike which he ignored for the moment, so anxious was he to see if even at the last moment a way might be found out of the position which had become almost insoluble.

He paid a tribute to the efforts which all had made to reach a settlement, and said that "they had endeavoured to reach a position where the Trades Union Council, on behalf of the miners, would give an assurance to the effect that they felt confident that, given another fortnight, a settlement would be reached on the basis of the Coal Commission's report.

If the Government had been able to get that assurance, it would have risked another fortnight of negotiation.

The discussions were resumed on Sunday evening, and another effort was made to obtain that assurance. Meanwhile, he had learned that certain overt acts had already taken place in anticipation of a general strike. They were acts interfering with the freedom of the Press, and the Government found it impossible to pursue negotiations further. The Government found itself challenged by an alternative body, which was incapable of arresting disobedience to its commands.

## Wrong to Continue

He became convinced last night that the General Council of the Trades Unions was not in control of the situation, and that it would be wrong for him and the Government to continue negotiating unless they got an immediate and unconditional withdrawal of the order for a general strike.

## SITUATION DEPLORED

Mr. J. H. Thomas, Labourite, who was one of the Negotiating Committee of the General Council of Trades Unions, deplored the situation. He emphasised that this was a purely economic dispute, and that there was nothing revolutionary in it. If a ballot were taken in this country he did not believe that two per cent. of the balloters would vote for anything revolutionary.

The responsibility to try to improve the situation rested upon all; and it was for Parliament, as representing the people of the country, to try to avert it. A last effort ought to be made.

## THREAT A MISTAKE

## Pust Pence Effort

Mr. D. Lloyd George, the Liberal leader, appealed to Parliament to see whether something could be done even now to avert a catastrophe. He thought that the threat of a general strike was a mistake. But he pointed out that the men responsible were men who had fought hard against the subversion of the Constitution.

He had seen many strikes, and the House of Commons had always kept its head, and in the end it was the intervention of the House of Commons that had brought about peace. He was certain that that was what would happen here.

He asked whether it would not be possible to resume negotiations? He did not believe that the House would object to a continuance of the subsidy during the fortnight that would be necessary to conclude the negotiations. He begged the Prime Minister to make another effort for peace.

##### T.U.C. CONDEMNED

## Constituents Not Consulted

Sir Robert Horne (Conservative, Hillhead): strongly condemned the action of the Trades Union Council in threatening a general strike. The Council had taken action, he said, without consulting its constituents.

## MINERS' WAGES

## Contingent on Reorganisation

Mr. Ramsay MacDonald, Labour leader, emphasised how much the Trades Union Congress had worked for peace. He claimed that the miners' wages could not be reduced except in relation to the reorganisation of the coal industry. The Coal Commission, in its report, expressed the opinion that any sacrifices by the men should be contingent upon a final agreement for improving the organisation.

In conclusion, he said that if a general strike developed, whatever their views and differences might be, let them enter into it not with hot heads but rather with cool minds, determined to give fair play and justice every time.

## FOR PARLIAMENT ALONE

## Prospect of Progress Desired

Mr. Winston Churchill, Chancellor of the Exchequer, declared that before there could be any continuance of the subsidy, as had been urged, there must be some prospect of matters being further advanced.

As Chancellor of the Exchequer had a greater interest politically in averting a breakdown than any other member of the House. The question whether it was just or not that there should be a continuance of the subsidy in the present circumstances was a question which Parliament and Parliament alone was competent to decide.

A report of the debate will be continued in a special midnight message.

## PRESS DISLOCATED

## Fleet Street Presents Desolate Appearance

London, Later:

One paper no evening newspapers appeared to-day. None will be printed to-morrow throughout the country.

Fleet Street, usually a hive of industry in the evening, has at present a desolate appearance with the exception of hundreds of empty vans.

## REPLACING THE NEWSPAPERS

## Broadcasting at Fixed Hours

Special constables have been ordered to report at the London station of the Broadcasting Company, which is functioning for the issue of official communications which the Government will decide to broadcast.

The Government announces that it is considering the important question of supplying the public with news in newspaper form. Meantime there will be broadcasting of news five times at fixed hours to-morrow.

The Postmaster-General notifies that no foreign or colonial parcels will be accepted, whilst inland packages will be restricted to a maximum of eight ounces. No parcels will be delivered within the London postal area.

The public are urged to economise in the use of telephones, telegraphs, and postal services.

A Board of Trade order establishes a milk pool in London.

The Provision Exchange has agreed to last Friday's market prices being made the maximum prices until further notice.

The Government spokesman, addressing the Pressmen at noon, reported that the news from the country was eminently satisfactory. There was no excitement, and everything was normal. Offers of services were pouring in all over the country. Food stocks were normal on the whole and in many cases above the average.

The Stock Exchange has been disorganised, practically no business being done. Prices have as a precaution been marked down but there is no selling pressure. Home rails are one or two/points down.

## 1914 Scenes Recalled

"They failed! A general strike is on to-morrow." In these words the miner's Secretary, Mr. A. J. Cook, told Reuter of the fateful failure of the negotiations between the Cabinet and the Trade Unionists soon after eleven o'clock last night.

Meanwhile, around the Houses of Parliament, the scenes recalled those of 1914 at the beginning of the war. Thousands completely blocked parliament Square, whilst Downing Street was impassable. Throughout the evening dozens of extra police with the greatest difficulty cleared the way for taxis and motor cars leaving the Palace yard section.

The crowd repeatedly sang "The Red Flag" and raised cheers for the miners. Another section sang "God save the king" and there was a rush at their direction. The police however controlled the crowd and there was no disorder.

## Orderly and Deliberate

Orderly and deliberate was the manner of the general strike of workers in transport, printing, building, and various other industries, which became effective at midnight.

Men whose shifts began before midnight finished their turns of duty. Thus the night omnibus and railway services concluded, the vehicles being taken to the depots. The men did not book on after midnight.

## EARLIER NEWS

The General Council of the Trades Union Congress has sent a long letter to Mr. Baldwin expressing surprise and regret at the Government's decision to terminate the discussions, and declaring the public will judge the nature of the Government's intention by its "precipitate and calamitous decision," and deploring that "the sincere work in which the Council has been engaged to obtain an honourable settlement has been wrecked by the Government's unprecedented ultimatum."

As regards the Government's first reason for breaking off the negotiations, namely, that specific instructions were sent to trade unionists in several industries and services to cease work, the letter says it is not unusual for workmen to cease work in defence of their interests as wage-earners, and the specific reason for the decision in this case was to secure for the workers the same right as employers insist on with their workers, namely, that negotiations shall be

namely, that negotiations shall be conducted free from an atmosphere of strike or lock-out.

The Council disclaims any knowledge of overt acts including interference with the liberty of the Press mentioned in the Government statement, and says the decisions of the Council definitely forbid any such independent and unauthorised action. It regrets it was not given an opportunity of investigating and dealing with the alleged incidents before the Government made them an excuse for breaking off the peace discussions.

The magnitude of the threatened general strike can be gauged by the fact that already a million miners are idle, and the general strike will involve 1,500,000 workers in the transport and allied trades. Meanwhile thousands of volunteers have already enrolled at the temporary recruiting office—a wooden hut in the court yard of the Foreign Office.



The Electrical Power Engineers Association representing technicals employed at the electrical status, have sent a letter to the Premier declaring they have resolved to help to maintain essential public services is connection with the supply of electricity.

Hyde Park was closed to the public at midnight, and is to be used as a milk distributing centre for London. Arrangements have been made to call up special costables.

The "Dilly Mail" leader to which the, printers objected was headed. "For King and Country," and said inter alia: "The general strike is not an industrial dispute. It is a revolutionary moment intended to inflict suffering on the great mass of innocent persons of the community and, therefore, put forcible constraint on the Government. It cannot be tolerated by any civilised Government. We call 'on all law-abiding men and women to hold themselves for the service of King and country.'"

A number of police had to be brought from Downing Street to clear a way for him, and eventually Mr. Churchill was compelled to take refuge in the Home Office.

Mr. Churchill while walking from Downing Street to Whitehall was rushed by a crowd which the police were keeping back on the opposite side of Whitehall. They rushed across and hemmed in the Chancellor of the Exchequer.

Meanwhile a "queue of a thousand strong was Hining up in the Foreign Office quadrangle, waiting to sign on as volunteers to help the Government.

The mobbing incident is minimised by spectators, who declare that the crowd blocked the Chancellor's path through force of numbers, but little hostility was displayed.

The Japanese miners are expected to send similar messages to-morrow, whilst it is reported the leading labour organisations are discussing the question of supporting the British strikers in all ways possible.

It is reported that after a special meeting this afternoon the Japan Transport Union have cabled to the British Transport Union "Our comrades will fight to the last."

## OPERATIONS CONSEQUENT ON SUBSIDENCE

## MAINTENANCE WORK

Diving operations in progress off the entrance to Queen's pier, a “China Mail” representative learned on enquiry, were in connection with the replacement of portions of the rubble mounds on which the concrete entrance to the pier rested and which had subsided, an occurrence which is always a possibility with such foundations until they have thoroughly set, a somewhat lengthy process.

The action of the 1923 typhoon is thought to have been responsible in part for the original trouble.

The work of replacement which is included under the head of Maintenance, is now practically completed.
