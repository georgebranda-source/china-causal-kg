## TO SUPPRESS PIRACY

There will naturally be considerable misgivings in regard to the latest piratical outrage that resulted in a China Merchants' steamer being controlled by pirates for over nine hundred miles and taken to the usual rendezvous at Bias Bay. It may well be asked how much longer are these outlaws to infest the China Sea and interfere with merchant vessels? Is it not high time that the Governments of Hong Kong and Canton made common war against the pirates and rooted them out once and for all from Bias Bay?

If Mr. Eugene Chen is in earnest in his desire to tackle the piracy queen he is placed in a position of immense advantage in that Bias Bay is in Kwangtung territory. Moreover, ninety-nine per cent. of the victims of these

In the course of an interview with a Press representative Mr. Eugene Chen is reported to have declared that he hoped that "arrangements for the termination of the boycott would proceed smoothly, after which "we shall then be able to tackle the piracy question, perhaps with the help of the British Government." This, like his utterances in respect of the ending of the boycott, is a definite statement. There is not the slightest qualification. It is a bold declaration of policy in the campaign against the pirates, and, as such, it is one that must be respected but never forgotten. The pessimists, who—like the poor—are always with us, have been sceptical about Mr. Eugene Chen's boycott statements and about his ability to keep his word. He himself, it is reported, has admitted that he was unaware that the boycott movement was so deep-rooted—hence the delay in giving effect before October 10 to the Canton Government's resolve to terminate the boycott. Unless steps are taken without further, and to wipe out the pirates and their stronghold at Bias Bay Mr. Eugene Chen may likewise have to confess that he did not realise how deep-rooted are these pests and that the "tackling" process may take much longer than he imagines.

In the matter of the help of the British Government Mr. Eugene Chen knows full well already that this has always been available and will continue to be available without the slightest scruple. We have the men and we have the ships only too eager to embark on a punitive expedition against Bias Bay. And there is no need to tell Mr. Eugene Chen that unless the question of "tackling" the piracy question is accelerated by the Kwangtung Government the work will be taken out of its hands by the British armed forces. However, co-ordination and co-operation between the Kwangtung and the British Governments is naturally the best course to pursue, and, on the settlement of the boycott, we shall await the next move by Mr. Eugene Chen and his colleagues in the way of "tackling" the piracy question with considerable interest. He has promised to do so. We look to him to fulfil his promise in the same hope and faith as we now regard his word of honour regarding the termination of the boycott on October 10.

## CORRESPONDENCE

## TO MY CHINESE FRIENDS

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"] Sir,—Knowing that your paper is very popular amongst my Chinese friends, I would thank you to insert my letter as it may help to a better understanding among us and give both sides a chance to air their views on the housing matter.

Now, my Chinese friends, don't think I am out to find fault with you or that I think that we Europeans are perfect. No, no, I do know that we are all imperfect and don't see it. We all have habits which among ourselves, or our own caste or class, we find natural, while they are irritating and annoying to outsiders; and this is a point which gives more annoyance in the matter of housing than any other.

For instance, a Chinese friend of mine built a row of flats, quite modern, and suitable for a couple or a bachelor to live in. As my friend said, he will be very particular in choosing his tenants. I took a flat on the top floor but what was my surprise when moving in, to find that the ground and first floors were occupied by Chinese. Finding that they were nicely and cleanly furnished and looked intelligent, I made no objection.

It was a hot day and tired out after a day's moving work, I started for dinner but hardly sat down when the unearthly yelling and howling of a dog made me look for the cause. Looking down I found on the first floor a little poodle tied to a chair trying to free himself and yelling in a most heart-rending manner while the family was sitting around and were not disturbed by all this noise. Now I cannot imagine a European who can

ly to free the dog which they did and I went to my table with good hope to have a quiet dinner and rest afterwards but soon found/out that I reckoned without my neighbours. Suddenly the ear-piercing yelling of a child started, then another voice arose, probably the amaah trying to question the child by overshouting it. Then some other voice was heard which tried to over-shout the amaah and the child, then came a deep bass voice which overshouted all of them but it seems the child knew better for it had a good lung and continued to shout loud enough to be heard over all the others. This went on for at least 40 minutes and may have lasted longer had I not shouted down to "shut up" and I am sure my Chinese friends must have thought me very rude but I could not stand it any longer. Now we know children must cry. We know there are naughty children and parents will try to keep them quiet first by talking to them nicely but if that does not help we all know that a little spanking well help and that a child must know it has a master just as much as a dog. Not so with Chinese. They will not spank children, especially boys, and a child once it knows it gets no punishment when it is naughty, will play havoc with you; it will grow up conceited and bad. It is too late when the boy is over ten years old to remodel him. He must know who is his master while he grows up. I have seen amaah many a time imitating a child when it cries, instead of trying to quieten it. No wonder that children get sulky and bad tempered. It is for the welfare of children and especially for boys that I call the attention of my Chinese friends to this matter; as every medical man will tell you, long and loud crying is very dangerous to kiddies' health.

It was past 10 when I wanted to sleep. An uproar of voices, shuffling, and splashing of water woke me out of my first slumber. The servants started to do the washing with a noise and loud shouting that upset all the former noises. My European neighbour in the next house informed me that this used to go on in their house sometimes until 3 in the morning till one day they applied to the Police who made a little order in the matter, explaining to them that in European quarters Chinese have to adhere to the law and cannot make noises like that late at night or early in the morning. This again is a point to be spoken about. First of all I am sure that no European can get a servant wash up to 3 in the morning and I would like to impress this on our great labour leaders at home and out here who think that Europeans great labour

I don't object to Chinese music on the gramophone—nor to the Chinese cymbal and singing to it—but I do object to the monotonous flute playing, to which I have to listen to the practising of a single note and which never starts before 10 p.m. Why not shut doors and windows when going on for such practices which certainly are nerve-racking? Generally about midnight there are two men holding a conversation at the top of their voices which would carry 3 miles broadcasting length without a broadcaster. This goes on sometimes until early in the morning and resounds all over the house, doors and windows being open. I am sure it is not to annoy anybody they do so but they simply don't know any better.

I suggest to you, my Chinese friends, if you can't be without noises—shut your windows or if you enjoy the yelling of dogs or babies put them in the kitchen or outhouses and close doors and windows after 10 p.m. and give your neighbours a chance to sleep.

I may give you some more advice on sanitary and social matters but for the present, please don't think that any spite or ill-feeling prompted me to these lines. It is simply to show you that all species and classes have their habits which are annoying to others and if any of you can tell me which foreign habits are annoying to you, tell me so and you may be sure we will do our best to please you. Before closing, let me tell you it is not pleasant when coming home to find the main entrance to the house blocked by servants and children. Mind, I like the children, and have a little joke for them every-time I meet them and they greet me wherever they see me with a cheery hallof

I hope that Chinese papers will copy this letter and that it will help to a better understanding and nobody will be more pleased than your sincere friend.

Hong Kong, Oct. 5.

## RUSSO-ASLATIC BANK'S AFFAIRS

WHAT EXPERTS SAY!

Mr. Baine's letter in connection with our report of Mme. Stavrjanki's deposits and transactions with the Russo-Asiatic Bank raises the question of the liability of the local branch of the Russo-Asiatic Bank to that of the Bank as a whole. It was therefore deemed expedient, in the interests of all concerned, to obtain as much information on the subject as possible.

In the course of an interview with the "China Mail", Mr. C. L. C. Sandes, manager of the local branch of the Mercantile Bank of India, Ltd., stated that it was a general principle of international banking to law that every branch of a bank could be called upon cash a letter of credit issued by the head office or any other branch of the same bank.

That principle certainly applied to his, and as far as he knew, to all other British banks.

Mr. Sandes was unable to say whether or not it applied to the Russo-Asiatic Bank.

. Questioned as to the present legal position of the Rusao-Asiatic Bank as far as extra-territorial jurisdiction in China was concerned, Mr. Sandee said that since the cleavage with the "Reds" some few years ago, the Bank had been managed by a committee with headquarters in Paris.

The French Minister in Peking had made representations to the Waichiaopu on the subject which was still under discussion.

Branches Still Solvent?

He understood that an endeavour was being made for local liquidation as it was a well-known fact that certain branches of the Bank were perfectly solvent.

The “China Mail” made three abortive attempts to interview Mr. Baines with a view to offering him every opportunity to add anything to what he had already stated in his letter, but was unsuccessful.

Mr. A. Lecot, manager of the Banque de l'Indo Chine, agreed in principle to the remarks of Mr. Sandes, adding that he felt confident that a solution would be found and that, as far as Hong Kong was concerned, depositors in the local branch of the Russo-Asiatic Bank would be paid in full.

## VAUDEVILLE

### GLOBE TROTTERS AT THE STAR. PEGGY JAMES ILL

Mr. Dick Norton certainly deserves the sympathy of his many friends in Hong Kong. The typhoon is responsible for much. Dick, himself, nearly lost his voice and now vivacious Miss Peggy James, who partners Mr. Norton in a number of items, is laid up with fever and was unable to appear last night.

In the circumstances, the second programme of the season suffered considerably, and particular credit is due to the remainder of the Company for "keeping the "flag flying." This would particularly apply to Mr. Leo Brooker, the light comedian of the show, who worked like a Trojan. We are glad, however, to note that there is every prospect of Miss James appearing this evening. Mrs. Norton, (Miss Lolla Forbes) is due to arrive from New Zealand to-morrow, and will appear both at to-morrow's show as well as on Saturday evening.

The concerted numbers last evening were quite entertaining, one or two numbers being really clever — notably “alphabetical poker.” Ralph Lynne in a couple of female impersonations scored heavily, and Herbert Morris received quite an ovation for his rendering of Dvork's “Humour-esque.” Mr. Dimoff's playing on the balalaika remains the outstanding attraction of the show.

## CARMI THOMPSON

## DISTINGUISHED VISITOR IN HONG KONG

"CHINA MAIL" CHAT.

## -

The "President Jackson" arrived this morning and on board was Colonel Curmit Thompson.

Colonel Thompson was sent by President Coolidge to make a general survey of the economic conditions of the Philippines, in the middle of last July.

During his stay, he visited all its provinces. He spoke in glowing terms of the undeveloped natural resources of the islands and its possibilities. He made special mention of the riches of the Philippines in the form of rubber, iron and coal which according to him were lying idle waiting for capital to develop them.

Colonel Thompson left Manila on October 4 instead of October 28, contrary to his previous plan.

The numerous persons whom the Colonel had to see, those who craved for an occasion to interview him, and the almost daily receptions and balls held in his honour, were some of the reasons for his premature departure from Manila. "If I were to stay there until the 28th as I had planned, I am afraid I will not be able to make my report to President Coolidge.

On his return to America, Col. Thompson will submit a report to President Coolidge of his survey, in the Philippines, and he will also recommend measures that should be taken by the United States to advance the prosperity of the Filipino people.

## Regret at Departure:

When interviewed by a “China Mail” representative, Col. Thompson said: “It was with a genuine regret that I left the Philippine My stay there has been profitable to me, and I hope it will benefit all the people of those islands. I will always look back upon the past three months as the most interesting period of my life. I owe an everlasting debt of gratitude to the Filipino people for the helpful consideration which they have extended to me and my party. From the moment we landed on those shores, to the moment of our leaving, they have always extended to us their friendship and their unbounded hospitality.”

## Confidence in Future

When asked as to his opinion as to "Whether the Philippines were ready for independence, and the economic conditions of the Philippines, Colonel Thompson refused to make any statement and said: "It is apparent that I should not discuss matters to be considered in my report until I have presented it to President Coolidge, but without violating my instructions, however, I can say that the Filipino people in all walks of life are hungry for education.

“I have noted with great satisfaction a desire among the Americans and the Filipinos alike for some constructive policy, with will ensure the economic development of the country. I have said on many occasions, and I will say it again, that the Philippine Islands are on the threshold of the most prosperous era in their history. They have just begun to understand the wealth of those islands, and to realise that the world is waiting for the production of its soil, metals, and its minerals from the mountains and the priceless lumber from the forests. When this prosperity is accomplished, it will result in a higher standard of living for all the people; better working conditions for labour and higher wages. This development will innure to the benefit of the Filipino people and will fix them on that firm foundation where they can compete with the commerce of the world.”

## Stay at Peking?

Colonel Thompson is accompanied by Mr. James Jappe, who is the executive secretary to the Colonel. Mr. Russell Porter is also traveling with the colonel as the special correspondent of the "New York times."

The party will continue their voyage on the "Jackson" and will disembark at Shanghai. It conditions are favourable, the party expect to visit Peking. During the colonel's stay there, he expects to prepare his report to President Coolidge, and will continue his trip from Shanghai on the "President Jefferson."
