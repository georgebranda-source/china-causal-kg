Hongkong, Saturday, Sept. 11, 1926.

The past week must be numbered among the most important in the history of China in that within it have been encompassed events which are not only of great importance as affecting the balance of power in China but which have involved relationships between her and foreign powers. The Nationalist troops have undoubtedly succeeded beyond expectations on the Hankow front and Wu Pei-fu seems temporarily eclipsed, at any rate. The deciding factor in regard to the outcome of further hostilities between the North and South seems to rest with Marshal Sun Chuanfang and if, as has been reported, he has issued an ultimatum to the Nationalists and already adopted hostile action, the position of the latter would indeed be a precarious one: Chang Kai-shek, in advancing across the Yangtse, has in effect burnt his boats and with a hostile force both before and behind him he will need all his skill and all the armaments which came into his possession with the fall of Hanyang to succeed in attempts either to force his way further Northwards or consolidate his position at the scene of his recent triumphs.

Whilst interest still centres on the movements of foreign gunboats consequent on the succession of outrages on British and other mercantile marine vessels and the distressing incident at Wanhaien which resulted in the loss of British officers and men, it is obvious that until the foreign ministers at Peking and their respective Governments have formulated a

policy, the Naval forces on the China station can only hold themselves in readiness for emergencies. So far as the British Navy is concerned the Wanhisen incident closed with the rescue of the officers of the two captured boats and the bombardment of the town harbouring the Chinese troops which not only refused to release the British officers but opened intense fire on the British naval forces which carried out the rescue. The two British ships are still in the hands of the Chinese forces and as none but the small gunboats can approach the region there is likelihood of a naval expeditionary force being despatched to effect their release, a course which would in all probability lead to considerable bloodshed. The situation is still further complicated by the action of Chang Kaisbek's forces at Hankow in firing on British vessels entering the port and whilst the naval forces in those waters are sufficient to guarantee protection to foreign residents in any eventuality the situation as regards shipping is a delicate one. It is doubtful if direct retaliatory action will be taken in the absence of international agreement on the issues involved but, on the other hand, vessels engaged in legitimate trade carried on in accordance with treaties to which China has been a party have a right to the protection which the naval forces in the China seas afford and in the carrying out of which at Wanhisen they conducted themselves with such gallantry.

## Recreation Ground Shortage

Even the Government can hardly deny that there is a demand for more ground in the Colony for recreation. Every season an appointed committee meets to allocate the various pitches to different applicants. No club has yet been known to appeal against a decision, but there have been instances of "humble petitions" by organisations whose scope has been enlarged. At yesterday's meeting of the Hongkong Football Club the question was raised as to whether such grounds are private or public property. From what was gathered it appears that a site is private to the extent that it can be used on specific occasions—which may be on certain days of the week or continually—but the Government remains the owner. Have outsiders, therefore, the right to use such grounds when they are not occupied by those to whom it has been set down? We are in sympathy with rugger enthusiasts who have branched out into their own club, hoping to get a better ground in King's Park, and more time for use if practicable. The argument of the Football Club is also worthy of consideration. Opens paces for recreation in Hong Kong are limited. Everybody wants to play on a Saturday and some are solving the difficulty by playing on Sundays. Not having consulted expert opinion, we can only say that the solution lies in migrating to the New Territory. Playing pitches may be found there to suit those who do not mind going a distance, and for branches of sport where accommodation for spectators does not count a great deal. There are also possibilities in New Kowloon.

### Spelling of “Hong Kong.”

Last Saturday it was officially announced in the "Government Gazette". That the Secretary of State had fixed the spelling of the Colony's name as "Hong Kong." Hitherto there has been no general practice. Some have been content to make it one word "Hongkong." Others have taken a fancy to two capitals in the eight letters. We are open to correction but it has appeared to us that permanent residents favour the unofficial spelling. Our experience has also been that in the case of envelopes from Europe and America, the more favoured rendering is "Hong Kong, China." The addition of "China" may be put down to a desire for clearer guidance to the Post Office. Strictly speaking, as any Chinese scholar will agree, the Colony's name should comprise two distinct words. It is Chinese, of course. Reference books say that the name originally meant "Fragrant Streams." "Heung meant'sweet' and is mispronounced." "Hong." "Kong" as it first stood, was for "river" and indicated waters. The second character in "Sweet Waters (or River)" has also been mispronounced, as the sound of the second word in our Chinese name means harbour rather than water. There arises the question of firms that have incorporated the word "Hongkong" in their name. We have come across a good many and there seem to be only a few with "Hong Kong" in the letterheads. We suggest that for names already in use the spelling, whichever it may be, be continued; while in reference to the place, we follow the Government's decision.
