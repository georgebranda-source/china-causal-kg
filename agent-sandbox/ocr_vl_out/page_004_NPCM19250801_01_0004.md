### "TAKE MEN AND MONEY."

The idea of conscripting both men and money in the event of war, is gaining ground. The world war saw the almost universal adoption of conscription, as it related to man-power, by countries concerned, but not that of wealth. An approach to such a state of affairs was made when, as in the case of Britain, certain industries were temporarily nationalised and prompt and effective use was made of them to speed on the war to a successful conclusion. The extension of this idea is summed up in the words: "The whole power of national service, lives and property, for the defence of the nation." Those who seek to promote the adoption of the suggestion, and are urging legislation on it, hail principally from America and Canada. They argue that war can only be effectively, and economically, carried on, and quickly brought to a conclusion, if conscription of wealth and man-power is the rule and not the exception. In this way rich and poor, old and young, contribute to a common cause. The pacifist with the individual with a pronounced belief in the force of arms, is, so to speak thrown into the common melting pot. The profiteer is automatically obliterated, whilst the arm-chair critic, the dispenser of white feathers and the conscientious objector, by a machine-like process become as one in an effort that, because it is a piece of law on a statute book, may go a long way to prevent the outbreak of war, and in the event of war is likely to finish it in a very short space of time. The idea has about it a considerable amount of commonsense. We are inclined to suggest that the Colony should have a similar piece of legislation in mind to guard against the possibilities of strikes which are nothing less than attacks on the stability of a friendly place and people..... The suggestion is thrown out as a suitable and profitable week-end discussion that should be the cause of some interesting thought. Thrice armed is he who hath his quarrel just, but the getting in of the first blow is also part of a piece of uncontradicted wisdom. We know nothing better to meet such unjust quarrels than the one forced upon us, than a solid wall of conscriptive effort as has been indicated.

## Our Regard for the Past

Much has been done in recent years both through public subscription and by private effort to preserve for the nation, woods, open spaces and buildings of historic interest. In 1912 it was realised that the suburbs of London must soon extend to Stoke Poges, and that the speculative builder might endanger the seclusion of the country churchyard with inspired Gray's "Elegy." Accordingly, three acres with the Penn Memorial were bought and presented by the late Sir Bernard Oppenheimer and Mr. W. A. Judd, and later, Canon A. T. Barnett and Mr. Judd secured the remaining ten acres of beautiful meadow land fringed with trees and held it until the purchase price could be raised. The deeds have now been handed over to Lord Grey of Fallodon as representing the National Trust. The centenary of Keats is being celebrated by the saving of the old house, with its beautiful garden, at Hampstead, in which much of the poet's finest work was produced. In 1920 the property came into the market as "an eligible building site," but a National Committee was formed and with generous assistance from America the freehold has been bought. It is hoped to restore the premises as a resting-place for the Keats relics bequeathed by Sir Charles Dilke. Lord Curzon's tenure of the Indian Vice-royalty was made memorable by the restoration of many old buildings and monuments and in recent years he took a leading part in similar work in Britain. ## CHINA'S PROBLEM

POWERS TO MEET SHORTLY?

## London, July 81

Reuter learns that nothing is known in well-informed circles regarding any United States proposal for a conference at Peking in the autumn on the question of extraterritoriality in China, though it is true that an Anglo-American exchange of views has been progressing on the subject. It is not thought that anything so concrete has hitherto emerged, but it is known that the British Government has all along been agreeable to the holding of such a conference when practicable. It is felt that the improvement in the situation in China, now apparent, is such as might warrant the convoking of the Powers as envisaged in the Washington Treaty.

## CORRESPONDENCE

SHAMELIN INCIDENT.

To the Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—The President of the "Canton Christian College" in a very belated explanation (over a month after the incident occurred) states that not one of the framers of the now notorious Resolutions had "any idea whiver of suggesting which side fired first."

But the first Resolution states that "a the afternoon of June 23, 1925, a Chinese patriotic parade marching along the Bund and Shaki, Canton, was fired upon by Shameen troops."

The second Resolution states that “the Parade was proceeding in an orderly manner.”

A subsequent Resolution proclaims that its frumers are "of opinion that the blame and responsibility for the merciless and unjustified assault rests upon those who directed the fire from the Slumeen."

These statements were flung broadcast about the world with all the authority of 17 American professors of an American College of standing.

But to quote Dr. Henry, the Resolutions were "personal expressions of opinion"—do they read like that and under the circumstances could they be taken as such?

Dr. Henry says that before leaving the campus on the morning of June 24, he suggested that the few remaining members of the Foreign Staff (there were obviously 17 still there) should frame some Resolutions suitable to the occasion. Is it usual in American Colleges for appeals to the American Government, and the American people on matters involving the graves international complications to be issued by individual members of the Staff, acting collectively as such, without the Head of the College seeing the text of the document either before or after it was promulgated? ## RUBBER EXPORTS

## INCREASED PERCENTAGE ALLOWED

CONCESSIONS APPROVED.

(Reutor's Servicè.)

## London, July 31

The Colonial Office states that it is officially announced that the percentage of the standard production of "rubber" exportable under the minimum duty from Ceylon, the Straits-Settlements and the Malay States for the quarter beginning August 1 has been fixed at 75, being a ten per cent. increase over the preceding quarter.

His Majesty's Government have approved certain concessions with regard to the assessment of standard production in Malaysia proposed by the High Commissioner of the Malay States. It is anticipated that the effect thereof, together with the recent release by the High Commissioner under legal advice of 6,000 tons of uncoupled rubber in Malaysia, will materially ease the situation.

EARLIER NEWS.

The industrial group in the House of Commons received a deputation of the Rubber Manufacturers' Association, who pleaded for a modification of the Stevenson scheme to the extent of 5 to 10 per cent. above the present level, otherwise, it was declared, many of the smaller rubber manufacturers would be compelled to go out of business within the next few months. The group promised to consider all the facts and seek an early opportunity of discussing the matter with Mr. L. C. M. S. Amery, Secretary of the Colonies.

## DEARER PAPER

## INDIA INDUSTRY'S OUTLOOK

#### THE USE OF BAMBOO. (Reuter's Service.)

## Simla, India, July 31

The report of the Tariff Board on the application of Protection to the paper and paper pulp industries opines that the industry is not likely to survive without protection; it therefore recommends that the existing 15 per cent. ad valorem duty on all sorts of writing paper and printing paper, other than newsprint, containing 65 per cent. t.m.r. more mechanical pulp, be changed to a specific duty of one anna a pound. No change of duty is proposed on any other kind of paper.

The Board does not recommend protective duty on pulp. The board opines that bamboo pulp holds immense possibilities in the production of cheap paper in India and recommends the Government to explore the question fully. By assisting the bamboo pulp industry up to the maximum Government liability of twenty lakhs the Board holds that abundant supplies of bamboo are likely to become the basis of a considerable industry, capable of meeting the country's requirements, and even providing substantial quantities of paper pulp for export if world prices of paper remain as at present. The bamboo development will be slow, but the rapid diminution of European and American supplies of coniferous wood, justifies the belief that the increase in the price of paper is inevitable.
