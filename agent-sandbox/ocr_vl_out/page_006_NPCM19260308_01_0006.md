## Hongkông; Mơndng, March 8, 1926

A striking revelation is reported to-day to have been made by Wu Pei-fu and the allies, although it may be discredited by some as a form of counter-propaganda common to the Chinese rival war lords. It is alleged that, after the retreat of the Second Kuominchan Army, Bolshevik pamphlets were found by the successful allied troops addressed to farmers and labourers; thus proving, it is asserted, that the Kuominchun and its leader, "the "Christian" General, Feng Yu-hsiang, are pro-Bolshevist. In the face of this important discovery—which, of course, has yet to be proved beyond the shadow of a doubt—the Allied military leaders declare that they "will fight the Red Government until it is destroyed."

There may be a semblance of truth in the story. Prominence is given in the "North China Daily News" of March 3 to a message from Mr. Rodney Gilbert from Peking, who reports that "the Russian Red Conference held in the Capital resulted in a decision to "extend all possible support to the present military patrons of the Peking Government." It was also reported that Moscow had agreed to make further advances of men, money and arms. The conference, to which many Chinese were admitted, opposed "Comrade" Sverdloff's coming and the establishment of a branch of the Third International at this juncture, since the time was not thought of to withdraw support from the Kuominchun politicians and military. In an arresting paragraph Mr. Rodney Gilbert says:

Moscow has been told that since anti-Red slogans were the war cries of Peking's opponents, the collapse of the Kuominchun would mean the suppression of all Soviet work in North China. Hence it must make every effort to save the cause by supporting Marshal Feng Yu-hsiang, A-sum of $6,500,000 has therefore, been made available together with some arms. The Embassy also asked for 7,000 troops, including 800 men, half in uniform and half in mufti, to control Peking, but it is unknown whether the Soviet has agreed to this.

Taking this report in conjunction with Réuter's message today regarding the discovery of Bolshevik literature addressed to farmers and labourers, it would certainly appear on the surface that the Allied war lords have not exposed anything in the nature of a mare's nest. The "Christian General's connections with Moscow have long been under suspicion, apart from the mere fact of a purely commercial transaction involving the purchase of arms from Russia, and although his henchmen have always strenuously denied that he is pro-Bolshevik, the average individual can form his own conclusions. The sinister plot of the Reds to dominate the Capital, as revealed at the Russian Red Conference, cannot but strengthen the arguments of those who have before now vehemently denounced the "Christian General's coquetting with Moscow.

Apart from the reasons actuating the Allied war lords in participating in the continuance of civil war in China, the best friends of China cannot but hope that, provided the connection between the Reds and Peking is an accomplished fact, Feng Yu-hsiang and those who would sell their country to Moscow may be eliminated for all time as the result of the present struggle.

## BROADCASTING

What should prove an interesting pastime in Hongkong, practically is non-existent. Evenings which may be considered dull, remain so when they could easily be brightened by a means which is bringing in its train much progressive revolution—introducing new ideas, and bringing thousands in touch with such things as good music. There may be good reasons for the lack of broadcasting facilities in the Colony. It cannot be said that what Hongkong thinks or does to-day, Shanghai or Manila, copies to-morrow. Manila is controlled by Americans. It seems to follow that what is good enough for say, New York, is also good enough for the people of the islands—or those who live in its very fine capital city. The result is that broadcasting is not a new innovation; it is as common almost as going to the theatre or playing a gramophone; and that Manila rejoices in broadcasting programmes. Shanghai is perhaps so much "alive" in its social activities by reason of the diverse and interesting nationalities that compose its population. Shanghai has its quota of broadcasters, with programmes that bring delight entertainment and instruction. Those recently arrived from home speak glowingly of the broadcasting services which exist in Britain, and the joy they bring, not merely to crowded areas, but to those seemingly "out off" from the hurlily burly of life. It is now being suggested that broadcasting be made a public service at the end of the present year; and that a Corporation consisting of frame-five to seven nominees chosen by the Crown be appointed to take over the staff contracts, and apparatus of the existing Company; that the Commissioners be persons of

Judgment and independence and empowered to consult appropriate societies, organisations, and advisory committees to ensure proper consideration of all phases of broadcasting. There are other interesting details connected with what seems a piece of pure socialism—all designed to make broadcasting more perfect and interesting than it is. But we are concerned with its use in the Colony, and the desire to see Hongkong in line with its more advanced neighbours — Shanghai and Manila.

# COAL COMMISSION.

UNANIMOUS REPORT NOW READY

VOLUMINOUS DOCUMENT.

(British Wireless Service.)

It is officially stated that the report of the Coal Commission was signed last night by Sir Herbert Samuel, Chairman, and the other members of the Commission.

It is understood that the report, which is a unanimous one, will be presented to His Majesty the King to-morrow.

In view of the wide terms of reference and the immense field is surveyed in the report, much importance is attached to the findings of the Commission, which are at present a closely guarded secret.

A great volume of evidence was placed before the Commissioners, and quite apart from this and the voluminous statistics, the main body of the report is said to extend to nearly 300 closely printed pages.

The executive of the Miners' Federation will meet in London this week to consider the Commission's recommendations, and will on Friday present a report to the National Delegate Conference of the Federation in London.

# MID LEVELS

The Committee of the Mid Levels Association has issued to the members the report of the first period of the Association's existence. (July 31 to December 31, 1925).

The Hon. Secretary and Hon. Treasurer's statement of accounts shows a balance in hand on December 31, 1925, of $113.61.

Since the formation of the Association 89 members have been enrolled. The present membership is 83, the reduction being due to departures from the Colony, etc.

Four meetings of the committee have been held since the general meeting on July 21 last.

Apart from matters connected with the strike, the Committee have brought to the notice of the Government several matters which they considered would be for the benefit of members, such as the provision of a children's playground near May Road Station, the unsatisfactory Chair service at the Robinson Chair Stand and the question of Traffic Control.

In response to representations by the Committee the Peak Tramways Company, Limited, have met the wants of residents using the May Road Station by altering the time to suit their convenience. It is hoped that the request which was also made that an adequate shelter be provided at this station will be acceded to in the near future.

The Committee extends their cordial thanks to the Committee of the Ladies' Recreation Club for the use of their Club room on several occasions, to Mrs. Harman for allowing the Committee meetings to be held in her flat, and to Mr. E. J. R. Mitchell for auditing the accounts.

It is hoped that members will help the Committee by inducing their friends to join the Association and that they will bring to the notice of the Committee any matters which they consider can be usefully dealt with by the Association.

The report is signed by Mr. A. S. D. Cousland, as Chairman.

## BALKAN PACT

## GREECE AND ITALY MAKE FIRST MOVE

Géneva, March 7.

It is understood that M. Roufos, the Greek Minister of Foreign Affairs, who is representing Greece at the League Assembly, discussed with Signor Mussolini in Rome the possibility of a sort of Greco-Italian pact or guarantee of integrity and security in Albania.

It is learned in Greek circles that such a pact, to which Yugo-Slavia may also adhere, will be the starting point toward a Balkan Pact, in which Italy will constitute a Halson between it and the Locarno Pact.
