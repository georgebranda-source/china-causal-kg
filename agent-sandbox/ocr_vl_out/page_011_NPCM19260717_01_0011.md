# SOMETHING A KIN TO EXTRA TERRITORIALITY WANTED.

Harbin, June 28.—The Soviet delegation to the Mukden Conference has issued a statement protesting against the work of "hostile elements," which work has as its object the breaking up of the Conference. After outlining the Soviet's demands at length, the statement sets forth the Soviet's aims where the status of Russians in Manchuria is concerned as follows:—

Soviet Citizens' Rights.

"Considering the work of the Railway from a commercial viewpoint, one cannot, but agree that the present status of Soviet citizens, who have no legal standing whatsoever, is obviously detrimental to the Railway. Every commercial enterprise, especially one in which two governments are concerned and which has in view the carrying on of normal work and its own prosperity, must, first of all, concern itself with the legal standing of its vital working force which carries on the complicated work of the undertaking. Any abnormality in the position of the employee or workman in the enterprise will have its harmful effect upon its economic development and upon its strength. Judging from an objective viewpoint, we cannot refrain from mentioning that a considerable part of the Chinese workmen and employees of the Chinese Eastern Railway, through enjoying better legal standing than the citizens of the U.S.S.R., need to be put on an equal basis with Soviet citizens in matters of an economic and cultural character, namely, they must be granted equal remuneration similar living quarters, as well as their own clubs, etc.

"It is well known that the employees of the Chinese Eastern Railway and the citizens of the U.S.S.R. in general do not claim special privileges on Chinese territory as do other foreigners who are citizens of imperialistic governments."

The Judicial Commission.

"The Judicial Commission consisting of representatives of various countries defending extraterritorial rights in China, has paid a visit to Harbin, and it has been its intention to gain some political profit by using those who are deprived of any legal standing as proof of the necessity of retaining the extraterritorial rights in China. Soviet citizens in North Manchuria will never do such a thing as to take recourse to a third party in matters of establishing their normal legal standing on the territory of the Chinese people; they will take up this question with the representatives of their country and of China."

"The employees of the Chinese Eastern Railway and citizens of the U.S.S.R. residing in North Manchuria, having no legal standing, are unable to live quietly and to carry on their peaceful business which otherwise would lead to the strengthening of the economic life of this country whose owners are the Chinese people."

"The lack of legal standing has a bad effect upon a number of other questions which cannot be solved successfully."

"Owing to certain circumstances, for which neither of the two parties concerned is to blame," a number of vital questions, having a most serious bearing upon the Chinese Eastern Railway have not been solved; the question as to the administration of the railway by representatives of both countries has not been decided and is, consequently, still pending.

"The absence of a fixed outline for the work of the Chinese Eastern Railway affects its economic situation, especially when it concerns the laying of sidings which are to help increase the freight transportation of the railway. This very important question, upon which depends the development and the prosperity of the railway, cannot be solved because the parties concerned have not come to any agreement on the subject and, naturally, when the Soviet-Mukden Conference was convened, great interest was exhibited and attention shown on the part of all people connected with the railway."



"It is thought by the Soviet delegation that should the question as to the establishment of normal conditions on the Chinese Eastern Railway not be solved within the nearest future, the interests of the railway and those of the U.S.S.R. and Chinese will suffer in the end, resulting in a grossly unsatisfactory state of affairs for both countries."

"Judging from the hostile Manchurian press and from the activities of third parties who are leading an exasperating war against the establishment of Sino-Soviet friendship, it becomes clear that these parties are striving in every way to militify the progress and work of the Conference.

The hostile press, pursuing definite ideas, spreads rumours to the effect that the representatives of the U.S.S.R. have entered into agreements with a certain Power, and that these agreements infringe the interests of China. It is quite clear that these forces for evil are aiming to ruin the efforts that are being made to establish normal relations between the representatives of the U.S.S.R. and Mukden. It seems to us that both the Soviet and Chinese governments could find, for the benefit of the two countries, a mutual understanding and decide the fundamental problems which have been brought up at the Soviet-Mukden Conference.

"The prophecies and expectations of the enemies of Sino-Soviet friendship and of normal conditions that ought to be established on the Railway, must be defeated. This can only be done by both parties striving to settle once for all, the questions raised at the Conference."

"It is hoped by the Soviet delegation that all the disagreement existing between the two sides will be overcome, it being necessary for both sides to trust one another, Furthermore, neither side should single out the questions brought up at the Conference but should decide them in all their scope."

"We find that it is high time that the question of introducing normal conditions on the Chinese Eastern Railway be settled not only by words but by deeds," because the absence of normal conditions' weights heavily on both sides and hinders the development not only of the Chinese Eastern Railway but of the entire country which is closely connected with it.

"Not only the Soviet citizens but also Chinese, who are concerned with the development of the country; await the introduction of normal conditions on the Chinese Eastern Railway and the establishment of a normal legal status for Soviet citizens in Manchuria."

"Everyone, connected with the Chinese Eastern Railway, follows with interest the phases of the negotiations of the Sino-Soviet Conference at Mukden and is not only interested in questions concerning the Chinese Eastern Railway, but in questions of a general nature. It is fully expected by the Soviet delegation that the efforts now being made at the Conference will establish friendly relations, between the U.S.S.R and Mukden through the connecting link which is the Chinese Eastern Railway."

In another article an attempt will be made to outline the Chinese reply to this semi-official statement of the Soviet delegation to the Mukden Conference.

## CHINESE ENTERPRISE

The arrival in Singapore of Mr. R. J. Milne to act as technical advisor to Mr. Tan Kah-kee in the conduct of the World Biscuit Factory in Havelock Road directs attention to yet another of the many avenues along which the leader of the Chinese commercial life of Singapore is utilising his remarkable business energies and acumen. Mr. Tan Kah-kee is best known as the proprietor of the largest rubber conditioning factories at Kallang and of the busy rubber goods manu-factory at Sembawang Road—businesses which have spread their tentacles over a wide area, for there are no less than 62 branches of these two concerns in this country, the Dutch East-Indies, the treaty ports of China, Borneo, Siam and Rangoon. But actually Mr. Tan Kah-kee's industrial interests are much more comprehensive, and, working himself from early morning to late at night, he is the directing force behind a variety of other concerns, including paper making in Johore, biscuit making in Havelock Road felt hat manufacturing in Sembawang Road, and more recently, soap manufacturing, the annual aggregate turnover of which must be in the neighbourhood of forty million dollars.

Born in Amoy 53 years ago Mr. Tan Kah-ke's first business interests in Singapore were wrapped up in the retail of rice, from which he turned to pineapple packing, then pineapple planting, before joining the earliest of the rubber planters. A rice mill for the milling of parboiled rice for Indian consumption was soon added to his interests and before he left for China in 1919 he commenced the rubber treating factory, which has

now developed into the largest of its kind in the country. On his return from China in 1922 Mr. Tan Kah-kee commenced the manufacture of rubber goods on a large scale at his converted pineapple factory in Sembawang Road. The manufacture of topees and ordinary felt and wool hats are successful adjuncts to that business; during the last few years paper mills have been commenced in Johore, biscuit manufacturing carried on in Havelock Road, and attention is now being directed to the manufacture of soap.

It is just over a year since Mr. Tan Kah-kee took over the business formerly, and still being carried on under the style of the World Biscuit Factory, started about five years ago at a time when the Ho Ho Company owned the only large biscuit manufactory in the country. A quarter of a million dollars was allocated to the business, but since then the most up-to-date machinery has been sent out by Messrs. Baker, Perkins and Co., the well known biscuit machinery suppliers, and last week Mr. R. J. Milne, an expert in biscuit manufacturing, arrived in Singapore to assist as technical adviser in this concern. Heretofore this factory has catered only for the poorer classes, but it is now intended to manufacture a more superior type of biscuit for the ordinary market, and to very much increase the daily output of the factory beyond its present 12,000 pounds a day.

It has been computed, on current prices, that the annual turnover of the Tan Kah-kee rubber treating factories at Kallang is considerably over thirty million dollars, and as the factory deals with something like two thousand tons of rubber every month the statement is not far wide of the mark. Mr. Tan Kah-kee is not only the proprietor of this huge business but he is also one of the largest private rubber estate owners in the country, his rubber lands occupying some sixteen thousand acres. He is thus able to supply his own rubber for his conditioning godowns and also for the manufacture of rubber goods at Sembawang Road. Here ten thousand pairs of rubber shoes are turned out every day, in addition to a wide variety of other rubber articles, and it is stated that the turnover of this business is about five million dollars.
