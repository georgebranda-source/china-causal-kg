It is a well-known psychological fact that weakness and helplessness in others appeal to all that is best in one in a position to help, more particularly when their position is due to circumstances beyond their control, and that it is seldom that even bodily peril is shirked when occasion requires is shown by the splendid responses which are always made to appeals for volunteers when terrible disasters visit particular localities and there are human beings in dire distress and peril who may be saved by the efforts of others. The local application is, of course, to the good work which is being carried out in connection with the collapse of houses near the disinfection station in Chinese quarters in the Western part of the City. Most of the rescuers are firemen and police, and the cynic may possibly hold that this coming within the scope of their duties there is nothing further to be said about it. Those who have seen something of their work on the ruins during the past 80

hours or so, know that there is a lot more that can and ought to be said about it. After all, their duties do not oblige them to work like navvies clenring debris away, to lower themselves into openings which may or may not provide sufficient support for them, and generally to work at the highest tension for as long as it is humanly possible to do so. That is what our firemen and police have been doing, and although in proportion to the numbers buried the rescued may seem small, they represent all that human effort could accomplish, and those who have seen the terrible task with which they were faced must have marvelled that anyone at all should have been taken alive from such a colossal heap of debris. To those who have relatives there still unaccounted for little hope must now be held out, but such as it is, the consolation is theirs that all was done that could be done, and that the workers will continue their unsparing efforts so long as there is the slightest possibility of further success attending them.

When St. Paul wrote to the Christians in Philippi to hearten them under their double burden of poverty and persecution, it is significant that he should have exhorted them to dwell upon the pure, beautiful things of life as a sort of antidote against their troubles.

For all who have ever put up a real tight against the numbing, hardening power of earthly sorrow and adversity, know well the comfort and inspiration that can be drawn from these things. It was not for nothing that we were told to consider the lilies. The Christ whose inurnate life was full of sorrow, suffering, hardship, from beginning to end, was continually looking away at the loveliness of earth and sky, drawing from it all his finest imagery; and most of us, in our little way, come sooner or later to understand what these precious things of God's material creation can do for the human heart weighed down by grief or anguish having in it often the very texture of despair.

It explains something that is otherwise unexplainable—how newborn flower, or green spray, or wild bird's song at dawn, even when only reproduced in loving words upon the printed page, have such glamorous attraction for lives that seem spiritually the most hopeless. It is that what we call the beauties of nature is just God giving us a glimpse—doubtless as much as we can bear—of sheer ultimate Perfection in a world that, as a whole, is obviously, bewilderingly, imperfect in so many ways.

## THE TURF LIBEL CASE:

Fools bring libel actions in order that lawyers may live in comfort, comments a writer in the “Outlook.” “Nobody should bring an action for libel except the Angel Gabriel” is a saying attributed to Mr. Justice Darling. Somebody qualified it by adding “and then only if the Angel Gabriel retains Sir Edward Carson.” The wealth of sense lying beneath the surface of these jests is well understood by lawyers.

There is no legal action whose apparent simplicity is so insulting to the meanest intelligence as an action for libel. It seems so simple for the lay mind to understand; whereas, in reality, there is no more complex and complicated or deal for a jury than to give proper decisions in a libel case where one or more defences are set up.

It is for the judge to say whether the words complained of are capable of constituting a libel; it is for the jury to say whether they do so in fact. This is only the preliminary step. If a defence of "fair comment" is relied on, the jury must decide what is "fair," whether there is malice, for if so, no comment can be "fair." Then the question arises, what is "malice," which might well perplex the joint minds of Aristotle and Spinoza.

All that is required for the defendant, if he desires to launch this attack against the plaintiff, is to give seven days' notice before the trial to the plaintiff, with particulars of the matters relied upon for the attack. This is the weapon employed, to reduce the damages to that contemptible little coin known as a farthing.



No case has been tried in recent times where a more inconsistent or unreasonable verdict has been given than in the Turf libel action of last week. The defendants, by their defence, admitted that there was no truth in the statements made in the words complained of in the libel. Therefore, the only questions for the jury to decide were (1) Was it a libel? (2) If so, how much damages? As it seemed obvious to the jury and others that the words constituted a libel, the judge's summing-up might have copied the shortest summing-up on record (that of Mr. Justice Hawkins), "Gentlemen: how much?"

Moreover, there was no cross-examination of the plaintiffs to discredit them in mitigation of damages. Indeed, there could not be, for there was nothing discreditable upon which Messrs. Purefoy, Harper, and Beasley or Mrs. Bendir could have been successfully attacked. Yet, the jury found that the "words" were libellous, and assessed the damages at a farthing. A verdict for the defendants would have been more intelligible, and better for the plaintiffs, for such a verdict would merely have meant that the "words complained of" did not amount to defamatory matter. But the stigma of a farthing damages dealt a more serious blow to the plaintiffs than the original libel itself. To sum up... It was a libel; there was no evidence in mitigation of damages; therefore the damages could only have been substantial.

Mr. Lloyd George is not so foolish as to bring a libel action against Mrs. Asquith which, if he did, would be enjoyable for everybody except the plaintiff and defendant. I trust that he will not consider it libelous of me to say he is not so foolish as to emulate the Angel Gabriel.
