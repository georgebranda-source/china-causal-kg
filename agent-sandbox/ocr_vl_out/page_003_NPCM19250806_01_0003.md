## TIGER AND BISON HUNTING

STORIES OF MAN-EATERS: A CATTLE-LIFTER.

The following appeared as a letter in the daily "Statesman," Calcutta.—"Jungli Kooltra" writes.—Being very fond of shikar perhaps my own experiences will be of interest to other sportsmen. I am certainly not a "great shikari," but I am very keen; I love jungle life and do a big game shoot whenever I can arrange to go for one—not a very easy matter, however, nowa-diys.

Most of my shooting has been done in the Central Provinces, and as bison are preserved in most of the forests there, my experiences with bison are limited. But I have had some experiences with tigers, including a man-eater, which will perhaps be interesting.

In March last X and I went to the southern part of the Central Provinces, and it was suggested to us by the local authorities that we should go after a notorious man-enter which had been doing great damage for some months in a certain locality. We agreed with some reluctance, as we had intended going to another spot where we knew tigers were fairly numerous, but at the same time we were keen on trying our luck for a few days with a man-eater, which is not usually bagged in the same way as a civilised tiger. We got news of him immediately, as he followed our baggage carts for a few hundred yards within a mile and a half of our camp. The villagers emphatically told us that the man-eater would not touch a bait, but we tied out four young buffaloes, as there were said to be a couple of other tigers in that locality. The next morning we saw the shikari and others returning to camp minus one buffalo. We were filled with joy assuming that there had been a kill. But we knew from the faces of the shikari and the men as soon as they came near that something had gone wrong. Their story was that when they approached the third bait, which was tied about a sixty yards the other side of a deep nullish, they walked up to about fifteen yards of the buffalo, which was standing up, when they saw a tiger seated under a bush within ten feet of the bait. It was then about 8 a.m. The men (seven of them) formed a bunch, and they and the tiger stared at each other for a few seconds; the men then retired backwards till they reached the nullish, from which place they did sprints in record time!

## THE TIGER'S CUNNING

We went quickly to the spot, where we found the young buffalo still alive and without a scratch on him, and foot-prints of the tiger about four feet from the bait. We also saw the place where he had been sitting and the direction in which he had gone. The cunning brute would not touch the bait, but had sat there waiting for the men to come for it some time. Fortunately for the men the tiger was visible from the direction in which they came, otherwise they would have walked up to the buffalo without seeing him and there would certainly have been a tragedy.

Now ordinarily one would have expected a tiger, particularly a man-eater, to have attacked the men when they had gone so close to him. Two bounds would have reached them, but he did not move a muscle. On discussing the matter with the villagers they told us that the man-eater never attacked when he had been seen, and that he always got his victim unawares from behind. We did not get a chance at that brute, as he moved from that locality after a couple of days, evidently having got suspicious. This was believed to be the same man-eater which attacked Mr. George, the Divisional Forest Officer, in November last.

On a previous shoot my beaters were going through the jungle to the place from where the beat was to commence, when they suddenly came on to four tigers—male, female, and two nearly full-grown cubs, seated in the shade of a tree.

The tiger kept roaring and charging towards the men, who stood their ground, while the tigress led the cubs away. Had all four tigers charged the men with the intention of doing damage; they would have made quite a good "bug." This beat was ruined for that day, but the next day those four tigers were beaten out. The tigress led the cubs and the tiger formed the rearg-guard. It was a hot day and the tigers were apparently not inclined to travel. The tiger kept only about 25 or 30 yards in front of the beaters, and frequently tapped round and growled and snarled at them, but did nothing more. He did not charge the beaters at all. The sad part of this story is that I tried to bring off a "right" and left us one does with snipe, and hit the tigress and one of the cubs, both of which got away!

Every year in the merry month of May appears in the newspapers. a short paragraph calculated to make those whose social achievements have not equalled their social ambitions anything but merry. Its wording is always precisely the same, and it is as follows:—

The list for Ascot Royal Enclosure tickets closes on—, and no applications can be considered after that date. They should be addressed to Viscount Churchill, Ascot Office, St. James's Palace, S.W. 1.

After that, says a writer in the "Sunday Express," the fully-qualified and the unqualifiable are alike in the outer darkness, for whom there is no hope. ## STEAM PLANES

GREATER POWER, BUT LIGHTER ENGINES.

Dr. John S. Haldana, the president of the Institute of Mining Engineers, in a paper read at the annual meeting of the institute at Cardiff, said that some people seem to believe that the use of coal for steam engines was going to be more or less superseded. He believed that steam engines in the future, and the not very remote future, where steam was raised by coal or oil, would take the place of internal combustion engines in a great many cases.

In the case of the steam engine they had to jacket every part of it to retain the heat, but in the internal combustion engine they had to adopt other measures to get rid of the heat, otherwise the steel would not stand the temperatures to which it was subjected. This was a waste of heat energy.

The future development of the steam engine would be along the lines of increased steam pressure with smaller and lighter engines. The boilers also would be reduced in size as the pressure went up, so that the complete engine and boiler would be much smaller and lighter than an equivalent internal combustion engine, and would in all probability be more suitable and even superior for use in neocolumans and motor-cars.

The team engine would eventually beat the internal combustion engine.
