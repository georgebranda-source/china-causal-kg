### Tables "Turned."

## Officers Overpower Pirates on Bridge

Shortly before midnight, eight hours after the pirates had assumed control, the 2nd Officer suddenly pointed to distant lights and called to the pirates covering him with their revolvers. "There are the lights of Chilang Point," Turning to look, the pirates were momentarily off their guard and the 2nd Officer seized the opportunity of hitting both on the head with a deep-sea sounding lend. The two officers, in possession of the revolvers of the two pirates in question, had a commanding position from the bridge.

Of the subsequent happenings—the defence of the bridge throughout the night against onslaughts by the pirates in the course of which eleven were shot dead; the unsuccessful ruse of the pirates to regain possession of the bridge by pushing the Chief Engineering in front of them; the joining of the Captain and 2nd Officer on the bridge by the Mate and other officers who were pulled up through a skylight from the room in which they have been locked when the pirates first gained control—of this, and much else, little other than the barest details are obtainable from the officers who participated.

In spite of the reticence of the officers to detail the parts which each played in this epic of the sea, the story already published bears tribute to their gallant conduct throughout a night whence danger threatened from almost every quarter. It might be added that communication between the officers on the bridge and those mentioned yesterday as having been locked in and guarded by pirates in the Mate's room was restored through the lulling of the suspicions of the pirates by the friendly attitude of the officers.

After the ship had been in the possession of the pirates for some six or seven hours and, knipwing the officers to be unarmed, the pirates allowed them to go to the saloon and it was here, that on the action being taken by the officers on the bridge, the officers below joined them by breaking a sky-light and being pulled above whilst the guards below hastened to find out what the shooting was about. The officers were preceded by the Russian lady passenger who was hoisted first on to the bridge.

## No Armistice

## Pirates' Request Refused By Officers

To the danger from lurking foes was added that of fire, the pirates lighting large quantities of waste under the bridge in the hope that the officers would be smoked out. The set of the wind was against the object, however, and the pirates themselves were in it inent danger from the flames which spread to the quarter where they had taken up their stand.

The officers on the bridge, strengthened by those who had joined them below through the searchlight, were in a strong position and, recognising the helplessness of their case, the pirates endeavoured to treat with the officers through Mr. Lapsley, the European passenger who is still unaccounted for—having, it is believed, been taken away by the pirates.

The pirates indicated their willingness to cease hostilities if the officers would give up their arms and combine in ences to get the flames under. The overtures were ignored and the pirates then left the ship, as already detailed. This was about 4 a.m.

It was subsequent to the departure of the two boat loads of pirates that the two officers and wireless operator left the ship in the remaining boat with the Russian lady passenger, the fire being at its worst at the time and the action being taken as a result of a consultation between the officers who deemed it the most advisable course. The boat was being threatened by the flames and the opportunity to place the lady passenger in safety might not have arisen again. It was also intended to place on board the Chinese lady passengers, but the half burned painter parted and the boat drifted away.

## Grim Relics

Charred Bones of Pirates on “Sunning.”

On the arrival of the “Sunning” off Taikoo and the boarding by officials, “grim relics of the fight and conflagration were still to be observed among the charred beams and woodwork, twisted bars and other remnants of what had been the bridge and amidship accommodation of the officers and passengers.

The height to which the flames rose when the fire was at its worst could be gauged by the funnel, marks of scorching and blackening extending half way up its length. Forward and aft there remained little signs of the conflagration, the fire merely having blackened and scorched the derricks lying across the hatches.

Lying on the remains of a sky-light was a heap of charred bones, all that was left of a pirate who had fallen when shot from the bridge and been unable to move when the flames reached the spot where he had fallen.

Yet another gruesome sight in the bunk of what had been a cabin was that of the charred remains of a pirate who, apparently having been wounded, had "dragged" himself to what he considered a place of safety—only to find himself trapped and unable to move when the fire broke out at the instance of his fellow conspirators.

On the "arrival aboard of the Police and other officials, several of the arrested men, stated to have participated in the combined attack of the pirates, were sent off under Police supervision, all being managed. Others, one of whom is thought to be mortally wounded and two bearing marks of severe head wounds, were taken off for medical attention. The corpses of the pirates who were shot dead were also taken away by launch.

Each of the remaining passengers was closely examined by Police officials before being allowed to leave the ship, statements being taken from each and some being detained pending a satisfactory explanation.

There remained but four of the officers aboard, when the "Sunning" anchored off Taikoo yesterday, these being the skipper. Capt.

J. Pringle, the Chief Officer, T. Beatty, the Second Engineer, W. Orr, and the wounded Chief Engineer, G. Cormack.

Mr. Cormack was taken ashore and removed by ambulance. Although wounded in the chest and thigh he was cheerful and able to converse freely.

## RIGID SEARCH

Chinese opinion in Hong Kong is unanimous that rigid search is necessary at all ports of call on river and/or coastal boats bound for Hong Kong

At the same time, the local authorities are praised for their vigilance in having scared away

piratus gangs from Hong Kong so that they must ship at other places. Criticism of the alleged unwarranted rigour exercised by searchers on Hong Kong wharves has considerably moderated. Sympathy is expressed with the officers crew and passengers of the "Sunning" for their terrible ordeal, but they are thought lucky for not having had to abandon the ship in the confusion of the night when beset by flames as was the case with the "Tai On."

Chinese engaged in the shipping trade are still against suggestions to segregate native passengers while under way on account of possible dangers at sea and making too many innocent sufferer for the sins of the few.

The principal of a firm of Shanghai merchants in Hong Kong said: "It is impossible at present to expect our (the Chinese) Navy to perfect a system of patrols or convoys which foreign warships are put to considerable trouble in maintaining over even a small area.

"Every gang has been traced to Bias Bay except the outlaws on the West and East Rivers (of Kwangtung) who are also bandits and robbers and these are scattered over hills in the adjoining territory."

Novel Chinese Solution.

"Bias Bay will provide the solution. A precedent exists, as extradition treaties are provided to help friendly nations in arresting prisoners. Therefore the only cure is for the Nationalist Government to provide the troops and to ask for the co-operation of foreign Navies in a campaign at Rias Bay.

The object, however, should be solely the hunting down of the pirate gangs, and not the bombarding of the vicinity at random.

"After that the local military commanders could hold the elders and headmen of each community responsible for their respective clans not having any pirates, at home or abroad. Severe penalties for clansmen would have sufficient deterrent effect on the erring brethren, or the others would see to it that the offenders were delivered to justice."

"This practice was followed with conspicuous success in the latter years of the Manchu dynasty, when Kwangtung was free from robber bands."

A former compradore on a Jardine boat in North China said he very much doubted if the killing and arrest of part of one gang would serve as a lesson to others

His experience of pirates was that they did things on a very businesslike scale and took the expense (including purchase of passages, possible loss of their weapons) as being more important than the risk entailed.

## CANTON ADVANCE

London, Nov.

In the House of Commons, to-day, in answer to questions, the Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs, Sir Austen Chamberlain, said that the advance of the southern forces in Central China did not appear to have adversely affected the personal security of British residents or to have involved any serious danger to British property, though inevitably there had been some disturbance to British establishments in the army zone.

The anti-British agitation in Szechuan had led to the destruction of some British property at Chungking, and it had been considered advisable for the women and children to leave that port.

Conditions in mid-China were still uncertain, and it would be premature to pass judgment.

Questioned as regards the "Sunning" incident, Sir Austen Chamberlain asked for notice of the question. He also requested notice of the question whether he could assure the House that there was a sufficient naval force at Hankow to ensure the safety of British residents there in case of need.—Reuter.

In an article in the "Financial News" on the Chinese crisis, Mr. Hilton Young says, the establishment of the rule of Southern Nationalists throughout China, which recent events have made by no means improbable, would result in a vast area inhabited by nearly one fifth of the world's population erecting between itself and the rest of the world the same impenetrable barrier of suspicion, distrust and dislike at present isolating Russia, and thus the basis of credit would be destroyed and international trade would languish.
