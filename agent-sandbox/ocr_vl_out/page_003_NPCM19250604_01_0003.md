DEATH OF THE EX-SHAH OF PERSIA.

A. Reuter telegrun from San Remo states that Mohammed Ali, formerly Shah of Persia and father of the present Shah, died recently of a heart attack at the Villa San Michele, where he resided. The remains are to be taken to Teheran.

Mohammed. Ali," sixth of the dynasty of the Kajars, which secured possession of the Crown of Persia after a civil war extending over 15 years, from 1770 to 1704, came to the throne on January 8, 1907.

Up to the year 1906 the form of government in Persia was similar to that in Turkey before the proclamation of the Constitution. The Shah, within the limitations imposed by the Mohammedan religion, was "an absolute ruler. In 1905 the Nationalist movement arose, and in January, 1906, Muzaffard-Din, the father of Mohammed Ali, gave his consent to the establishment of a Constitution. On coming to the throne Mohammed Ali signed the Constitution and took the prescribed oath: On June 23, 1908, however, Parliament was dissolved and the Parliament building partly demolished and sacked by his, Cossacks.

In the same year an attempt on his life was made by bomb. More fortunate than his grandfather, Nasr-ed-Din, he escaped with his life; but many were injured by the outrage. It did not come as a surprise; for even among moderate reformers suspicion had been felt that

he might not prove entirely loyal to the Constitution which was granted by his father a little more than a week before his own accession. This hostility and this suspicion did not disappear with time. The difficulties of effecting a radical change in the system of government, the intrigues of courtiers who had everything to lose by reform, led to misunderstandings which kept the Shah and his people apart and were a constant source of friction. The fact that the members of the National Assembly were not invited either to the coronation or to the Selameh-Ann, the general Durbar by which it was followed, an omission due probably to the intrigues of the Palace clique, gave great offence; and the delay in the acceptance of the revised form of the Constitution even produced something like a rebellion in Tabriz, the stronghold of the revolutionary societies. The natural

desire of the new Shah to have a strong, man on whom he could depend as Prime Minister led him to recall the Atabeg Azum and to place the direction of affairs in his hands. Unfortunately Atabeg, whose autocratic tendencies were well known, was detested by the people, who immediately jumped to the conclusion that he was brought back to Persia to re-establish the old despotism.

In July, 1909, Mohammed Ali was forced to abdicate. It was arranged that he should live in Russia, and a pension was provided, which was to be paid as long as he remained there. Shortly afterwards it was announced that the ex-Shah had left Odessa, presumably with the connivance of Russia. Twice since then he has returned to Persia. In 1911 he made, with the help of his brother Sarad-ed-Dowleh, vain attempt to regain the throne. His adherents raised trouble in various districts, and threatened to advance on the capital. One of them, Arshad-ud-Dowleh, got within 40 miles of Teheran, with a force consisting mainly of Turcomans. He was defeated by a smaller body of Nationalist troops, and was executed.

A second attempt in 1919 to regain the throne failed, and the ex-Shah has since then lived in retirement. His son and successor has been absent from Persia for the last two years, and government is virtually a dictatorship by the present Prince Minister. Riza Khan.

Early in 1824 a Republican movement made such headway that the deposition of the Shah and the introduction of Republican government were to be moved in the Mejlis on March 22. At the last minute unexpected hostility to the proposed change of regime was vigorously manifested, and on April 1 the Premier announced the demise of the movement in a proclamation declaring that a republic was contrary to the Islamic religion and prohibiting all mention of the subject under threat of penalties.
