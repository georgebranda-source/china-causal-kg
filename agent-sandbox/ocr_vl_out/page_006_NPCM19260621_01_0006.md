## Hongkong, Monday, June 21, 1926

AN HISTORICAL SOCIETY

There may be a comparative handful of people in the Colony to-day who will participate in the celebrations of the centenary of Hongkong fifteen years hence. Three years hence Western Australia will complete the first century of her existence as a unit of the British Empire. In this connection, according to the West Australian, the most powerful newspaper in that State, the Premier has initiated a project to collect all available old prints and photographs of historical interest, to be published as a pictorial record of a century's endeavour. This step moves our contemporary to remark: "There is not in our midst a public body whose primary interest lies in the recording of the intensely interesting local history of the State. We lack an Historical Society."

Although Hongkong's centenary is much further off, the same plea could be made even now for the establishment of an Historical Society. How often has it been deplored that with the passing of the old "China hands" there are also irretrievably passing fragments, pages, chapters of our local history and social development? Outside of scanty and sporadic newspaper contributions the bulk of this information goes with the actors in our daily drama. Whose work is it to collect and preserve these records while there is the opportunity?—no one's as yet, but with an Historical Society in active existence a treasure house would exist, full of informative memoirs of those who in their varied ways have built up the Colony to what it is to-day. Father Time works unceasingly and too easily does his erasing hand move the events of the past into oblivion. The present year has witnessed the retirement for good of a large number of old residents, whilst others have passed away—few of them leaving behind (If Sir Paul Chatter be excepted) much of a tangible nature, on which to lay the foundation of an Historical Society.



An Historical Society, with enthusiastic working members throughout the Colony, would effectively circumvent Time, the obliterator, by causing to be written up the episodes and encouraging the collection of the stories of those who recollect something of what has been—those “good old, days” in Hongkong of which we still hear something. With research articles, reminiscences, Government records, old prints, and photographs, how much of the past could be preserved!

The idea is not new. The language of this plea is not original. But that cannot discount the immense value of an Historical Society to the Colony. Let it not be said of Hongkong, as it is now being said of Western Australia, that three years before our centenary there is to be deplored the lack of an Historical Society. Such

a body cannot fail to be of the greatest interest during the stay here of every present British resident, whilst it would be performing an incalculably useful service to the Colony in the years after the great majority of us have departed for good to other climes. We may not have a centenary nearly due to act as an incentive to form an Historical Society, but we do have the lesson of Western Australia before us, and we surely can profit therefrom!

## CATHOLIC CONGRESS

GREAT WELCOME TO ELEVEN CARDINALS.

PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE.

Chicago, June 19th

A welcoming assemblage of 14,000 packed the Coliseum, the City's largest auditorium, when Cardinal Bonzano and other distinguished ecclesiastics to the Eucharistic Congress came on to the platform.

The outstanding feature was a message from President Coolidge, delivered by the Secretary of Labour, Mr. Davis, who had specially come from Washington.

The President declared the United States had long been under the imputation of putting too much emphasis upon material things. "Perhaps we have been subject to that kind of criticism not so much because we really are more interested in material prosperity than others, but because in that direction we have been more successful than others."

The President argued that material prosperity could not be secured unless it rested upon spiritual realities, all business being founded on mutual good faith, as indeed all modern Governments were.

Mr. Davis pleaded on his own account for religious tolerance, and was tumultuously applauded when he said: "If there is any anti-Catholic prejudice in America it comes from persons who make a speciality of prejudice." — Reuter's American Service.

Amid “unsurpassed scenes of stately splendour the International Eucharistic Congress was formally opened by the celebration of Pontifical High Mass in the presence of eleven Cardinals and upward of six thousand people.

Masses were celebrated in the Churches in the Archdiocese of Chicago.

Pilgrims from all nations joined in the spiritual bouquet of a million Communions, promised to the Pope by Cardinal Mundelein, Archbishop of Chicago.—Reuter's American Service.

## Delegates Suspect

Out of 77 jugo-slav delegates to the Eucharistic Congress 64 have been detained and ordered to proceed to Ellis Island for examination. It is officially stated that the authorities are not satisfied that the delegates, who are all laymen, come to the United States solely to attend the Congress.

### LIQUOR LAWS. THE CANADIAN CUSTOMS SCANDAL

Ottawa. June 19.

Sweeping recommendations in favour of the dismissal or suspension of certain officials in Montreal and other Quebec ports of entry, the retirement of a number of high officials in Montreal, and the stiffening of the preventive service and prohibition of clearances on liquor vessels from Canadian ports for the United States and "Rum Row" are contained in the report of the House of Commons Committee.

The Committee also recommend prosecutions for the recovery of moneys from a long list of companies found to have evaded the Customs and excise laws. Reference is made to the "slowly degenerating efficiency" of the Customs Department in recent years, and the ex-Minister of the Bureau is criticised.

The Committee find there is some presumption that a proportion of liquor, allegedly consigned from Canada for foreign ports and cleared without payment of excise on sales and tax, returns to Canada for consumption.

The tightening up of regulations by the imposition of taxes on all liquor released from bond or entering in bond is recommended and also a reduction of the ports of entry, which are 681, as compared with 270 in the United States and 80 in Great Britain.—Reuter's American Service.

## COAL CRISIS

GOVERNMENT'S EIGHT-HOUR BILL

TO BE RUSHED THROUGH.

## Rugby, Juno 20

The new Bill enabling a return to the eight-hours working day in the coal industry will be formally introduced in the House of Commons to-morrow, and the Government intends to pass it through all its Parliamentary stages as rapidly as possible.

The measure does not repeal the Seven Hours Act, but merely suspends it for a period which has not yet been defined, but which will be announced to-morrow. It will probably be from three to five years.

The Government also intends to pass into law before the Recess a second Bill incorporating measures of reorganisation in the industry.—British Wireless Service.

## SOVIET'S MISTAKE

## WARNINGS FROM BRITISH STATESMEN

Strong references to Russia were uttered by Ministers in speeches to-day.

Mr. Winston Churchill, in London, spoke of the Soviet's mistake in thinking the "same sort of stuff with which they bamboozled the moujiks would suit Britain." He was of the opinion that the United States policy to Russia was right as was proved by the fact that without in the slightest way countenancing Bolshevik principles, they had done more trade with Russia than we.

Mr. Churchill warned the people against lending money to Russia, and that the British Government would not accept responsibility if they were defrauded. He advised traders to get payment or full security before despatching goods.

Lord Birkenhead, at Luton, declared that nobody was deceived by the protestations of Moscow that the Russian Government was not behind the money forwarded for the alleged purpose of alleviating distress in the coalfields, and concluded that unless we observe signs of change it will be necessary to decide whether we should retain any degree of association with a country whose avowed purpose, known to the whole world, is to undermine the historical greatness of Britain.—Reuter's Service.
