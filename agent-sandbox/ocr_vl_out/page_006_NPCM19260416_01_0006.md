## Hongkong, Friday, April 16, 1926

THOSE STATISTICS.

Recently the "Straits Times" devoted a column and a half to an editorial eulogising the value of trade statistics to the Straits Settlements. More recently the Associated Chambers of Commerce of Australasia discussed the Commonwealth statistics and made a plea for certain reforms with a view to securing their greater utility to the commerce of that country. Here we are still content to go on as we have been since the Statistical Department ceased to function, and there seems nobody strong enough in the Government Service to insist that it be re-opened. The Colony may not exactly yearn for a Mussolini or a Pangalas: but there are some respects in which the people of Italy and Greece are almost to be envied. Both these gentlemen have a mind of their own and they have the pluck (or "nerve," as some describe it) to see that they get their own way. Dictators need not necessarily always be tyrannical and ruthless in their methods. A little benevolent autocracy may sometimes be welcomed if only as a change from a monotonous regime that lacks verve, and is seemingly afraid to reverse a decision proved to be inimical to the interests of the community.

The General Chamber of Commerce has expressed its opinion in favour of the re-opening of the Statistics Office, whilst the Chinese Chamber is apparently half-hearted. Meanwhile, until the Government considers both views the arrears of statistics are getting further behind, and valuable ground is being rapidly lost. Before the strike commenced the value of the Statistics Office was not taken into account from the mere point of view of expenditure. It was just swept away in the first flush of enthusiasm over the retrenchment campaign. If its reopening now is not justified, its existence up to the middle of last year could not possibly be defended. But it is conceded on all sides that it did do valuable work, and not even a Government official would admit that the expenditure on it was money wasted. In these days of keen international competition a country without statistics is surely inviting that state of lethargy that lends to decay in all that affects its trade.

Present conditions in respect of Canton cannot continue. No one is more aware of that than the authorities in this Colony. Why not, then, anticipate the future just a little and be prepared for that resuscitation of trade that must follow in the wake of a truce between Hongkong and Canton? To express it in other words, why permit the Canton embargo on British trade to interfere with the consideration of the re-opening of the Statistics Office?

Since the annual meeting of the General Chamber of Commerce the subject has lain dormant, doubtless awaiting a meeting of the Legislative Council. But, in view of the interest that undoubtedly exists in the matter it may be suggested that the Government should make some pronouncement on the subject even before the Council sits again.

### MR. SUN FO

It is remarkable the number of people who persist deliberately in not seeing the wood for the trees. Here is an individual—if not fashioned as a god—entitled to claim that God made man in his own image. Yet this person will see what is known as'red,' if the word 'indicating' Hongkong's ancient road vehicle is not spelt as he thinks it should be. Jinrikisha it probably is. "Ricsha, ricksha, rickshaw, it may be, but there is only one way—and that is his. Talk to this type of person about the social conditions attached to the calling of a puller of humanity, and he is found uninterested......Having written in full sympathy with a class who perform a most useful function, it is not likely, at this late hour of the day, that we shall take back what we have said. Our strictures, if any, are towards those who control the ricksha traffic. There are, for instance, rules governing in what manner a ricksha may be used. It is ordinarily licensed to convey human beings. There is a rule we believe that prohibits the conveyance of "sides" of meat and articles of food in large quantities. And yet how often does one see a vehicle so used! We think another rule should be added, and that is to prohibit a ricksha being used to convey a person suffering from an infectious illness. How often this happens, we know not. But the spectacle is fairly common of poor sufferers, looking lost to this world, being taken to some place, presumably for treatment. We have full sympathy with the lot of those unable to secure and pay for an ambulance; but it should not be difficult for a simple method to be devised that would cater for those who need to be taken to hospital. The matter does not appear to have been thought of. Here then is a further opportunity for the sympathetic to demonstrate their sympathy, or for the Police department to devise a plan which will prevent possible contamination and consequent suffering by those who may use an "unclean" ricksha.

The "coal situation" underwent a further development to-day. Hope for Resumption.

In the afternoon the Miners' Executive met the Industrial Committee of the Trades, Union Congress and reported the result of their interview with the Prime Minister.

### BOYS GOOD TURN. PRODUCES 790,741 SCOUTS IN AMERICA

How a London Boy's good turn was the direct cause of producing 790,741 Boy Scouts in the U.S. is told in a recent issue of the "Scouts' Weekly News." About eighteen years ago Mr. W.D. Boyce, the American publisher was in London. Caught in a fog in the neighbourhood of Fleet Street, Mr. Boyce was conducted to his hotel by a newsboy who happened to be a member of a Scout Troop. When Mr. Boyce offered the boy a tip he was greatly astonished and impressed when the boy saluted and told him that as a Scout he had to do at least one good turn a day and he was unable to accept a present.

Mr. Boyce was so impressed that he had a conference with Sir Robert Baden-Powell and as a result, upon his return to America, he proposed a Boy Scout organisation based upon the lines of the British Boy Scout movement. He finally succeeded in enlisting the help of friends in Washington and in due course an organisation known as the Boy Scouts of America was formed and on February 8, 1910, Federal Incorporation was granted by special Act of Congress.

As a direct result of the London boy's good deed, says the "China Press," there are now three-quarters of a million American Scouts and the movement is still growing.
