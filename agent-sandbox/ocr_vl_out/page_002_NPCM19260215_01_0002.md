## FEARS ALLAYED

Sound Position of Hongkong

## Bank

## 1926 FIGURES

## ：

Those who have been anxious regarding the position of the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, which has been an object of special attack during the last six months of trouble in Hongkong and Shanghai, will read the preliminary figures for 1925, just issued, with much gratification, states the "China Express & Telegraph".

Especially will they welcome the declaration that the final dividend, namely, £3 per share, with a bonus of £2 per share remains unaltered. This makes the usual distribution of £8 per share for the whole year, and when one considers the extremely chaotic state into which China has fallen and the boycott directed against British interests generally, the result can only be described as a highly satisfactory one for all concerned.

Of course, the Bank did not escape the attack unscathed. While it was able to write off $1,000,000 from Bank Premises Account, as in the previous year, and to carry forward about $3,400,000, virtually the same amount as it brought in it is noted that the new addition to Silver Reserve is to be only $500,000 as against $1,000,000 a year ago. This implies a reduction of about half a million dollars in net earnings, a loss which, in view of the conditions mentioned, comes as an agreeable surprise to many shareholders who feared that the Bank stood to suffer much more heavily.

The announcement of the usual dividend has allayed all fears, and public confidence in the Bank's ability to weather the storm is already reflected in a sharp rise in the shares. Although British interests in China, and particularly in Hongkong, continue to suffer, there are ample grounds for hope. There is a growing feeling in Canton that the boycott should come to an end; the militarists who have so long disturbed the country are inclined to give up the ruinous struggle, and the representatives of the Powers sitting in Peking are most anxious to adjust the country's grievances.

Some time must elapse ere a settlement is reached, but men- time the people seem to be wearying of civil warfare, and of the outside influences that have upset their foreign relationships, and there is the Customs revenue, the one shining star in a beclouded firmament, which reveals that, in spite of all the trouble, China's trade during the past year has reached a record figure. What it might have amounted to had peaceful conditions prevailed there is no saying, but that the China trade field offers wonderful scope for all in the future cannot be disputed.

In the present circumstances, however, it is fortunate for the Bank that China is by no means the only field in which it operates. It is soundly established throughout the Far East, and during the year has, no doubt, been able to recoup some of its China losses from the prosperity prevailing in the Federated Malay States and the Dutch East Indies, while maintaining its record of steady progress in Japan, Siam, Indo-China, the Philippines and elsewhere.

## CHINA'S LOANS

## ) Mã

In view of the chaotic conditions that prevail in China at the present time, surprise was expressed during mail week in some quarters at Home that that country is able to pay the half-yearly interest coupons on certain of its loans.

The explanation lies in the fact that the loans on which the service is being met are specially secured on certain revenues.

There are, for instance the Maritime Customs which are under the control of an international committee. The revenues from these never pass into the hands of the Chinese Government, but are paid into the contracting banks in monthly instalments. In this way the funds necessary to meet the service of certain of the loans are provided and coupons are paid on their due date.

Among the loans secured upon the revenue of the Maritime Customs of China are the 4 percent Gold Loan of 1895, the 5 percent Gold Loan of 1896, the 4 percent Gold Loan of 1898, and the 5 percent Gold Loan of 1913.

There are, of course, a number of Chinese loans in default—viz., the Hukuang Railway 5 per cent. Gold Loan 1911, the Tientsin Pukow 5 per cent. Supplementary Loan of 1910, the Canton-Kowloon 5 per cent. Loan, and the "Vickers" and "Marconil" 8 per cent. Loans. A year ago the December coupon of the Hukuang Railway Loan was paid after a delay of nearly two months. The total amount of these loans in default is about $11,500,000.

## KNITTED GOODS

## The Proposed British Duty

All the reports treat the matter as an increase in duty, but this cannot be the case, as there is no duty on knitted goods in Britain at present. It is interesting to note how Japan protests against duties imposed on her manufactures in other countries, but claps on duties against other countries' goods quite unconcevedly.

A strong agitation is in progress here against the proposed duty on knitted goods in Britain, says the Japan Chronicle of January 28. On the January 27, the leading manufacturers and exporters in Kobe and Osaka held a meeting at the Oriental Hotel, when plans were formulated to oppose the passage of the Bill through the British Parliament. It was decided that, in compliance with a request from the London Chamber of Commerce, reports be compiled demonstrating that there is no likelihood of dumping in London and other British markets by the Japanese knitting industry. These reports are to be distributed to the London Chamber of Commerce, firms and Commercial Attaches. Propaganda will be carried on largely through other channels. Mr. Sotoumi Tetsujiro, President of the Federation of Knitted Goods Exporters' Guilds, went up on the 26th to Tokyo to enlist the co-operation in the matter of the Foreign Office, Commercial Department and all political parties. The Kobe Chamber of Commerce is also getting to work energetically.

## A KNOTTY PROBLEM

## Jaya And Its Labour

The total land area of the Netherlands East Indies is approximately 2,000,000 square kilometres. Of this wide area, Java, an island of 131,500 square kilometres, is the most advanced in development. In both agricultural and mineral production a high stage of progress has been reached and there is little suitable land available for further exploitation.

This fact is responsible for one of the knolltiest problems confronting the administration of Netherlands India. While Java is an exceedingly fertile and well-developed island, it is greatly over-populated. There are, in this little island, which is about the same size as England, over 36,000,000 persons, while in the remaining 1,870,000 square kilometres belonging to Holland in these waters there are only about 14,000,000 persons. While Java thus over-populated, the remaining regions—Sumatra, Dutch Borneo, Calebies, and the smaller islands—are suffering from a lack of man-power to develop their resources.

The problem seems easily spluble by the emigration of the superfluous population of Java to the parts where labour is needed. This has actually been tried, but the Javanese as a race are not amenable to emigration, and the experiment has not met with the success that was anticipated. An elaborate code of regulations dealing with emigration was drawn up, but these, though eminently favourable to the emigrant, have not satisfied the requirements of the Outer Possessions. The experiment has been tried chiefly with the rapidly advancing East Coast of Sumatra District, where labour is urgently required for the rubber, tobacco, oil palm, and other estates and, in a lesser degree, with the Dutch East Borneo. But, generally speaking, the experiment has not proved successful, owing chiefly to the Javanese tempera

Yet it is in the Outer Possessions that future advancement must chiefly take place, and some way out of the present difficulty must be found. The main industrial centres of Sumatra are in Acheen, in the north of the island, the East Coast District to which reference has already been made, the Padang Highlands in the west, Djambi in the south-east, and in the south where a number of prosperous tea plantations are situated. In the last-named districts there is still plenty of suitable land available for tea. Petroleum is the principal industry of Djambi, though there are also extensive native rubber holdings.—"Times."

## Police Patrol

An armed motor cycle patrol under the control of Chief Inspector Kent was one of the features made to cope with crime during the week-end and their sudden appearance in out-of-the-way places and at unexpected times undoubtedly had a good moral effect on evil-doers.

The cyclists were armed with revolvers, while an officer in each side car carried a rifle ready for action. Altogether, five machines were on the road on the Hongkong side and four in Kowloon on Friday. This new patrol system was continued on Saturday.

Special attention was given to the stopping and searching of suspicious characters during the tour.

No untoward incidents so far as the Hongkong Fire Brigade is concerned occurred on Friday to mar the Chinese festivities. Equipments at all stations were kept in readiness to proceed at a moment's notice swing to the greater risk involved by the cracker firing danger, but there was no call.

The equipment of the Central Fire Station was set in motion at about 9 o'clock Saturday morning when a call was received from No. 44, Des Vaux Road, less than a hundred yards away. Some bedding on the second floor of the building was set alight by an oil lamp. This was quickly extinguished by the firemen with hand buckets.

## Accidents

There was fortunately an absence of serious accidents on Friday in spite of the crowded condition of the streets owing to Chinese New Year.

A Chinese boy was taken to hospital with injuries to his leg. He was accidentally struck by a dust cart in Elgin Street. A Sanitary Board coolie had also to be sent to hospital, being knocked down by the same cart in the same place. In a tram car accident a Chinese woman received injuries to her feet and was sent to hospital.

A Chinese boy received bruises in a motor car accident in the central district. Another boy was knocked down in Kowloon by a motor cycle and injured in the head. In both cases the lads were not detained in hospital.

What with the fire swept appearance of the Hongkong Hotel and the detonations from innumerable crackers, the centre of the City presented a warlike appearance yesterday.

None the less, the Police Headquarters informed the "China Mail" upon enquiry "late, last night that nothing untoward had occurred to mar the celebrations.
