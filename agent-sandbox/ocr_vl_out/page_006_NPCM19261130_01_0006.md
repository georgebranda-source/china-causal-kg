### Hong Kong, Tuesday, Nov. 30, 1926

## TROUBLE AT HANKOW

Feelings akin to despair will be caused by the news, from "Hankow. That trouble should occur there again is bad enough: that it should occur at this juncture is particularly unfortunate. The Nationalist Government, by its efforts to suppress the boycott at Canton, has gained much credit in the eyes of the world. This credit, it is hoped, will be greatly enhanced, during the next few weeks, by "the cleaning up" of Bias Bay, with the aid of the British Navy. After that the Nationalist Government, but for the threatened trouble at Hankow, could have stood before the world with a claim for new consideration, based not merely on words, but on a record of actual achievement. The Nationalist Government, we are prepared to believe, is not in full sympathy with the threatened trouble at Hankow—from the sincere efforts it has made to suppress the boycott at Canton, we are ready to believe, indeed, that it would end the trouble at once, if it could do that with ease, having learned to its cost how such movements useful as they may be at the outset, can get out of hand and become an intolerable curse. The Nationalist Government, we are sure, would settle the trouble at once if it could do so with ease. But it feels it cannot do so with ease. The student and Labour elements at Hankow are too strong in its opinion to be put in their places out of hand. The Nationalist Government evidently thinks the unruly must be given their heads, and only when they have become tired through the recklessness of the pace, must they be curbed. This policy doubtless has points in its favour for the Nationalist Government, but it can hardly be expected to appeal to the Powers whose nationals' lives and property are endangered as a result. The Nationalist Government, therefore, can hardly blame the Powers for taking any steps they deem necessary to protect their subjects. Nor can the Nationalist Government blame the world for setting the trouble in the North against the efforts to secure better order in the South, and qualifying its good opinion according

ly. The world naturally looks to a government to enforce law and order in the territory it controls. The Nationalist Government is seeking recognition, we are told, as the representative Government of all China. The world does not expect the Nationalist Government to suppress trouble in Peking or Mukden—it will be satisfied if the Nationalist Government can enforce law and order in Hankow and the other parts of China where the Kuomintang flag flies to-day.

## The Navy and the Yangtze

The London Times makes the Cantonese invasion of the North a lever for denouncing hesitation in taking adequate steps to protect British interests on the Yangtsze and for having a slap at the Bolsheviks. It states that to trust vaguely to some ultimate Chinese reaction against Bolsheviks is wholly unprofitable, for by the time the reaction comes there may be little left for us to save and the state of China herself will be appalling. On the Yangtsze, our contemporary points out, opportunities for provoking further conflict are innumerable, and the worst enemies of Britain are watching to take advantage of them. It is obvious who these "worst enemies of ours" are. Equally obvious is it that they will continue their insidious propaganda against us in China in defiance of the Russo-British Trade Agreement expressly forbidding anti-British propaganda outside Russian borders. How that propaganda is working may be gleaned from the cables informing us of the serious position in Hankow, where the British community are "in daily expectation of an incident which would involve bloodshed." They now declare that our naval forces are not adequate for their protection, and they ask the Home Government for an assurance that the naval forces will be strengthened. This appeal is under the consideration of the Government, including the Admiralty. Only a few days ago it was stated in Parliament that our naval forces in China were considered adequate; but the new situation in Hankow has altered the aspect. If the Chinese authorities themselves cannot, or will not protect British subjects in Hankow or elsewhere on the Yangtsze the British armed forces have no alternative but to step into the breach.

## The Communist Fallacy

In trade, in politics, in agriculture, in the Army and Navy, in manufactures, in education and in law, the Communists propose to supersede the fit by the unfit. All the trained, experienced and capable directors, organisers, managers, salesmen, architects and engineers, and all the educated professional men are to be murdered or exiled that their places may be filled with the rag-tag and bob-tail of the slums—men ignorant, unskilled, unjust and violent. Such is the plan, such the hope of the apostles of revolution. They desire to establish what they term the dictatorship of the proletariat. Now, apart from the criminal lunacy of the suggested means, let us consider the political idiocy of the end. The dictatorship of the proletariat, as exemplified in the case of Soviet Russia, means the rule of the peasants and soldiers. But that rule would surely be more than a form of political government. It would imply also the confiscation of all public and private property and the control of all industrial and commercial concerns by the proletariat. Once they have abolished law and order, and discipline and business

and common sense once they have successfully blown out the brains of the commonwealth, they will be able to wave the red flag and live happily ever after. It is the most impossible, hideous and absurd delusion that ever betrayed fools to their own ruin. Anyone who has any real experience of popular movements, political or industrial, knows that the rank and file never do anything but listen and cheer and vote. All the real work is done by a few energetic workers. The great mass of the people never take an active part in public affairs. They resent the labour and effort and trouble. They must have leaders. They always did have leaders. Without leaders they are a helpless mob. Do the Communists expect to be able to dispense with leaders? Do they expect the whole mass of the proletariat to develop a common will, a common purpose and a common discipline? We have no hesitation in saying that they do not expect anything of the kind. They intend the proletariat to have not only leaders, but masters, and they intend to secure those posts for themselves. To put the whole thing in a nutshell, the advocates of the dictatorship of the proletariat do not intend to allow the proletariat to dictate. No, they are to be dictated to.

## BANK NOTE CASE

### MR. TOWNS GIVES FURTHER EVIDENCE

JUDGMENT RESERVED.

After hearing further evidence in the Summary Court yesterday afternoon, Mr. Justice Wood reserved judgment in the case in which the Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation is sued for $500 by a Chinese lady who claims payment for a bank note of that value which she alleges was accidentally washed and ironed in a jacket sent to the wash.

Mr. G. T. Towns, acting Chief Accountant of the Bank, recalled, said that he had searched the Bank's books and had been unable to find a case where indemnity had been paid for a lost note of which the number was not known. There had been indemnities paid but only in cases where the note had been partially destroyed and on which the number was intact.

Replying to His Lordship, witness said that so long as the number of a note was identifiable and so long as the promise to pay appeared, he would advise the Bank to pay, although the signatures were absent.

In reply to the submission of Mr. Eldon Potter, K.C, (for the Bank) that the question of whether the Bank was prejudiced entered into the matter, His Honour said it was a question of whether they could prove to the Government that they had paid out on a good note. If they could prove that they could surely get their $500 back.

Mr. Fitzroy said it was obvious that the Bank could get the money from the Government. If the business was run as it was done in England the trouble would not have arisen. They were negligent in not keeping the numbers of notes of such considerable sums.

His "Lordship reserved judgment."
