## DEATH

### Hong Kong, Monday, Nov. 15, 1926

## WAYS THAT ARE DARKLY OFFICIAL

The Government appears to be under the impression that information need only be given to the local newspapers as a concession. That view is entirely wrong. The newspapers represent the public, and in that capacity can demand as a right any information the public is entitled to know. When a reporter walks into a Government office, the taxpayers of the Colony walk in with him. The Government appears to have forgotten this, for new regulations have been issued imposing serious handicaps on the gathering of news from Government departments.

Whether these regulations are the outcome of a recent incident when a confidential document got into print or whether they are merely a piece of Bumbledom, is not certain — the point hardly matters, anyhow, because the regulations are as little defensible on the first ground as they are on the second. To penalise four papers for the mistake of one, to say the least, is extremely unfair; and to apply regulations inspired by one specific incident to every department in a general arbitrary manner is to show a spirit that would be childish were it not so exasperating.

The public can judge for itself just how futile and annoying the new regulations are. Instructions have been circulated to the Heads of Government departments that all information for the Press in future is to be placed on a table in each office, and the Press is to receive only such information as it finds on that table. Possibly those may not be the exact terms of the order, but that is the way the “China Mail” has found the order being interpreted.

One result of this is that the five papers are placed on a strictly even footing when it comes to getting news from a Government department — enterprise, initiative, friendly rivalry all come to nought in the face of official unimaginativeness. That is bad enough, but it affects the newspapers only. More serious is the fact that the public is being thwarted in large measure of its right to know exactly how its money is being spent, owing to the Gilbertian situations which are the outcome of the regulations.

One of these situations arose when a “China Mail” representative, having heard casually—as one does in Hong Kong—that a certain new work had been put in hand, called to see the head of one of the sub-departments of the P.W.D. for details. This gentleman, while admitting that he had the details at his finger ends, could only refer the reporter to the Press table. Need it be said that the Press table was as bleak as it had been ever since it was set apart for the reporters?

The next chapter in the comedy was for the reporter to question an official in close touch with the head of the P.W.D. himself. This gentleman explained that the head of the sub-department was quite right in reading the new regulations as he had done, but as a “concession” the reporter would now be given the information he sought—but not by the man who had the details at his finger ends. Oh dear, no! The information must come from yet another official. This gentleman admitted he was quite out of touch with the whole subject, and indeed he was, for he had to search through a bulky file for even the barest details!

What if a mistake was made recently by a newspaper? What if the mistake was "serious"? Is that any reason why the Government should now treat the newspapers in this childish manner, why it should grudge the public the information taxpayers the world over have the right to receive, and why it should treat the responsible officials of its own departments as mere ciphers, lacking the discretion of children or the dignity of office boys!

The time has come when the Government must realize that neither the Press nor the public is content with any longer to wait for a Budget speech and a Budget debate once a year for information as to how the Colony's money is being laid out. Therefore, the new regulations, serving only to cause delay and exasperation and to throw fresh burdens on overworked officials, must be swept into the wastepaper basket, for it is only there that they will cease to bait the Press, insult the public and make the Government look ridiculous.

## Coal Strike Folly

Hopes of a coal settlement rose with the change of attitude on the part of the miners' delegate con-

ference, and the prospects of finding a formula which would meet with the acceptance of both sides became brighter than at any time during the last few months of this intensified struggle. The stupidity of the mineowners, which Mr. Baldwin has denounced in Parliament, and the combined follies of Messrs. A. J. Cook and Herbert Smith, whom Mr. Garvin has brilliantly called "those twins of calamity," are largely responsible for a state of affairs which has already cost the country at least £300,000,000 and will cost it more than we can tell in the loss of foreign markets in the future. The Government itself is vulnerable to the accusation that by interaction it has let the dispute go towards the worst conceivable solution—a dictated peace, sullenly accepted by the vanquished. Whatever the outcome of the present negotiations, the Miners' Federation will have received a shattering blow as the result of the prolonging of the dispute. Messrs. Cook and Smith may reap the discredit they have richly earned, but at the same time propaganda for nationalisation of the coal industry will have received a powerful impetus and the ability of the Government to bring about the reorganisation of the industry may have evaporated. The future of the coal industry at Home is a matter which depends on issues deeper than those which have been made the subject of a dispute that has been prolonged beyond all reason.

## CORRESPONDENCE

VOLUNTEERING

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"]

Sir,—The crisis of June 1924 and subsequent months cannot but have afforded proof positive that the maintenance of an efficient volunteer corps is an essential to the well-being of this Colony. Without casting any reflection upon the work of the battalion of regulars then on garrison duty here and whilst refraining from any observations with regard to the meagre sum of $64,000 which the Colonial Government considers sufficient for its maintenance, it may be said at once, and without fear of contradiction, that the Volunteers were a very live factor in the preservation of peace and order during those troubles times.

Now, Sir, I understand that certain of our leading "taipans" have only very recently given an undertaking to the Volunteer Advisory Committee not to put any obstacle in the way of their employee's attendance at the annual volunteer camp. Why, therefore, are we told by the Press that only mere handfuls are turning up at Fanling? There must be something radically wrong somewhere. There can be few firms who cannot spare a man on one Saturday morning during the year, thus enabling a volunteer to leave for camp on Friday evening and get the benefit of a full day's training on the Saturday.

From enquiries made, it would appear as though many “taipans” are very reticent in the granting of leave with the result that a man probably finds his way to a football match on the Saturday afternoon and then does not consider it worth while to go out to Fankling in the evening. The whole point centres round the fact that it would be these “taipans” who would be the first to shout should another crisis come. Why, therefore, do they not do their utmost towards the efficiency of the H.K.V.D.C.?

EFFORT TO SUIT ALL TASTES.

### [By An Old Playgoer.]

This is “cricket Week.” The batsmen representing the Colony may or may not pile up the score, but the Hong Kong A.D.C. are still “not out” and are in very good form.

On Saturday night four pieces were presented. It was fairly obvious that the most "popular" part of the programme was "The Man With the Bowler Hat" which came last of all. We all know Mr. Hannibal so well that we can be certain of a good laugh if his name is on the programme. A "jolly" as well as "a good fellow," the Hon. Secretary and Treasurer of the local A.D.C. has a sense of humour as valuable to "a good mixer" as his business acumen is valuable to himself in his commercial life. Perhaps it is his delightful sense of humour that enables Mr. Hannibal to succeed in whatever he attempts. Anyhow, he "put it across, the footlights" on Saturday night.

### “A Matter of Time.”

At about 9.30 p.m. the curtain rose and we are introduced to "Jack" and Jimmy" who discuss woman punctuality and marriage. Messrs. Brownjohn and Thomson speak up well and do all that is required of them to convince us that they are modern young men. Hankering after the great adventure of matrimony. "Ethel" is attractive enough to make most men willing to wait hours for the privilege of a smile, or a few words.

She charmingly reminds the sterner sex that “man proposes—woman changes her mind.” She is only on the stage for a few seconds—hardly time enough to thoroughly appreciate her smart clothes—but she is just Miss 1926 to the last word. Mrs. Bloxham always takes well with an audience. She put all of us in a good humour in that amusing curtain raiser.

“The First and the Last.”

John Galsworthy has a great reputation both as a dramatist and an author. This little bit of drama gave Miss Madeleine Chamberlain her opportunity and she took it. She was very convincing in the difficult part of a not quite nice lady. The two men, both of whom have shown talent in other A.D.C. productions were good, but—Well, to be quite frank Galsworthy is at times irritating. You want to see a piece like this more than once to appreciate fully the people who portray these difficult parts. Galsworthy has, of course, genius, but he is difficult. He rather overdoes all this business about the cruel, cruel world and man's injustice to man, etc. There is cruelty and vice and sorrow and meanness in almost every part of the world—even in Hong Kong, but things are not really so bad as Galsworthy makes out. Nor are people quite happy when they are shown everything contemptible in human nature.

Two brothers and a loose woman provide all the entertainment. One of the brothers is a successful lawyer with high hopes of becoming a judge. Mr. Nihill tried hard to make this part convincing. In "Saint Joan" Mr. Nihill was a great success. He is one of the most talented men in Hong Kong with a deservedly fine reputation for A.D.C. or any other sort of work. On Saturday night he somehow or other just did not altogether convince. A huge notice—a confession of murder—was pinned in a most conspicuous place on a couch but although he touched it and circled round it he apparently did not see—it but picked a tiny object off the floor. Of course he always acts well and his elocution is splendid but this time, he just disappointed us. Perhaps the real fault is with Galsworthy or maybe it was the local atmosphere. It is a very difficult part—an unpopular job. Mr. Nihill is clever on the stage. No one else in Hong Kong could have done the work better. This brother is the strong successful selfish man—in the end he lets an innocent man hang rather than injure his own prospects. Any jury investigating the deaths of the "weak" brother and his paramount would elicit quite enough evidence to make the "strong" man get all the scandal he so dreaded.

In a way this drama was the most ambitious effort that the A.D.C. have made for years. The three characters were very, very difficult to portray and it is astonishing that local talent was available. It was really a triumph for the dauntless three.

Mr. Lucey is very versatile. In item 2 of the "Medley" he was the underdog, the weak brother, the "waster" who inspires the emotional paramour with affection so deep that she consents to com-

mit suicide with him. In item 3 of the "Medley" he is a burglar, alternately crafty and brutal. He has a natural gift for acting—if the mantle of the famous Sinclair has not exactly fallen on his shoulders it is true to say that he has done more than any individual in Hong Kong to carry the torch that Sinclair held in his hand for so long.

Mr. Lucey has earned our gratitude. He has great courage for he does try to keep up the standard of A.D.C. work. Most of us say that we want to be amused at the theatre. Mr. Lucey wants to do more for us than that.

He carried the items 2 and 3 on Saturday night on his own shoulders. He was fortunate enough to have Madeleine Chamberlain and Mrs. Chater to act up to him. The star of the evening was Madeleine. Lucey was at his best as the burglar. The weak brother was perhaps not weak enough towards the end.

The Burglar and the Girl.

Why did we guess what would happen at the end before we were half way through this item 3? If only we had not guessed it, what a thrill we should have had! It was just one of those tiny slips that make all the difference. Mrs. Chatter is no inexperienced actress.

For ninety-nine per cent. of the work, she deserves full marks. She just lost that odd mark because she gave the show away too early. She offered everyone the hint and the burglar was the only one that missed it!

Mr. Lucey and Mrs. Chater had the stage to themselves and they really did "entertain us. They were first class in a most exacting episode. We like excitement in a theatre—it is actually more popular than farce if it manages to grip the audience. The two in this piece did really win out. Although item 4 will be voted the best of the "Medley" by most people, on the whole item 3 was really the most entertaining of the lot. And yet item 2 demanded more of those who took part in it. However, the laurels go to Mr. Lucey.

That Bowler Hat.

The moment "Billy" Logan slipped on the stage and sat in an armchair with his hat on we said: "That's the man in the bowler hat." There was no need to lock at the programme, it was so very obvious that the A.D.C. had secured the well known local sportsman for the name part. It was just a delight to see him watching his old friend Hannibal doing just the sort of thing that Hannibal does so well.

Hannbal and Fay Grossman were supposed to be a rather "stodgy" married couple, although the lady was far too attractive to make us believe that she would settle down to the sort of domesticity we saw at the beginning of the farce.

Like all such farces it depends for success upon the personalities of the two or three chief people in it, Hannibal is always most amusing—the delightful thing is that he so obviously enjoys himself while he is entertaining others. There was once a popular song: "Is everybody happy—sure we are; then make your neighbours happy too." Hannibal always reminds us of that song.

The other people were recognisable despite the disguises. Charles was ready to "give 'in 'ell" and spoke up like a man. Reynolds was the villain—you must have a tall dark man for a villain. Southam was the hero—he agonised when Charles tortured him. Mrs. Bloxham made "up for all that to him—she was the heroine.

## Conclusions

The girl in the drama said more than once: "What a pitee." We said it more than once as we saw some empty seats. Hong Kong may be hard up, but Monday is often much more in evidence than on Saturday night.

It, is indeed, a reflection upon the general education of the community that the house was not crowded. It is the duty of every citizen that claims to be educated to support art such as the A.D.C. give us.

Some of us will attend more than once or twice. We sincerely hope that every reader of "The China Mail" will see this "Dramatic Medley." It is a good show and it is well worth the money.

Messrs. Lucey, Hannibal and Nihil and Mrs. Chater and Miss Chamberlain have spent many long hours in the laudable effort to entertain us. All of the others have given up much time for rehearsals, etc., including those beyond the scenes such as Mr. Cornell, Hong Kong must encourage them. We can assure them that they have done well. We want to see the theatre packed full for all the other performances.
