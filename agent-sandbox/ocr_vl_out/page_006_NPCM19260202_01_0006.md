### Hongkong, Tuesday, Feb. 2, 1926

CANTON TAKING RISKS

Yesterday's local papers chronicled, quietly and briefly, the fact that Canton strike pickets, seeking to prevent the fish dealers putting their wares aboard, fired a number of shots at the river steamer "Kinshan." There is no doubt about the attack having been made—scarred and chipped paintwork tell the tale. This incident is fresh proof, if such were needed, that the boycott is not a patriotic movement organized by the Chinese people but is simply a piece of mischief-making carried on by lawless gangs to serve their own ends.

Presumably the Canton Government knows its own business best, but quite apart from the ethical side of the matter, we think it is making a grave mistake in allowing these outrages to continue. For the moment we are not referring to the possible international trouble which might be invited by prolonged attacks on the trade of a friendly nation, but more to the internal dangers the Canton Government is likely to provoke by allowing the strike pickets to pursue their lawless course.

Unless the situation is taken in hand without further delay one of two things is likely to happen. Either the strike pickets, growing in power and insolence, will challenge, not merely the authority, but perhaps the very existence of the Canton Government, or the long-suffering merchants and tradespeople, goaded beyond endurance, will seek their own remedy, or turn to the first

them relief. "The Canton Government" may feel that it can accept these risks without anxiety, but it would do well to remember that China, politically, is a land of sudden changes.

The dangers of the present position seem to us so patent and the promises of peace so alluring, that we quite fail to understand why the Canton Government does not suppress the strike pickets out of hand. This would be no betraying of labour to whom we are told the Canton Government feels so beholden. With the exception of the actual strike leaders, labour is suffering as much as anyone through the boycott and would be among the first to welcome relief.

the Canton Government should suppress the strike pickets, but perhaps the strongest of them all is simply this—if the Chinese people are in favour of the boycott they will continue it of their accord, but if they are not they will let it die out. If they should elect to continue it, the world will then know where it stands. It will know that the will of the masses and not the private scheming of a handful of terrorists is behind the movement. But we do not believe for a moment that the boycott would last half a day if the strike pickets were disbanded; and we are confident that this is the opinion of the rest of the world. The Chinese are a sensible race with an instinct for trading as strong as our own. They only suffer the boycott because they are put in fear of their lives. That is why the world looks to the Canton Government, as a responsible governing body, to end the intolerable tyranny of the strike pickets.

## RUMOUR

"They say" is half a lie, an old proverb reminds us. Very often it is a complete one. Of the ravages of rumour it is impossible to think in complete detail. "They say" begins a gossip—and immediately reputations are torn to ribbons. Disaster is foretold, hope is raised or dashed—all from an idle piece of chatter sprung no one knows whence. Fortunes have arisen out of rumours, and fortunes have disappeared. Values have appreciated or depreciated. No one as a rule stops to think of the possible source of a rumour—the cause or reason of it, or the possible effect its continued repetition is likely to have on men and things. A new Ordinance is called for—intituled as local phraseology has it—"An Ordinance to Prevent the Dissemination of Idle Rumours Likely to Lead to Unsettlement."

Hongkong is prone to rumours. The malady affects all classes. The so and so Company, "they said," had received large contracts. Hence its shares rose in value. This was a common form of rumour which at one time flutters and is given to the things

type. Not so long since that old bcgey of the "They said" appeared. The end of the world was at hand. But Hongkong is still on the map and its recent escapade in getting in front or behind time has been rectified by an unerring Royal Observatory! We cannot suppress Seers and Prophets, but the time has come to endeavour to suppress the idle rumourist who adds nothing to life but an unholy fear of the unknown. Let the reader ask himself or herself if the "unruly member" these last weeks has not been allowed to exercise its functions unduly in passing on the disturbing thought. Fear begets fear. It is possible to pass on the fear to others. Or a suggestion insisted on becomes almost reality. It communicates itself to others who become to believe in it and in the end to act upon it. There is a clear duty imposed upon us all. Of a speech we are asked to apply the formula—(1) Who made it? (2) What did he say? (3) How did he say it? The same thing should apply to rumour. Good Government is the minimum interference with the liberty of the subject and freedom of speech. But there comes a limit, and that limit is reached when the idle tongue of rumour is given free reign and allowed to help on the cause of unsettlement.

## Hayley Morris

Owing to his connection with the Far East the trial and appeal of Hayley Morris and his young wife have aroused as much interest out here as at Home. From beginning to end it has been a most unsavoury case, the stories of the girl victims of his passions being pathetic in the extreme. His appeal has failed, as was to be expected by those who read in the Home paper the exhaustive reports of his very remarkable trial. He has been proved to be a thorough scoundrel. He has sinned not only against the law—he has shaken the confidence of many in mankind. It may be questioned by some whether such cases as this should be published at all in the Press, but these critics can be quite easily confounded. More than any punishment in prison the exposure of a man in the position of Hayley Morris will be hard to bear. For his feelings, either during the sensational trial or when he is eventually released, no account need be taken. He has received his deserts. The real point of the publicity given to the case is the serious warning that it must undoubtedly convey to young and impressionable girls. But for the publicity and the lessons afforded thereby the list of those who fall on all too easy prey to the passion of wicked scoundrels might be swelled, reacting on the moral tone of the whole community.

CHRISTOPHER.

The new official report on GIB Gibraltar, just published in London, is causing some amusement by its firm statement that “no aliens are ever allowed to be born there.” The civil population is now 17,000, besides some 8,000 aliens (Span-iards), and 1,500 British subjects who come into work daily from the adjacent Spanish territory.

Many visits to Gibraltar have revealed several strange little peculiarities. Spain, for example, officially regards Gibraltar as a part of Spain and includes it in its postal system and rates, while Great Britain only regards the Spanish as aliens and makes them all leave the town at gun fire, except those few who have residential permits, and even those must go indoors and stop there at the second gun fire.

# THE USE OF SUBMARINE.

But, strangely enough, in spite of the rigid regulations regarding other nationals, the Moors are allowed to come and go, just as they please. It is true that Gebel-Tarik was the first landing place of the Moors in Europe, and the British powers that be evidently regard it as still a Moorish city. In fact, one Moor, at Tangier, was heard to speak of Gibraltar as "our port in Spain."

It is perhaps not a good thing to give exclusive attention, as was recently done, to the question whether the submarine can be abolished altogether, for that, at the moment at least, is not a practical matter. On the other hand, the regulation of the use of the submarine in war is not only a practical question but a very urgent one, for the submarine exists, there are always possibilities of war in some part of the world, and as soon as there is war between any two naval Powers the controversy over the rights of the submarine will arise again acutely. There is a school which takes the view that in the futuro no naval Power, when hard pressed, will submit to any restrictions in its use of the submarine. That is a gratuitous assumption; much depends on the character of the Power in question and on the nature of the struggle in which it is engaged; much again, on the condition of the world and the strength of weakness of the League of Nations at the time. These things are all hypothetical but we can say with confidence that the

more the nations of the world can be persuaded to adopt and promise loyalty to a code of law on such a question the more difficult it will be for any one of them to break away in time of stress and defy public opinion. The Grotlus Society has just been urging the adoption of a code somewhat stiffer than that discussed at the Washington Conference of 1921, which has never come into force. The Society's provisions (remarks the "Manchester Guardian") would forbid absolutely the destruction of neutral merchantmen unless their conduct was openly and flagrantly unneutral, and it would only allow the destruction even of enemy merchantmen—supposing that this could not be forbidden—if the safety of the crew and passengers was provided for. All these details are proper matters for discussion at an international conference, and it is highly desirable that the prohibition of "unrestricted" warfare and the settlement of the necessary restrictions should not be lost from sight and mind because the Washington agreement has not been generally accepted.
