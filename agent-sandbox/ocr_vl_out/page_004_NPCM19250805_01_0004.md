## Hongkong, Wednesday, August 5, 1925

Fearless criticism is to be commended when there is evil to be righted; it is excusable when there are two sides to the question and the critic has reason to believe in the justness of his cause; but when delivered in ignorance of the true facts either through lending a too ready ear to contaminated sources or failure to institute proper enquiries it may cause incalculable harm besides damaging the critic's reputation. In the third category, without a shadow of doubt, all who know the true facts of the case must place the article in "John Bull" from which the following is an extract:—"The British cruisers which have arrived in Shanghai and the marines and bluejackets landed in that City are bolstering up the system of child slavery and child murder and the British taxpayers' money and authority are being employed to enforce indescribable misery, disease and suffering on the infant life of China." As against this terribly distorted version in which there

remains not even a. vestige of the truth, let us take the facts as we who are more or less on the spot are aware of them. We know that the industrial conditions in foreign mills in China are undoubtedly superior to anything prevailing in the native mills; we know that the failure to secure the attendance of the two thirds majority of the Shanghai ratepayers which was necessary before reforms in all mills could be instituted was due to the deliberate abstention of a section of the ratepayers who were not British and who had been influenced to that course by owners whose mills would be most affected by the proposed reforms; we know, too, that the British Government proposed to take further steps to legislate in the event of the deadlock resulting from the absence of a quorum continuing. Unfortunately, the proportion of informed to uninformed would be at its lowest amongst those readers of a journal which has come to be regarded as the organ of the "working man" and whilst in many cases the allegations of "British oppression" will conflict with the innate good sense and reason of the "working man" and lead him to a true appraisement of their value, there will be many homes in which credence will be placed in such statements through lack of information to the contrary and the equally untrue statements of members of a certain political party which have redounded in the Far East to their everlasting discredit. It was to counteract the influence at Home of such gross misrepresentations that the Hongkong resolutions (now supported by Amoy and likely to be further supported in the near future by Shanghai) were despatched Home for publication, but if the "champions of the working class" have descended to such depths in their efforts to prove the Government in the wrong and to weaken its hands,

It is doubtful if they will allow to be published in their organs what amounts to a grave censure on themselves. As a result there will still be many at Home who will not have a chance of being able to judge for themselves the falseness even of such statements as the following:—“At the bottom of the trouble is the exploitation of the Chinese people by the foreign industrialists in whose interests these costly warships are being despatched to China.” In a previous article we gave it as our considered opinion that at “the bottom of the trouble” was Bolshevik activity and almost it would seem that certain British organs and politicians are going out of their way to encourage it.

## SCHOOLBOYS

If striking or absenting servants are to be punished, it follows that schoolboys who set such a bad example must also be punished. It seems simple logic and simple justice. And if the former did so because of fear—craven or otherwise—then the punishment in the case of schoolboys must be doubly severe; for schoolboys are in the first line of intelligent thought, and of course, know no fear! But, Mrs. Beeton again—first catch your schoolboy striker. And this, in addition to the facts in connection with this particular matter suggests the thought that heads of schools would be far better engaged in considering the effect of their past teaching and influence, and taking steps to make it more perfect than it is. It is possible to write and speak a considerable amount of rubbish on education in Hongkong, and on this subject very few angels step in where fools do not fear to tread. The fact is that a number of Europeans are set in the midst of hordes of boys and young men and expected to give them the essence of Western education. The teachers are European and the young people are Chinese. The hours of tuition bear no comparison with the hours that boys are either in the home or the street. A boy may not even be under direct parental control but under the somewhat hazy guidance of a person known as a guardian. In addition it has to be remembered that just as there are what is known as "rice-christians," there are also those who may be termed "rice-educationists"—the many who desire a smattering—for time and money does not allow of anything else—of that which may possibly help to get them some sort of office positions. This somewhat sketchily states the position; it is designed to show the difficulties attached to local education, which must be the despair of the idealists in a singularly important profession. In addition, it has to be remembered that the schoolboy comes into a contact with masters and superiors, that has hitherto been unknown to him, and, being treated as a supposedly intelligent entity, is prone to lose a sense of proportion, and, in homely phase, being offered an inch, takes a mile. Above all there is the universal schoolboy spirit—not unknown in Britain—which is quickly roused, passionate, idealistic, and at heart intensely patriotic. Who will deny that the Chinese schoolboy has not been moved with a sense of wrongly inspired patriotism which has moved him to acts of foolishness? If this is agreed, shall we blame the boy or the system or influence which has had no effect upon him in a great crisis, and shall we stoop to punishment where it can be inflicted without any possibility of injustice being done? We are certainly in favour of a measure of punishment, leaving the declaration of that punishment to those in a position best to decree and administer it. In this way, it is likely that youthful culprits will see the error of their ways, and be inclined to use a little thought in future actions. For it is thought that needs to be insisted upon. And if seventeen professors need it, how more, seventeen hundred, or seventeen thousand schoolboys. Suggestions have been made by heads of schools as to future methods. A crisis is of no use if it has not taught anything; and this particular crisis will have been of no avail if it has not urged Heads of Schools, teachers, the Education Department, and the members of the Education Board, to take serious thought for the morrow and to see wherein it is possible to lead to a reasonable state of efficiency. The nation that has the schools has the future, said Bismarck. In the case of Hongkong and the larger case of China, that has still to be proved.

## FATAL DIVE

INQUEST ON INDIAN DRIVER.

An Indian mule driver attached to the Army Service Corps-dived from a barge into the R.A.S.C. camber at the Hongkong Naval Yard on July 7 and was drowned. An inquest was held yesterday at the Kowloon Magistracy to inquire into the circumstances.

A fellow Indian mule driver said that he, the deceased and four other men were on guard at the Naval Yard in connection with the strike. After the tiffin interval on the day in question witness went to the camber for a swim, and while on an anchored steamer to which he had swum he observed deceased about to pump into, the water. Witness called to him that the water was deep but he took no notice. He rose to the surface once and then disappeared. Witness swam to the spot but could find no trace of the body.

L/Cpl. C. F. Masters, East Surreys, who was N.C.O. in charge of the guards, said he saw the deceased jump into the water from a barge and as he did not re-appear, witness had the barge removed, thinking that he might have been caught underneath. He then dived into the water followed by others and grappleings were obtained from the water police. Nothing was seen of the body, however, until it rose to the surface 24 hours afterwards.

Staff Sergt. Finlayson, of the Royal Army Service Corps, in reply to a question by the Coroner, Mr. E. W. Hamilton, as to whether he had received orders regarding swimming in the camber, said that no one was supposed to unless a life boat was in attendance.

Capt. E. W. Morris, in command of the corps, said that he was not aware that Indians bathed in the camber. Since the fatality strict orders had been issued that nobody was allowed to bathe in the Naval Yard basin.

The Coroner found that the deceased met his death through an accident. If the unfortunate man had not either deliberately or ignorantly disobeyed orders the accident would not have occurred. No blame could be attached to anyone.

## POETIC CHINA

## RAILWAY CENTENARY; TRIBUTE

On July 3 the delegates to the tenth International Railway Congress were entertained at the waggon works by the London and North-Eastern railway—an event which explains why the railway centenary is being celebrated now instead of in September. Railwaymen from most countries where the steel offspring of Stephenson's genius link town and country and sea together have met in England for that conference. Mainly through the initiative of workmen on the Italian railways, a handsome bronze shield was presented in recognition of Great Britain's record as the railway pioneer. But another tribute, sent all the way from China, is of at least equal interest. It takes the form of three exquisitely embroidered scrolls, the black Chinese characters showing in bold relief against the golden background. Mr. Chin Chun Wang, chief delegate of the Chinese Government to the International Railway Congress, has translated the message of the scrolls as follows:—

In celebration of the centenary of the first British railway:

Here a century ago was born the world-wida system of rail

Here to-day are gathered representatives of all nations in praise and celebration, conquering spaces and linking seas and land.

Leading progress and prosperity hand to hand.

Oh! yo' inventor, great benefactor of mankind.

Yea, we shall ever enshrine thee in our hearts and grateful mind.

With hearty congratulations,

KUNG CHAO YEN,

Minister of Communications,

Peking.

CRUISER "BRISBANE'S" MOVEMENTS.

(To the Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—The recent reports in the Press about the solicitude of the Australian Labour Party over the employment of H.M.A.S. "Brisbane" in China are misleading. They give one the impression that the "Brisbane" has left Hongkong for Mediterranean waters as the result of the discussion in the Australian Federal Parliament.

## SCHOOLS AND THE STRIKE

To the Editor of the China Mail.)

Sir,—First of all let me say that I think the students in the Hongkong Schools had very little to do with and very little influence in the strike, and that any students who caused trouble should be treated as children and punished accordingly. Secondly, the fees at all Government Schools should be carefully revised. The trouble began in the Government School, Queen's College, where the fees are much too low and are the same as they were in 1910, viz. $60 per annum for a Day Boy. Several other schools in the Colony, Church and Mission Schools and Private Schools, have much higher fees and have raised their fees in recent years; for example, the fees at some schools are as high as $160 or more per annum for a Day Boy. I do not advocate that all fees should be the same, but it is worthy of note that to educate a boy in a Grant-in-Aid Non-Government School costs the Government, on the average, about one-quarter the cost in a Government School like Queen's College, for the same type of education.

I beg to suggest that the Government should

(1) Fine $10 all boys who were absent from Queen's College on June 18, and refuse them admittance to all other schools in the Colony.

(2) In future, charge an Entrance Fee of $10 for all new boys at Queen's College.

(3) Double or increase the fees at all Government Schools.

(4) Forbid all other schools in the Colony, Grant-in-Aid and Private Schools, to admit boys who leave Queen's College without meeting the conditions laid down.

(5). Have a more strict inspection of all schools teaching work up to Matriculation Standard, and not controlled by and/or staffed with at least one Euro-

(6) Keep in touch more closely, through English Inspectors, with the Vernacular Schools.

(7) Give the Heads of Shoals regular opportunities of discussing educational matters with the Education Department and have several Heads of Schools on the Board of Education.—Yours, etc.,

Hongkong, Aug. 4, 1925.

## DRIVER'S CLAIM

A taxi-cab driver claimed in the Summary Court yesterday, $100 deposited with his previous employers, the Hongkong and Kowloon Taxicab Company.

This money was deposited by him when he signed a contract with the Company and if his service had been continued would have been returned to him at the end of three years.

His Lordship held that the contract covered the action of the employers and that the man could not claim the return of the money.

Mr. M. K. Lo applied for permission to amend his claim to one of damages for wrongful dismissal and this was granted, the case being adjourned sine die.

Mr. Turner, of Messrs. Johnson, Stokes & Master, represented the Taxi-Cab Company.
