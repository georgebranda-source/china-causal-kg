## Hongkong Monday, July 5, 1926

## JULY 4

In Hongkong, as elsewhere, American Independence Day will be celebrated today instead of yesterday. It is only proper that the greatest event in the history of our American cousins should not pass entirely unnoticed in this Colony. The total foreign community is so insignificant compared with the great populace of Orientals that every opportunity should be welcomed for bringing them together in a social and friendly manner. As joint members of the great English-speaking race, as co-workers in the great cause of civilisation, the Americans and the British have perhaps more in common than any other two countries. Here in Hongkong there has been the greatest esprit de corps between the British and the American communities — a camaraderie that is evidenced by the growing trade between Hongkong and "Usona," as a former American Ambassador to Great Britain once suggested as a short and appropriate name for the United States of North America.

Whatever the Treaty of Independence between Great Britain and America may have meant to Britons 150 years ago is a matter of less than academic interest to Britons of to-day. And the same may almost truthfully be said of the Americans. In their maintenance of freedom and liberty they have during those 150 years marched step by step with Britons all over the world. They have ever had the preamble of the Constitution clear in their vision—they have formed "a more per

fect Union, established justice,

ensured domestic tranquillity, pro-

moted the general welfare, and

secured the blessings of liberty to

themselves and their posterity."

A nation that has those ideals emblazoned on her banners cannot but command the esteem and affection of Great Britain and of the Greater Britain overseas. In any scheme for the uplifting of mankind in general she will not look to the British Empire in vain for co-operation. In all, that tends to advance the cause of Civilisation Britons and Americans will ever be found walking and working side by side along the corridors of Time—Allies in peace as well as in war!

## OBITUARY

THE REV. G. P. BOSTICK

The Kueiteh, Honan, Correspondent, on June 22, records the death by typhus of the Rev. G. P. Bostick, of Pochow, in Anhui. He contracted this disease on a journey he had just made to Tientsin and although he was brought up to Kueiteh for medical attention as soon as the illness manifested itself, all efforts failed on his behalf and he passed away yesterday morning after about a week's illness.

Thus closes a life of 37 years of missionary service in China, years of hard work, often under conditions of great danger and hardship, for Mr. Bostick was a worthy member of the old school of missionary and never spared himself in his efforts to advance the Kingdom of God in this land of his love and adoption. He was nearly 70 years of age and was contemplating retiring to the States next year, but his oft expressed thought that he would like to end his days in China has been granted and Chinese soil receives him at last. The tears of genuine sorrow shed by his Chinese friends and their manifestations of love and regard for him show that his labour of many years and hard work have not been in vain. Those to whom he has been truly a father in God will not soon forget him as they try to carry on in the work whose foundations were by him so well and truly laid.

### ST. STEPHEN'S

TERMINATION OF SUCCESSFUL

## de de de

The bazaar at the University grounds in aid of St. Stephen's College Building Fund was brought to a successful conclusion on Saturday. The opening was performed by Mr. Li Yautsun, Chairman of the Chinese Chamber of Commerce, who referred to the reputation for scholarship which the students of the College were maintaining and to the greater facilities which would be provided by the new school building.

The presentation of prizes to winners in various competitions and a vote of thanks to those who had assisted, brought the proceedings to a termination. Among those who were mentioned as having worked for the success of the bazaar were: the Chairman, Mr. Chau Cheung-nin, Miss Middleton-Smith and the staff of St. Stephen's Girls' School, Miss Siu Wai-sheung and other past pupils, Mrs. J. Hunt and friends Mr. Teo Tsun-on and University graduates.

A number of Chinese push cyclists were fined at the Kowloon Magistracy this morning for traffic offences. One received a lesson on the "rule of the road" before he was let off with a fine of $5.

Because of the alowness of coolie enlistment, and the great popular outcry against impressing coolies into service for the northern expedition, the Government has raised the combined pay, food allowance and straw sandal allowance from $15 to $20 a month per man. It is understood that the advance will also be enjoyed by those who have already been enlisted or shanghailed at the lower figure.

The Military Council has announced that all traffic over the Canton-Shiu-kwan railway will be confined to troop trains for the next fifteen days, or until July 16. Four troop-trains will be run north daily, each train carrying approximately 1,000 men.

The National Government makes official announcement that word has been received from Changsha and other northern points that Wu Pei-fu's allies have decided to evacuate the whole province of Hunan, and confine their military efforts to holding Hupeh.

A military disaster of medium importance was reported to-day to the Nationalist government when word arrived that 1,000 men of the Third Army, under General Chu Putak, were surrounded and disarmed, after large loss of life. The disaster occurred in a mountainous pass near the mouth of the Chien Chow River, when the detachment was marching to the Hunan-Kwangsi border to reinforce General Tang Shang-chi. They suddenly found themselves surrounded on three sides by the soldiers of General Yung Ta-tang, an aide of Cheng Kwingming. The Cantonese lost 300 killed, half again as many wounded, and all their guns and ammunition and supplies. No captives were taken, the survivors being allowed to struggle back to the railroad.

Yeh Kai-chin, who has been repeatedly defeated by General Tang Shang-chi, recently wired to Ho Yao-tso and Tang Hsien for help from the Hunan Protecting Army, but this help was refused, and then a telegraphic appeal to Wu Pei-fu himself brought orders to evacuate Changsha and move gradually over into Hupeh.

## To Evacuate Hunan?

The situation in Hunan is further complicated by the fact that Cheng Hung-hsi was recently badly defeated by the Cantonese troops, losing many men by death and surrender, and 1,400 rifles. He appealed for aid but got none, and has now offered his submission to Canton. Chen Hung-hsi will soon be in command of a body or re-fitted troops fighting for the Nationalist cause.

At Hengchowfu more than 1,800 war prisoners taken by Tang Shang-chi are being kept in the Sin Min Middle School compound.

The Canton Government yesterday formally accepted the submission and alliance of Generals Yuan Tso-ming and Wang Tien-pei of Szechuan and Kweichow, and their armies, totalling about 20,000 men, will be re-armed and paid by Canton. ## CANTON CUSTOMS

NO SIGNS OF PARTIES

WEAKENING.

Neither the Chinese Maritime Customs nor the Canton Government shows any signs of weakening in the struggle over the attempt to unionise the customs service.

Sun Fo, mayor of Canton, persists in his refusal to give police protection to the non-union Customs employees, who continue to eat and sleep in the Customs buildings rather than run the risk of interference by pickets while going to and from their homes.

In this connection it has developed that Sun Fo, who sq strongly champions unionising the Customs service, only last week wrote to the Commissioner of Labour strongly urging against the granting of a charter to a proposed union of Canton street cleaners. The major declared that civil employees of this class should not be permitted to form unions. But he still champions the unionisation of Customs coolers.

### PICKET "ARRESTED."

SEQUEL TO ATTEMPTED INTERFERENCE.

Early this morning an attempt by river strike pickets to interfere with the "Fatshan," the Hongkong steamer, resulted in one of the pickets landing in the Shameen jail.

A sampan loaded with chickens approached the "Fatshan" to market the fowl, when a picket boat intervened, and one of the pickets jumped on to the sampan. A launch from the "Fatshan" then chased away the picket boat, and the picket on the sampan was brought to Shameen under arrest. It is planned to release him at the Canton end of the British bridge. If he cannot establish his identity and affiliations with the pickets on guard there, he will probably meet with rough handling.

## Oriental Hotel Strike

The employees of the Sincere Company's hotel, the Oriental, have gone on strike in spite of the mandate of the Labour Commissioner that they must await further arbitration of their wage dispute. The hotel, the second largest in Canton, is now closely picketed, both guests and employees being subject to interference and annoyance. The Government is doing nothing to enforce its arbitration-mandate.

## Merchnnts Abducted

At Sheung Pak station on the Canton-Samshui Railway to-day four leather merchants who were buying tickets to Canton were seized by armed bandits and dragged away to be held for ransom. This station is only 20 miles from Canton. The station guards did nothing to interfere, nor did the 200 soldiers on the train which was at the station, offer any protection.

## HONOUR AMONGST—?

TALE-TELLING LEADS TO DISMISSALS

CANTON OFFICIALS "SQUEEZE"

A great scandal has been created here by charges and counter-charges which have resulted in the dismissal of three of the most prominent members of the Naval Administration Bureau on charges of graft. The men let out, by personal mandate of General Chang Kai-shek, - are Poon Man-chee, head of the bureau, and his two chief assistants, Ho Chit and Wu King-ving.

Poon Man-chee's position as chief of the bureau has been filled by the appointment of Lam Chiang-hoong, commander of the Canton and river forts. He now holds both positions.

As the tangled story goes, Mr. Yap Ho-sze, a prosperous merchant, suggested to Poon Man-chee the naming of Ho Chit and Wu King-ying, and said that the three of them could work with him, Mr. Yap, for large squeeze.

## CANTON PROTEST

The Canton Foreign Office has authorised the publication of the following statement, which is sent us by the Bureau of Information (Ministry of Foreign Affairs) Canton:

In view of the excellent relations now existing between the Government at Cmton and the American Consular authorities, the Acting Minister for Foreign Affairs believes that no misunderstanding will be created by the publication of the following letter, dated June 30, from Mr. Douglas Jenkins, American Consul-General, to Mr. Chen Yu-Jen (Eugene Chen), Acting Minister for Foreign Affairs, with the reply of the latter dated July 2:

## American Consul's Letter

Honourable Chen Yu-jen, Acting Minister of Foreign Affairs, Canton. Sir,—Adverting to this Consulate General's despatch of June 16 in acknowledgment of your note of June 4, 1928, concerning the abolition of the office of Commissioner of Foreign Affairs and the intention of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to deal with all international cases in the future. I have the honour to explain that while this Consulate General is pleased to correspond directly with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, it is of course understood that recognition is not implied. I have the honour, etc., etc.

(SGD.) DOUGLAS JENKINS.

American Consul General."

Acting Minister For Foreign Affairs' Reply.

With a clear apprehension of what it all means, my Government is striving to forward the work of establishing the new equilibrium between the Chinese system (i.e., the Chinese people in their organisation as a social and political economic aggregate) and the altered environment brought about largely by foreign intercourse and pressure.

(SGD.) CHEN YU JEN.

Acting Minister for Foreign Affairs.

And though unrecognised but withal the only ruling group in China at the moment that really governs, my Government is not without hope of planting the foundation of a great new structure of relations between China and America, and other friendly and profitable markets for their goods and services, which will enable the Chinese people to live in freedom and to work out the modernisation of their country in terms of the best, both in their historic experience and individual culture, and in the doctrinal system and material progress of the west.—I have etc, etc.

"Sir, I have the honour to acknowledge the receipt of your letter dated June 30, in which you explain,—what has already been quite clear and obvious to me—that recognition is not implied in your despatch of June 16 acknowledging my note of June 4, which notified you of the abolition of the office of Commissioner for Foreign Affairs and the decision of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to deal with all international cases in the future.

Though "in ordinary circumstances your letter might call for no specific reply, I believe the best interests of the American people, and of the Chinese people as represented by my Government, would be served if I make the categorical statement that while my Government (which has stabilised an independent political regime founded here nearly a decade ago, and has unified a group of territories larger in area than France and Italy combined, with a population of 60,000,000 people) demands that it be treated with respect, it neither desires nor expects from America and other Foreign Powers the sort of recognition which even considerations of political realism and international dignity have not prevented them from granting to the phantom governments successively set up in Peking by the Mandarin squeezers, military plunderers and ex-bandit chiefs. The Foreign Powers, apparently, have not yet realised that Peking has long ceased to represent the Chinese nation, and that it is to-day an organ of exploitation and plunder in the hands of the Mandarinate and the Northern Militarists. As long as this fundamental fact remains ungrasped by the Foreign Powers, the state of China must necessarily worsen and some of the ominous possibilities of the situation may well become realities.
