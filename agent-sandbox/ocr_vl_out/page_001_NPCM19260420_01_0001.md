## UNLIKE CLYDEBANK

POLICE "EVICT" LOCAL TENANTS.

300 YEAR INHABITANTS.

Three hundred years' association is claimed by some local Chinese, householders who were revicted by the Police yesterday after a long series of incidents starting from a deluge of a few wet seasons ago.

Early in the morning, divisional Inspector J. Ogg, of Wanchat station, took a posse comprising a European sergeant and ten Indians and Chinese down to Wong-nei-chong village. With the officers were representatives of the Public Works Department, for which several gangs of cookies had assembled to do the manual part of the job.

Verbal notice had been given on Saturday that the houses were to be demolished. As is characteristic of the Chinese masses, the inhabitants of the village delayed things till the very last minute. When the Police arrived they took matters stoically—after having hoped against hope for months on end, that they would be allowed to stay in tenements which nature had made both dangerous and insignificantly.

There was no "fun" similar to that when the Sheriff's officer went to evict the Clydebank workers who refused to pay rent. The only incident was when two women disputed ownership of a small board that was only worth a few cents. Knick-knacks and sundries that the foreigner was positive nobody could have any earthly use for were carried away with precision.

Each house was of only one floor and three ordinary office desks would have filled up the floor space up to the roof. The removal was a matter of only an hour or so and then the "pioneers" set to work with picks and shovels.

When the race-course was "mudded" a few years ago, the village was flooded out and a number of the hovels collapsed. With the deluges repeated, the place was deemed dangerous. Water stood several feet deep in the narrow lanes, and earth was piled up everywhere.

Negotiations are proceeding between the Government and respective owners for an interchange of lots. During the last months, a number of the small owners have been paid at Government expense to live away from their homes.

Yesterday the real work of pulling the village down began and modern houses will, it is hoped spring up very soon.

A number of the inhabitants are very hazy as to the conditions and they are afraid that they may be (in their opinion) unfairly treated. Their one obsession is to retain their present lots, but no one seems to be clear how he is going to keep the rickety house standing, or what he is going to live in. The Government has made a promise and a solution must come in time.

These people are of a clan which migrated to Hongkong long before it was seeded to Britain. Some have been there for not less than seven generations, whilst others maintain that their family has lived there for three hundred years.

In the property boom of three years back, a number were tempted to sell their “heirlooms.” But they did not leave the place. They went a little higher up the hill, “marked out a claim” on Crown land, put up matsheds, and are still living there to-day.

## RENTS ORDINANCE

PETITION TO CONTINUE RESTRICTION.

THE PROS AND CONS.

That a petition is to be prepared for presentation to the Government was the decision arrived at by the general meeting of the Hongkong Tenants' Protective Association.

Points mentioned by speakers were:—

Reasons advanced for resembling the Rents Restriction Ordinance (in the speakers' opinion): All effects of restrictions on owners of property; the increase of 15 per cent. on standard events (sanctioned last year) not being high enough in ratio to the rise in other costs of living; the number of vacant tenements in the Colony.

Arguments against the rescinding of the Ordinance were advanced, as follows: If no restriction is placed on the power to increase rentals, rents will be put up to such an extent that other costs will have to go up in proportion, thereby, tending to a high rate of living, demands for increased wages, etc., the statement that "if rents of restricted houses (i.e., those coming within the Ordinance) are put up, tenants can move" to houses at present vacant, is not true in fact, as the question of situation is of paramount importance to business, shops etc., bona fide landlords do not favour exploitation in rents, for which speculators and subsidiary landlords (i.e., "principal tenants") are responsible; the number of vacant tenements in the Colony, at present is not due to the presence of the Ordinance, but to the crisis.

As has been stated, the meeting was convened to discuss a proposal to petition the Government to continue the Ordinance (after June 30, 1926) or maintain some form of limited restriction.

## LINK WITH PAST

LADY BLAKE'S WORK

### (Reuter's Servica.)

(Keuter 8 Service.)

a

London April 20

The death is announced of Lady Blake, widow of the late Sir Henry Arthur Blake, G.C.M.G., K.C.M.G., C.M.G., F.R.G.S., (ex-Governor of Hongkong) at Youghal, Ireland.

Lady Blake was in Hongkong during the five years (1898-1903) in which the late Sir Henry Blake was Governor and in which period the New Territories were taken over from the Chinese (April 1899.)

. Lady Blake was the daughter of Bernal Osbourn, a well-known politician of the mid-Victorian period and the late Sir Henry Blake, who was a popular and able administrator, admitted that he owed much of his success in life to the advice and ability of Lady Blake.

Lady Blake took a keen interest in the movement organised to abolish the compulsory binding of women's feet in China and Sir Henry Pollock, K.C., who was Attorney-General during the late Sir Henry's administration, recalls a meeting in Hongkong, addressed by Mrs. Archibald Little, a moving spirit in the organisation, over which Lady Blake presided.

Lady Blake and Miss Olive Blake, their daughter, who married shortly after leaving Hongkong, accompanied the late Sir Henry to Ceylon where he was Governor for a short time; on his retirement in 1907 and since his death, Lady Blake has lived at the late Sir Henry's home at Youghal, Ireland.

The late Sir Henry Blake commenced his public career in Ireland, having been a resident magistrate and one of the five special resident magistrates selected by the Government to concert and carry out measures for the pacification of a large portion of Ireland.

Prior to coming to Hongkong, the late Sir Henry Blake had been Governor of the Bahamas and of Jamaica.

## JUNK PIRACY

## ATTACK IN CHINESE WATERS

CARGO STOLEN

A report made by the master of a junk which returned to the Colony on Sunday gives details of a piracy in Chinese waters on April 8 while on a voyage to Amoy with a cargo of kerosene and matches.

About 18 men set off from the shore near Sze Ling in Chinese territory and, bringing their boat alongside the junk, clambered aboard. Brandishing revolvers, they drove the crew into the forehold and closed the hatch on them. One member of the crew who attempted to lift the hatch cover to see what the pirates were doing was shot in the elbow.

The men were imprisoned for seven days and the only food which the pirates gave them was one meal a day. Hearing no noise on board on the last day of their imprisonment, the crew scrambled out and found the pirates had gone, having taken them cargo to the value of $1,724.

The junk made for Samun where a report of the occurrence was made to the Customs authorities and then steered for Hongkong. The Water Police were notified on arrival and had the injured man sent to the Kwong Wah Hospital.

## CAPTAIN FINED:

CAUSING OBSTRUCTION IN

HARBOUR.

RESULT OF "STRIKE:"

A fine of $40 (or, in default, one month's hard labour) was imposed on the captain of a local steamer, at the Marine Court this morning.

Defendant was Captain J. N. Catela, master of the m.s. "Wong Shek Kurig" who pleaded not guilty before Lentus Com. G. E. Hole, R.N. (Marine Magistrate), to a charge of anchoring in the harbour so as to obstruct the fairways at 3.30 p.m. on April 14.

Mr. W. R. Hillyer, boarding officer of the Harbour Office, testified that the steamer was three ships' lengths west of buoy No. B47, and that there was not enough room.

Replying to the bench, witness said that the ship was actually causing an obstruction to the s.s. "New Mathilde," whose captain he was going to call as a witness for the prosecution.

Captain E. G. Rapley, master of the s.s. "New Mathilde," said that he was taking his vessel from the mooring to Stonecutters Island and had to alter his course, thereby cutting through the line of buoys, because of the other steamer being in the way.

Defendant stated that he left Canton, owing to the strike, and had only a crew of two. He was short of gasoline and his company had promised to have gasoline ready near Green Island.

The fine was paid.

## DANISH AIRMAN

NEEDLESS ANXIETY IN SHANGHAI.

A FORCED LANDING.

(Reuter's Service.)

Shanghai, April 19.

Botved, who is flying from Copenhagen to Tokyo, has been missing since yesterday when he left Canton for Shanghai.

The telegram received yesterday stated that he passed over Foochow at 2.20 p.m. He was thus due at Shanghai about 5 o'clock yesterday afternoon. A large crowd, including the majority of the local Danish colony, vainly awaited him at the aerodrome till dusk.

No news has been received of the airman's whereabouts up to the time of telegraphing (12:35 p.m.)

The Foreign Office has received word that Lieut. Botved has been forced to land at Ninghal, in Chekiang.
