### “YOLANDA.”

A NATION'S PRAYERS

## QUEEN'S THEATRE GREAT ROMANCE

SPLENDID PAGEANTRY

A beautiful princess, a handsome prince, a stern father, a crafty king, an idiot son and a wicked ambassador—these are the central figures in "Yolanda," the film romance women round the lives of the Duke of Burgundy and Louis XI of France, now appearing at the Queen's Theatre. With such leading characters, need it be said that the story is romantic to a degree, that love, intrigue and combat a find their place and contribute towards the great climax.

Benides a fascinating story, "Yolanda" offers some brilliant acting, the character study of Louis XI, for one, being done with remarkable realism and penetration.

"Yolanda's chief claim to fame, however, is the splendour of its parceantry. The glamour of medieval court and palace is portrayed on uogigantic scale, not merely in the matter of interior settings, but in the screening of entire castles with hundreds of courtiers and men-at-arms. For these scenes alone, 'Yolanda' is one of the grandest films of medieval romance yet screened in Hong Kong.

## FLYING A SHORE

A NOVEL PROJECT

Sir Alan Cobham, the famous airman, intends to carry out an interesting experiment when he leaves for "American on board the "Homeric" next Wednesday.

He will take with him his little moth seaplane. As the liner approaches New York, he will Tower the seaplane into the sea with a number of mail bags, and taking off from the sea, will fly with them into the city.

Sir Alan Cobham thinks there is a possibility of considerable acceleration of the carrying of mails in this manner.

He proposed to work out all the details of lifting the machines over the side to water and also to see how far it is possible to take off from the ship itself.—British Wireless Service.

## BY AIR TO INDIA

EARL WINTERTON ON FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS.

London, October 29.—Earl Winterton in a statement to the Imperial Conference on October 28 said that there was no need to stress the importance of air communication between India and other parts of the Empire. India had a far larger population than all the rest of the Empire combined. She was the largest individual customer of Great Britain.

Since direct rail communication between India and Great Britain was ruled out owing to the present state of the world for many years to come, perhaps for all time, the air afforded the only alternative, supplementing and extending the present traffic by sea in persons and goods.

There could be no doubt that India was destined to play a prominent part in the development of air communications between countries of the Empire.

Any service between Europe and the Far East or Australia would en its way have to cross India.

It was reasonable to anticipate a function of these two routes which would almost inevitably be on Indian soil and ultimately would figure as one of the most vital points in the Empire chain.

## BRAKE FOR AEROPLANES

London, October 26.—Mechanism for providing aeroplanes with a touch needed brake was described by Mr. G. H. Dowty in a lecture at the Institution of Aeronautical Engineers. This consisted of a drum carrying a cable with an anchor fixed at the outer end. The anchor would be lowered on landing and engage a series of cross ropes, while the unwinding of the cable would cause the drum to rotate and operate the mechanism for obtaining the uniform resistance which would permit aircraft landing at 90 miles an hour to come steadily to rest within 200 feet, namely, half the length provided on landing decks.—"Times of Ceylon"

## 60 th BIRTHDAY

### THE LATE DR. SUN YAT SEN'S MEMORY

Carlton, Nov. 13.

One of the biggest popular celebrations ever held in China was the Canton commemoration of the 60th birthday of Dr. Sun Yat-sen held yesterday. A conservative estimate places the number of people attending the various exercises at over 500,000.

All but necessary work was stopped while the city threw itself into an ardent manifestation of love for the late leader of the Nationalist movement and a determination to bring his dreams of a united and free China to realisation.

The celebration began with a monster lantern procession on the evening of Thursday. The number of paraders was over 200,000 comprising members of the labour unions, student bodies, peasants, and the official and merchant bodies.

Streets Blocked 4 Hours.

Traffic was blocked in all streets for over four hours while the singing, shouting paraders marched through the entire city.

The formal celebration of the birthday took place on the East Parade ground at noon yesterday with speakers addressing crowds totalling over 150,000 from five platforms. The speakers on the main platform included Mr. Ku Mang-yu and Mr. Sun Fo (Nationalist Ministers).

Simultaneously hundreds of speakers from specially erected platforms throughout the city addressed street crowds while the processions were constantly moving about the city.

Madame Sun Yat-sen spoke before an indoor meeting at Chungshan University on the character of her late husband.—Canton Bureau of Information through Reuter.

## VOICE FROM GRAVE

WOMAN WHO WAS BURIED

ALIVE

Pekin, October 28.—A traveller just arriving at Peking from Yungching, Chihli, states that in the village of Heiwu in that district a young lady about twenty years of age who had been ill for a number of days passed away and was buried at once.

Three days after her death, according to custom, her mother-in-law went to her tomb to perform sucrificial ceremony by burning paper money. To her great astonishment, she heard cries from inside the grave and a voice which seemed to say.

"Your daughter-in-law is alive, pray be not terrified."

Upon this, the mother-in-law at once returned home where she assembled a dozen of brave men, who took with them shovels and axles. On arriving at the grave they tore it down and found the buried woman alive. She was so weak that she could not move. She was carried home, and still lives.—"Chung Mei."
