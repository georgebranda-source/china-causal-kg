## CHINA'S TASK

## EARL BALFOUR'S VIEWS

## Helping

That China must work out her own salvation was one of the points made by Earl Balfour in stating the Home Government's policy in the House of Lords yesterday.

## CALUMNY CAMPAIGN

In the House of Lords, Lord Goford, asking the Government for a statement with regard to the situation in China, emphasised that every party and class in Britain would welcome the development of a strong, prosperous and united China. He paid a tribute to the devotion of the small British communities in China. Every Englishman in England or China deeply, deplored the suffering caused to the Chinese. He appealed for the prompt fulfilment of the Washington Conference obligations as a proof of good faith and goodwill. He held the opinion that "the Chinese would find means to appoint a Chinese delegation representing the whole country and fulfil their obligations.

Lord Gosford concluded by urging the appointment of a British statesman of world reputation as a special commissioner to China.

Earl Buitour, in reply, dwelt on China's lack of real central government, and the internal troubles which, he emphasised, were not due to any foreign action. The Chinese had been masters of their own house. No substantial portion of the responsibility for China's present unhappy position fell to foreign countries and certainly did not fall to any British Government.

Earl Balfour expressed the opinion that there was no clear policy for Britain or any country having relations with China which could directly go to the root of the evil. China alone could bring about that change in her political situation which would give promise of happier relations between China and the rest of the world. All we could do was to do our best to mitigate the situation.

Earl Balfour expressed indignation at the flood of absolutely unfounded calumny poured out upon Britain with regard to the industrial conditions of China, perhaps from ignorance or prejudice, but he feared also from deeper projects intended not to help the Chinese but to injure the British.

Earl Balfour emphasised that the Government desired a judicial public enquiry into the Shanghai trouble as soon as possible which would command the confidence, not merely of other countries, but of China herself. He hoped the confidence arising from the treaty of Washington would meet soon, and emphasised that the delay in this respect was not due to Britain, which ratified the treaty immediately after the conference.

Earl Balfour emphasised British sympathy and goodwill towards China. We would do everything we reasonably could to help the Chinese to help themselves, but China must work out her own salvation and effectively obtain a vigorous central government, which would secure the ordinary requisites of civilisation, before anything that the treaty Powers could do would really produce a great or permanent effect.

He also emphasised the difficulty caused by the chaos in China but hoped nevertheless that the conference would be fruitful of good results. It was impossible for us to delay carrying out the Washington obligations with a view to relieving or modifying old treaties in such a manner as if properly used they would be of infinite benefit to China and he hoped no small benefit to China's friends and allies.

## GOLD STANDARD

HOUSE OF COMMONS ATTACK.

CHANCELLOR'S DEFENCE.

(Reuter's Service.)

After a vigorous defence of the Gold Standard by Mr. Winston Churchill, Chancellor of the Exchequer, the House of Commons passed the reading of the Appropriation Bill.

Sir W. Lees Smith attacked the return to the Gold Standard as premature and blamed it for increasing unemployment. He complained that it would not decrease the internal to correspond with the increase in external prices. "Everybody knows that the Australian Government was told to raise money in New York and not here." Mr. Churchill said that no responsible party challenged the principle of the Gold Standard. "If we had not taken this action, the rest of the Empire would have taken it without us and there would have come the Gold Standard, not of the pound sterling, but of the dollar."

Among “solid remarkable factors” to be considered as consequences of the Gold Standard, Mr. Churchill, mentioned the capital issues for domestic purposes, for the first half of 1925 exceeded more than double similar issues for the first half of 1924. Sterling had recovered the parity of the gold dollar, and had established the equilibrium of the Australian and South African currencies.

The Bank of England's gold had increased from eight to nine millions. The general money rate had eased.

The general tendency of foreign countries towards stabilisation had increased, as for instance India, which could now consider the sterling rate whereat to stabilise the rupee. He added that coal subsidy was very objectionable but greatly preferable to veiled subsidy on exports.

## DISLOYAL STUDENTS

GOVERNMENT CONSIDERING FUTURE POLICY.

EDUCATION BOARD'S VIEWS.

STRONG MEASURES CONSIDERED

The statement was made by the Inspector of Schools in the course of a discussion yesterday at the Education Board meeting that the question of policy with regard to the return to schools in the Colony of striking students was already before the Government and the Board would be informed of its decision.

Mr. E. Ralph's presided in the absence of the Director of Education, Mr. G. N. Orme.

The tone of the meeting was that, having “miserably failed in the simple act of duty and loyalty to their schools and colleges,” strict measures should be taken to bring home to them their disloyalty and to prevent any possible future political demonstrations.

The steps advocated by Mr. B. Wylie, on whose resolution the matter was brought up for discussion, were that all the ringleaders should be expelled and that steps should be taken in order that all expelled students should be refused admittance to other Government schools, grant-in-aid schools, private schools and, for examination purposes, to our University; that the general body of strikers be not admitted for a definite period, say, until the commencement of next school year; that all scholarships in the possession of strikers be cancelled; that the Government will direct that future indulgence by students in political demonstrations shall be followed by instant dismissal.

There were some people who would deprecate any step other than the demanding from the students in question an explanation of their absence from their studies and the dropping of the matter after they had been given a good talking to. It must not be forgotten, however, that the Government, if it was to continue the part of educator must clearly lay down as part of its education that politics were not the concern of students but of their parents.

Mr. Wylie also spoke of the need for care being taken in the event of measures such as indicated by him being taken that private schools might not harbour expelled students.

## UNITY NEEDED

Mr. Wylie's resolution, calling for an indication of the Government's policy, was seconded by Mr. E. F. Aucott. Prior to this so-called "strike," he said, it was unthinkable that in a British Colony, where the Government at a cost which is admittedly very low, has continuously provided generous educational facilities for the benefit of all classes and in which the Chinese students have so largely shared, numbers of the latter, led by their seniors, should so miserably fail in the simple act of duty and loyalty to their schools and colleges.

Although it is true that the "strike" began in a Government College, the fact remains that there were "strikes" in practically all the schools of the Colony. There is, I fear, a tendency amongst the Headmasters of some of the non-Government schools to lay all the blame on the Government Institutions, even to the point of pretending that they have no strike ringleaders in their own schools. I trust, therefore, that when it comes to the point of meting out reasonable retribution to the strike leaders, the masters of all schools will co-operate heartily with the Government and will make no attempt to shelter behind the excuse that their particular schools have no agitators on the roll. The fact is that a strike cannot be engineered in any school without leaders.

In conclusion, Mr. Aucott spoke of the need either for a reduction in the number of private schools or the provision of means whereby they should be more closely supervised.

Also speaking in support of the resolution, Dr. S. W. T'so said the question occurred whether by giving the students this liberal education they were, in doing so, proceeding along the right lines. The old Chinese teaching was that boys were sent to school to study and to do nothing else. In the olden days there were no meetings in which they talked about freedom and much else that they did not understand, but they were kept to their studies and were under discipline. To day the youth of China talked about freedom and revolution. Dr. T'so thought that there must be something wrong with our educational system. There was something wanting, and he was of the opinion that some other methods should be adopted. The giving the students was a matter which, he considered, should be gone into thoroughly.



## STRONG RULE

With regard to the Government's policy to returning strikers, they must make a strong rule with regard to all the schools. He agreed that the punishment must be severe and that stringent rules, must be brought into force, and suggested that the matter should be left to the Government and the various heads of the schools to devise the best means in this respect.

A plea for sympathetic consideration of the students' case was put forward by Dr. Wan Man-kai who thought that the great majority had been misguided and misled under the guise of patriotism. No amount of talking and reasoning had been able to influence them and almost it seemed as if they had acted whilst under the disease of insanity which became infectious.

It could not be gainsaid that the Government had spent a lot of money on education and the students ought to feel grateful. He did think, however, that although they had been misled and intimidated, that they were grateful. If they went to extremes it might be disastrous for both parties. He urged, in conclusion, that the Government should be liberal-minded.

Dr. Wan's remark as to gratitude was at this point commented on by the Chairman, Mr. E. Ralphs, who pointed out that day after day before they left it was indicated clearly to the students that they were contemplating a false step which might have serious consequences. It could hardly be said that they had taken a very happy way of showing their gratitude.

The Chairman also stated that the whole question was before the Government and when a decision was reached it would be passed on to the Board.

The Rev. Dr. Pearce hoped that the Government would endeavour to emphasise the necessity of loyalty on the part of all students to their schools. They ought to try and create a better atmosphere as a safeguard when crisis of this kind arose. If such an ideal of loyalty could be incultated into the students, he thought it might be a factor which would help to get rid of the trouble in the future.

The resolution requesting a statement of policy from the Government on the question was then put to the meeting and carried unanimously.
