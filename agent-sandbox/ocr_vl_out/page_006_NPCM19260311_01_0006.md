## Hongkong, Thursday, March 11, 1926

## A BOMB OUTRAGE

It is a welcome sign of the times of the placid mood of the community that, beyond the usual feeling of horror associated with bomb butrages, there has not been the slightest concern as to the safety of the public generally as the outcome of yesterday afternoon's regrettable incident at Hunghom. The nerve of the Colony are not on edge. The strikers shot their bolt months ago so far as law and order are concerned. The machinations of the intimidators were short-lived. The raids on certain areas by the Police, with the co-operation of the East Surrey Regiment, succeeded in ridding the Colony of undesirables. The criminal records soon began to testify to the efficacy of the drastic measures enforced, even armed robbers finding for a time that the last place in South China to ply their nefarious calling was Hongkong.

With the relaxation of vigilance on the part of the pickets in Canton, it was inevitable that there should creep through the cordon and drift back to their old haunts some of the criminal classes—hence the occurrence now and again of an armed robbery and of a series of burglaries in city stores. But, knowing the desperate nature of such criminals, the only wonder is that outrages of this nature have not been more frequent. It is evidence in itself that the preventive work of the Police has not been permitted to slacken in the more demonstrative duty of protection of the public from crimes with or without violence.

To revert to the incident at Hunghom it is the easiest thing in the world to place it at the door of intimidators, but all the circumstances point to exactly the opposite; and only unimpeachable evidence secured eventually by the Police will convince anybody that the motive was not personal revenge for some real or imaginary wrong. The setting of the crime was too crude in the extreme for it to have been the work of anyone but blundering amateurs unaccustomed to be entrusted with the execution of political offences. The bomb itself, if such it could be called, was a "home-made" product, although powerful enough to cause a fair amount of damage to a crowd. The sole professional touch—if such it can be described of the whole business lay in the fact that the assailant had the will

to make good his escape, but even this is not a promising in view of the panic naturally created in the vicinity of the explosion. Nor would it be the first time by a long way that an amateur bomb thrower, as compared with a professional political malcontent, has succeeded in eluding the vigilance of the crowd and of the Police subsequently.

In short, the whole outrage can be ascribed purely and simply to malice or revenge, outside the realm of which it is not worth comment, beyond regret that a number of innocent persons were injured and congratulation that other persons, including Europeans, escaped from the effects of the explosion.

## DIOCESAN BOYS SCHOOL

Without much crashing of cymbals, the transference of the Diocesan Boys' School from the island to the mainland has been accomplished. If “this” are progress” is always going on, it is always going on in connection with institutions—and particularly with educational institutions. The old quarters of the Diocesan Boys' School had outworn their usefulness. The methods were new, but not so the buildings. And so this move has been made; and from cramped quarters with little or no outlook—not even the hackneyed fine view of the harbour—a number of very fortunate schoolboys find themselves in the midst of spaciousness where, indeed, every prospect pleases. The schoolboy of the future in this Colony—at least that section which decides to place itself in the disciplinary and educational care of the Diocesan School, will give thanks—if the modern schoolboy ever gives thanks—for the commendable foresight, and wonderful initiative which has produced the most modern block of school buildings in this part of the world. The move has been a wise one—and having made it, perhaps other institutions who gasp for lack of space will consider copying such an excellent example. For in Kowloon and beyond, there is ample space. The youthful vision is not clogged; the mind has a pleasant prospect before it and the chance of producing mainly and independent young men should be more easy of accomplishment than under the conditions which are more or more extensions, makes, or developments, are to be encouraged. ## NIGHT ATTACK

(From Our Own Correspondent.)

Following is a verbatim account of a daring river boat, robbery in which Rev. David H. Thomas of the American Presbyterian mission, and his family with three children were the victims in a trip from Kachek to Holhow.

At the warning of shots at midnight they waded ashore and spent the hours until daylight in the swamps of the rice fields losing all their possessions.

"On March 8 my party consisting of my wife and three children aged 6, 5 and 3 years together with a Chinese amah, a Chinese pastor, two-school teachers and several other Chinese, left the inland market of Kachek, en route to Hoihow.

"We arrived at the river late and so took a boat instead of a car to Holhow. After journeying three hours, the boatmen came to a stop near the bank of the river and suggested that it would be better to wait until morning before proceeding further. One or two of them then left the boat with a lantern, going to a nearby village. An hour later they returned with several other men who quietly boarded the boat at the stern and we were under way again. The other boats ahead of us were consequently out of reach. We proceeded slowly, running around every few minutes.

## Firing Begins:

"I fell asleep but was awakened by the boat grounding again, and then firing began. The boatmen were frantic and left the boat. The Chinese began to flee with small bundles. The Chinese pastor advised us to take our children and flee. Picking them up we jumped into the water, the firing getting closer, now from a small sandbar."

"We reached the bank when I attempted to return to get our baggage which contained all our clothes and family silver, when the amah with one of the boys fell headlong into the water, landing in the rice field. This fully awoke the child and his screams were anything but mild."

"I ran to his rescue and gave up returning to the boat, concentrating on getting my family away as fast as possible. We were lost in a perfect maze of rice fields and were constantly picking ourselves out of the water. We kept on until we came to a grassy spot near a branch of the river and there we hid for three hours.

"I lay down in a swampy place — it was all swamp—and took two of the children, endeavouring to keep them warm with the heat of my body. My wife had the other. Three other little Chinese children were with us, two very small, who had been abandoned by their father in his flight and they had followed us.

"During this time, we fought mosquitoes, wondered where we would go from the place, and how we would get to Holhow. We heard a hail which sounded friendly. This came from one of the preachers from a nearby village.

"It was getting light so I went back to the boat which had gone well over. The station treasurer's books and a few pieces of clothing (stained with red dye which had spilled out) were all that was left. We returned to the boat. The boatman also returned and we continued our journey until we reached the road where we waited without hat shoes food sleep and caked in mud. A car took us to Holhow in the early afternoon.

"We are thankful to have escaped a worse fate but are still wondering at the frame of mind of the father of the two children who followed us into the rice fields. When we took them back to the boat where he joined them, we merely heard a scolding for our thanks not scolding us but the children for following the foreign devils!"

"CINDERELIA" IN A NEW SETTING

BUDDING THESPIANS

"The Little Mummers" or Kowloon Children's Dramatic Society were introduced by Mrs. Gay Cumming to the public yesterday afternoon when a matinee performance was given in the Star Theatre, which was almost packed, the juvenile element of the community assembling in full force. Ventures of this kind are not always a success, particularly if the requisite time is not given for rehearsals, for the children chosen for the cast require as much, if not more than, the amount of training of grown-ups. The eye and ear of the captious critic are usually considered out of place in a performance for "sweet charity," whether the principals are juvenile or adult, although the occasion does arise where a frank opinion, given in a kindly spirit for the ultimate benefit of those concerned, is rarely if ever taken amiss. That the biggest room in the world is the room for improvement applies to the appearance of little tots on the stage as well as to seasoned adults who are professionals in their art.

This need not be taken as the prelude for any unnecessary comments on the production yesterday afternoon of "Cinderella" in a new and original setting. The main test was the appreciation of the audience. It was a children's play performed for the benefit of children. And the children who formed the largest proportion of the large audience spent a real happy two hours, and were only sorry when the curtain went down for the last time. If any adult grew were at all bored it could not be on account of the "performance" on its merits at such short notice, but probably owing to the lack of insufficient orchestral music in the pauses between the various scenes. This is a small detail, however, and can easily be remedied both at the evening performance to-morrow and on any other future occasion when "The Little Mummers" play as they did yesterday afternoon for the benefit of Dr. Barnardo's Homes.

It was a happy thought not to confine the programme to the production of "Cinderella." The quartette of pretty dances and the recitation all "brought down the house." The dancing right throughout, including the minuet in the ante-room to the Queen's ballroom, provided a tribute to the skill of the Misses Violet Capel and Daisy O'Keefe, by whose permission the young artistes appeared. It would not be a kindness to say that the minuet was perfect, but here again it was obvious that the short time permissible for rehearsals was alone responsible for any slight shortcomings discernible.

In "Cinderella," the juvenile dramatists excelled themselves, one or two in particular performing so well as to leave not the slightest doubt in the minds of the audience that the future of the Hongkong A.D.C. is assured with such a fertile field to draw upon with the passing of the years. The audience, both juvenile and grown-up, enjoyed it thoroughly, and the ranks of "The Little Mummers" should be greatly augmented as the result of the performance, which, as already stated, will be repeated in the Star Theatre to-morrow (Friday) evening.

## LONDON STRIKE

## UNIONS' RECOMMENDATION REJECTED

A lively mass meeting of the strikers at Messrs. Hoe's factory rejected the recommendation of the Executives of the Unions to return to work, and resolved not to resume work until the non-Unionists were removed and a substantial increase in wages assured.

The Executives of the Union have called a meeting to consider the taking of disciplinary action against the strikers.

As a result of a meeting of the members of the executives of the seven Unions concerned in the dispute at Hoe's factory and Sir Allan Smith, Chairman of the Employers' Federation, the employers have agreed to defer the operation of the lock-out notices until Thursday, March 18.
