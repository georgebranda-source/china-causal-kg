## SHAREHOLDERS' APATHY

Anyone who has had experience of company meetings either in Hongkong or elsewhere in the East cannot but be in full accord with the "disgust", expressed at yesterday's meeting of the Telephone Company in regard to the poor attendance of shareholders. Mr. D. McNeill, who aired the grievance, expressed surprise that both in that and other companies with which he is connected the shareholders take no apparent interest in their own financial affairs—they are content to read the reports in the newspapers and then proceed to "chew the rag" as to the best way of improving affairs. They are satisfied to argue, instead of attending meetings and putting their views before the directors.

The Hon. Mr. P. H. Holyoak made the only rejoinder possible. The directors sympathised with Mr. McNeill in his views and he felt sure that the directors of any company would welcome criticism which was fair, and which was constructive and not destructive. Directors would welcome helpful criticism in preference to having shareholders whispering behind their backs.

There is no suggestion, of course, that in regard to the Telephone Company in particular there has been any whisperings behind the backs of the directors. It was a mere generalisation in regard to the attitude of shareholders of any company toward those whom they appoint as directors of their own affairs. If the cap fits any individual shareholder of any company or any clique of shareholders then, of course, they are welcome to try it on. Still, it is not "cricket" in the commercial world. If directors are worthy of the position to which they are elected they are entitled to receive the co-operation of shareholders—to be told the good as well as the bad and to be the receptacle of that kind of criticism that is based on the true interests of that particular company in which they themselves have a pecuniary interest. Even destructive criticism, if advanced in the proper spirit, can often be useful to directors, and it is certainly not to be discounted so long as it is expressed at meetings of shareholders and not behind the backs of directors. Time and again in the history

a quorum at shareholders' meetings has outrivalled Japhet's famous search for a father: Even the anticipated announcement of a dividend has failed to secure the attendance of shareholders. But there is apparently no remedy for this state of affairs. Perhaps Mr. McNeill, who initiated the discussion on the point yesterday, would like to take a leaf out of the book of Trade Unions and impose a nominal fine on those shareholders who fail to attend duly convened meetings. Compulsory interest of this kind would not, however, be very helpful toward the proper conduct of the company's affairs; and, after all, if shareholders cannot voluntarily be induced to attend to watch their own interests, then not all the protests of surprise and disgust will do any good. And there is this to be said—so long as the affairs of the company are being administered as they ought to be, the absence of shareholders may often be construed as a graceful compliment to the directors. Conversely, let one word be whispered that all is not well, and shareholders' meetings will be packed. Which, again, has been a common experience both in Hongkong and elsewhere.

# ARMY NEEDS.

SEARCH FOR A MECHANICAL

PROMOTION PROBLEMS

(British Wireless Service.)

In introducing the Army Estimates for 1926-7 in, the House of Commons Sir Laming. Worthington Evans, Secretary of State for War, said that it had not been easy to secure a reduction of 32,000,000, which the Estimates showed, as compared with those for the current year. The total net amount he was asking for was £42,500,000 compared with £44,500,000 in the current year. He pointed out that in 1921 the Army expenditure was £88,000,000 or twice the amount of the Estimates now submitted. Even of the £42,500,000 which he asked for nearly £8,000,000 would go to retired pay and pensions.

## "Mechanicalisation"

The Minister said that the manoeuvres of last Autumn had taught important lessons in regard to the use of mechanical vehicles. It was shown that the necessary mobility could not be achieved if horse transport accompanied mechanically moved troops. Until, however, a satisfactory type of machine could be evolved, which also had commercial possibilities, the cost of mechanicalising large forces would prove prohibitive.

Research and experiment were continuing with a view to evolving something which could be of general, as well as military, use, and which commercial users would stock, so that it would be available in large numbers in case of need. The extent, therefore, to which the field Army could be mechanicalised still remained in doubt.

It had been decided to approach the problem step by step. In the first instance they would form a small mechanical force of all arms for experimental purposes, and they would not attempt any large production of mechanical vehicles until they were certain that they had evolved the required type. They were, he was advised, already ahead of other nations in mechanicalisation, and there was, therefore, no justification for uneconomical haste.

## Greatest Asset

Dealing with the promotion of officers, Sir L. Worthington Evans said that the Army Council was unanimously of the opinion that no change in the present system of normal promotion from Second Lieutenant to Major was either necessary or desirable, and that nothing should be done which would in any way impair the greatest asset which the British Army possessed, namely, regimental esprit de corps.

The Army Council had, however, decided that in the junior ranks officers of marked ability should receive accelerated promotion.

Promotion, above the rank of Major would now be by rigid selection, increasing in severity as the higher ranks were reached. Above the rank of Lieutenant Colonel the selection would become still more drastic. It was hoped that by this means any officer of outstanding and proved merit would get his chance to reach the highest rank in the service.

## SOCIAL PROGRESS

### (British Wireless Service.)

In his address of welcome to the delegates to the International Conference on hours of labour, Mr. Baldwin said:

"We are all of us advanced countries and we enjoy the benefits of a high degree of social progress. Uniformity among ourselves may have far-reaching consequences to the civilisation of the whole of Europe. There can be no question in Europe of any country attaining prosperity at the expense of another country. ## TRAIN DERAILED

## FIFTY FEET PLUNGE INTO RIVER

HEAVY DEATH ROLL.

(Reuter's American Service.)

According to advices from the railway owners, 178 persons were killed and 75 others injured in the derailment of an excursion train, near San Jose, Costa Rica.

Three coaches left the rails and plunged fifty feet from the embankment into the river.

New York, March 15.

The casualty roll in the Costa Rica accident is now stated to be 248 killed and 93 injured.

Three coaches were demolished; of which one fell into the river from the bridge, and the others hung from a precipice 190 feet high.

There were 1,000 passengers on the train. The object of the excursion was to raise funds for a charitable purpose.

Three days' public mourning has been ordered.
