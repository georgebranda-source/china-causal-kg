## SHARE MARKET

POST-BOYCOTT EFFECT

Although the Boycott and Strike were officially off on Sunday last our market has not responded in fact most of the favourite Industrials show a decline on last week with Sellers prevailing.

Shanghai Market is firm with a good demand for Cottons and Rubbers.

The following are the principal alterations since last week:—

Banks:—Declined to a selling rate of $1,140 at which price sales took place, to-day $1,150 would probably have to be paid to obtain shares.

Unions: — Carne to business at $295/6 and it is doubtful if there are buyers at over the former price.

Canton Insurance and Hong Kong Fires: — Are unchanged with buyers but no sellers.

Trams:—Have sellers at $26 and less would be accepted.

China Sugars:—Are farmer with buyers but no sellers at $24, China: Providers:—Are still wanted, buyers offering $6.10. China Lights:—After a few transactions at $21 for the combined shares are now quoted nominal at $21%.

Hong Kong Lands: —Some business was put through at $643/65, and there are still buyers at the latter price.

Humphreys: — Rose to a buying rate of $16^{th}$ without attracting sellers and they close nominal at this price.

Cements: After sales at $14½ for the combined shares have experienced a decline, $133% having been accepted with buyers now only offering $13.

Dairy Farms: — After a few sales at $19½/19½ have no further buyers at the moment.

Electrics: -- Were more or less neglected and after a few sales at $67½ close nominal at this price.

London Quotations: — Have undergone no change.

## Military Parasites

There is excellent coal within 20 miles of Peking at the Menketou mines, but the military have taken all the cars and locomotives, and no reserves have been laid in. Camel transport, or transports by

Coal at the Mentekou mine costs $3 a ton. In Peking, 20 miles away. It costs $18 a ton. A year ago, with the same mine price, Peking paid only $7 a ton.

The militarists tax all goods hauled by rail $4 per ton, and also get a "victory tax" of $25 per car, and an additional tax of $500 for every freight "train of 15 cars." This explains some of the increase in price.

Even many rich people have no coal on hand, with which to heat their homes, and already the mercury is down nearly to 32 every night.

The authorities are making a determined "drive against the "Reds" both here and in Tientsin. Many Cantonese have landed at the latter port, and though scores have been arrested many have managed to reach Peking. "Yesterday, more than 30 of these were put into prison, and it is said that some of them found with incriminating literature will be decapitated. Many arrests have been made also from the personnel of the Peking University.

## Sneaking to Canton

An official order has been put out forbidding students here from leaving for universities at Canton. Especially since the Cantonese captured Hankow, Canton propagandists have been busy among the students, and many have sneaked away for Canton. They go disguised as coolies, or as soldiers.

Heads of all schools have been instructed to search dormitories and libraries for Red literature, and the police are destroying all such reading matter that is found.

Chang Tso-lin, from Mukden, has issued an order forbidding the Fengtien troops now controlling Peking from interfering in any way with the civil administration, and from oppressing the merchants or collecting arbitrary taxes.

The military has also shown its goodwill by issuing an order to the effect that hereafter all soldiers must pay fares on the trams. Last week the street railway represented to the government that because the soldiers rode free it was about to go into bankruptcy.

To-day "Young" Chang announced that he was moving his troops out as fast as possible. There are now about 24,000 troops in and immediately around Peking. He promises that within a week only one regiment will be in Peking, and one at the Temple of Heaven. The rest will be south-bound to aid Wu Pei-fu.

Home papers to hand are full of news from Hong Kong and China. At last, this Colony is getting the prominence it should, not through any fault of our own. But do the folks at Home comprehend the purport that is intended?

Here is one of the mysteries put to millions of readers of a London daily when the Wanhäsen rescue occupied pride of place:—“General Lieut. So-lung went over to the enemy.”

Evidence is not needed that narratives from the spot are welcome. Such news interests your friend. You can send no more welcome gift than the “Overland China Mail,” the weekly edition of all the local and Chinese news published daily, whether relating to Chinese or foreigners. It is the link with Hong Kong and compact enough to save a dozen letters.

But about this “General Lieut.” So little is known in the Old Country that names of places, generals, factions, organisations, &c., are sadly mutilated or misrepresented. The man referred to was Liu Tso-lung, who betrayed Wu Pei-fu and gave the Hanyang Arsenal to Canton.

The “China Mail” takes great pains in supplying footnotes and explanations to all messages which baffle the non-Chinese. A week's collection of these—in the “Overland”—make a handy reference for use here or abroad. Get a copy immediately!
