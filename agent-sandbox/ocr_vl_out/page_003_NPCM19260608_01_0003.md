HONGKONG AS GUESTS AT SINGAPORE.

## DICK HANCOCK'S SPEECH

Victors and vanquished gathered together at the Europe Hotel on Saturday (May 29) evening, when the Hongkong and Malaya cricket teams were entertained to dinner by the Singapore Cricket Club. The President, Mr. R. J. Farrer, occupied the chair and on his right was His Excellency the Governor, Sir L'hurence Guillemard, those also present including the G.O.C., His Excellency Major-General Sir Theodore Fraser, the Chief Justice, Sir William Murison, the Colonial Secretary, Mr. Hayes Marriott, the Attorney-General, Mr. M. H. Whitley, Mr. G. Hemmant, the captains and members of the Hongkong and Malaya teams and a large number of supporters. Mr. R. H. B. Hancock, the visitors' captain, was seated on the left of the chairman and Mr. N. J. Foster, Malaya's skipper, on the right of His Excellency the Governor.

The toast of The King was honoured on the call of His Excellency the Governor, and the chairman, in a humorous speech, proposed the health of the Hongkong Cricket team. Mr. Farrer thought it was a rather pitible thing that a man should be a wonderful "silly-point" and able to bat, yet could not bowl. By that he meant that Mr. Foster had refused to make the speech proposing the toast of the visitors. (Laughter).

The function was festive in character and marked by "good humour and geniality, and "the brief speeches were admirably worded, the occasion, in every way, proving a pronounced success.

## "Cricket a Funny Game"

His Excellency was deeply disappointed that, owing to pressure of work, he had not been able to extend to the Hongkong team the measure of hospitality he would have liked, but he hoped that the visitors would grace the proceedings at the King's Birthday Ball at Government House on June 3. (Applause). This visit of Hongkong was a tardy one, but Malaya was all the more pleased to welcome them on this occasion, and he hoped that the interval would not be so long before the next visit of the Hongkong team. Cricket was a funny game, and they knew that the light conditions in Singapore were not what they were expected to be, but the Hongkong team were full of good-will in order to play the game, win or lose, without any ranking. He noticed in the afternoon paper that the Canton boycott had been lifted and it might have been that this news reached the ears of that Hongkong team, by some means or other, before—it had reached their own Singapore. (Laughter).

In conclusion the chairman expressed the hope that the Hongkong team in future would come to Malaya much sooner and oftener. (Applause).

## Value of Interport Contests

The toast was received with great enthusiasm and Mr. Hancock on rising to reply, was greeted with much cheering. The company might think it funny, stated Mr. Hancock, that he should have pleasure in replying for the Hongkong team after their performance that day, but he had two very good reasons. The first was that they were honoured that evening by the presence of His Excellency the Governor—and a brother Carthusian. (Applause). ## THE GENIUS OF MILAN

## TOSCANINI FEARED AND ADORED

Milan bousts that it maintains the best opera in Europa, and Milan is probably right.

First the house—the historic Scala. It is huge—half as big again as Covent Garden—and, with its six tiers and its great prosecnium arch, truly majestic.

It is no building for scanty audiences or modicro performances. In such a space the dismalness of the second-rate would be unendurable. But audiences at the Scala are nowadays never scanty. The house is always full. Although a stall costs about £1 (or on special occasions a good den more), it is not easy to get a seat. The box-office exhibits the sign "esaurito" (exhausted—i.e., sold out), and you must go to the traders who haunt the portico.

Every passing traveller wants to see a performance at the Scala. There is good reason. The Scala under Toscanini, is first-rate.

I heard there the other night, "Vordi's "Trovatore"—an open_ut which some too clever folk may sniff," I am a convinced Verdian, but I never before realised what a fiery masterpiece, beautiful and thrilling, that "Trovatore" is. But in England none but the elderly have seen the "Trovatore" in first-rate performance, and never has anyone seen there such a performance of the work as Tocanini.

Toscanini is a conductor of genius. He is more like Nikisch than anyone else living.

What an experience, it was to witness his unfurling of the splendid old score! On such terms there is a brave future for early Verdi. Toscanini is a dictator, a tyrant here. Opera at the Scala proves that tyranny (if the power is in the right hands) is the best regime for opera.

He must be both feared and adored. The spring and zest in the orchestra told us that. The singers included a superb soprano, Claudin Muzio: as baritone our robust, ardent Covent Garden friend, Benvenuto Franci, a rather less excellent tenor, Pertile; and as the gipsy a fine dramatic contralto with an uneven scale, Elvira Casazza.

Everything on the stage was as handsome as handsome could be. Infinite pains had been spent on the preparation. But the crowning gift was Toscanini's. He it was who seized and brought to light the essential poetry, hot and palpitating, of the old masterpiece.—Richard Caffell, in the "Daily Mail."

## COOLIE KILLED BY SAILOR

Nanking, May 29.—A dispute over a ricsha fare led to struggles between negro sailors of a ship belonging to the International Export Company here and Chinese ricsha coolies at 9 o'clock yesterday afternoon at Hsia Kwan, which resulted in a ricsha coolie being shot dead. The negroes were on their way to their ship from the Chinese City when the trouble occurred.

There were at first rumours that the Chinese were would probably spread anti-British handbills on the ground of the sailors being of British nationality, and that the Chinese workers of the International Export Company would go on strike. But due to the strict precautions taken by the Chinese authorities, there has so far arisen no disturbance of any kind. With the May 30 incident directly ahead, the population here are feeling very uneasy.

## POLICE ACCUSED OF ERISES

Four members of the police force of Caloocan are under investigation for alleged dereliction in duty and acceptance of bribes, says the “Manila Bulletin” of May 31. Charges to this effect were filed recently by a citizen of Caloocan.

## SNAKE YARNS

### EQUIVALENT TO THE ENGLISH "TALL STORY."

Snake yarns are not necessarily about snakes. The term is merely the African equivalent of the English "tall story."

True, there are plenty of genuine snake yarns, says Captain Ian McLaren, I remember once, when on a shooting expedition in the Transvail, meeting three Australians whose hobby it was to catch poisonous snakes by the back of the neck. They then waggled a glass container in front of the reptile, rousing it to fury and causing it to bite again and again, leaving each time a drop of poison on the glass. This poison commands a high price among chemicals, and the Australians made a tidy sum out of their hobby.

That is a story about snakes; here, however, is a "snake yarn" in the true—or perhaps I had better say "untrue"—sense of the term

It is told of an old Dutchman who lived in Pietersburg. It was his delight to waylay shooting parties in the bar or on the verandaf of their hotel, and regale them with marvellous tales of the hunting exploits of his youth. His aim, of course, was liquid refreshment.

He spoke in a mixture of South African Dutch, Kaffr, and English, and his most famous tale-translated—ran like this:—

"Lions in the Bushveld? Let me tell you. Once I was camped near by Leydscorp. I had a splendid wagon in those days, drawn by eight span [pair] of donkeys. I was sitting by the fire when I noticed great eyes gazing at me out of the darkness. I looked around; there was a ring of eyes surrounding me—lions, thousands of them. I got my rifle; I fired again and again at those eyes. Lion after lion fell dead; I counted eighty-seven. But others took their places and the ring drew closer and closer. My ammunition was running short, so I decided we had better go. In the pitch darkness we had great trouble inspanning [harnessing] the donkeys, whovere plunging and rearing with terror.

"I shot a lane through the herds of lions and we set off. Never have I known donkeys go so fast. Three miles an hour is their usual speed; these were doing thirty! Like racehorses they were, and the great wagon bounded along like a fire-engine.

"Hour after hour they kept up this break-neck speed," and I could not understand it at all, for the lions had not followed us and the donkeys' terror should have subsided long ere this. I was more frightened clinging to that swaying wagon than I had been among the lions.

"Then, at last, the dawn came and I saw the reason for it all."

"Would you believe it, gentle-men?" here the old rascal struck an attitude," would you believe it? I'd got two of those lions inpanned with the donkeys!"

## A SELF-MADE SHAH

## CAREER CULMINATES IN CORONATION

Six feet in height, 50 years of age, with strong features and a rather severe expression, Reza Shah, whose dramatic career culminated in his coronation at Teheran, is an imposing figure. Used to command, he is primarily a man of action and, unlike his predecessors, he has shown that he will not be content to look on and lead a life of luxury. A postal motor-lorry was recently robbed by brigands near Kerman-shah. The Shah hurried to the Ministry of War early in the morning, ordered the Minister to proceed immediately to the scene, and dismissed the general commanding the troops in that district for allowing such a thing to happen. The brigands were caught and dealt with, and the looted postal matter was recovered.

Born of humble parents, Reza has spent practically his whole life in the Army, which he joined at the age of 12. He passed through all the ranks of the Cossack Brigade, until 1921, when, as a colonel at the head of some 3,000 troops, he marched on Teheran from Kazvin and effected his memorable coup d'état, his first aim being the re-organisation of the army as a barrier against the threatened Bolshevik incursion.

Reza's first open rupture with the deposed Shah occurred when the latter was proved to be implicated in a plot against his life at the end of 1928. The Prime Minister was exiled and the Shah himself went on a protracted tour of Europe from which he never returned.

In the following year the present Shah, then Prime Minister, realising that it was hopeless to expect the country to make any real progress under an absent monarch who was Shah in name only, started his ill-advised move in favour of a Republic, no doubt encouraged and influenced by Mustapha Kemal's success. This move was frustrated at the last moment by the hostile demonstrations of the people, egged on by the clergy. But last October Parliament unanimously voted the deposition of the Shah and appointed Reza Khan Pahlavi head of the Provisional Government.

A few weeks later the Assembly met and the Crown was bestowed on Reza Shah, who thus succeeded in emulating the first Napoleon by rising to a throne from the same humble beginning.—“Daily Mail.”

### TWO CHIVALROUS CHINESE. DEEDS THAT MAKE FOR FRIENDSHIP

The "North-China Daily News" of June 1, writes:—

The chivalrous behaviour of two unknown Chinese during yesterday's rioting, by saving small foreign children from the hands of a mob, as described by a foreign gentleman in a letter to this paper, makes very pleasant reading. He writes:—

## BANDIT-SQUIRE OF CORSICA

## HILLSIDE DEATH FIGHT WITH POLICE

Romanetti, a Corsican bandit, who for 20 years has kept to the hills and eluded capture, fell in an ambuscade laid for him by the police.

He had been twice condemned to death in his absence for murder, but the peasantry refused to betray him. He was one of the most important personages in Corsica and used to attend to his private affairs and visit market towns whenever he chose.

He was actually shot down while on his way to visit a wine cellar which he was having built as he had just bought one of the largest vineyards in Corsica.

Every morning he rode down from his hillside refuge to inspect his vineyards. Recently, police were posted on the roadside. Early in the morning Romanetti, mounted on a pony and preceded by a mastiff, rode down the road. When the dog-barked, warning him of the police, he unslung his carbine and opened fire.

## Hit Several Times

The police returned his shots, Romanetti was hit several times, but, throwing his carbine down, he pulled out a big Mauser pistol and carried on the fight until he fell from his saddle. When the police came up they had first to shoot his dog, which refused to allow them to approach, and they then found that the bandit was dead. He had a belt of ammuntion on him and was wearing grey corduroys and a soft felt hat.

Romanetti was one of the most picturesque of the Corsican bandits. He was kind to the poor and he had a keen sense of honour. When two of his guards were found guilty of bullying peasants in his name and thieving from them, he gave them summary judgment and shot them out of hand.

He also killed two police officials who tried to arrest him, and so orders had been given "for him" to be shot at sight.
