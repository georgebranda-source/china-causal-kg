Conflicting evidence was given yesterday at the Magistracy in the case in which Mr. L. G. Taylor, manager, and Mr. J. L. Durand, part proprietor, are charged with disorderly conduct. The case was adjourned until Monday afternoon.

CONFLICTING VERSIONS.

Mrs. Taylor, describing the events of July 14, when the incidents on which the charges are based are alleged to have taken place, said that Madame Flint told her she was no good and that she wasn't wanted there. Witness demanded a formal letter of dismissal from Mr. Durand and he promised to give her one. Refering to the alleged attack by Mr. Durand on herself, witness said that Mr. Durand abused her and kicked her in the stomach. It was whilst Mr. Durand was upstairs drafting a letter of dismissal for herself and her husband that her husband came in. She acquainted him with what had transpired and he went upstairs to Mr. Durand.

Shortly after, Mr. Durand came rushing down the stairs, continued witness, and grabbed hold of her. His hands were bleeding and witness said the dress she had put in as an exhibit in the case showed the marks.

Questioned about the wine she had that night, Mrs. Taylor said she and her two companions had one bottle of champagne at dinner. She then went to the cinema, and on returning had another bottle of wine. Five people were present when that bottle was used.

Witness denied in cross-examination that she had boasted of having sold $200 of champagne on the night in question and complained that this fact didn't seem sufficiently appreciated.

## STORM DAMAGE

ESTIMATED AT MILLION

DOLLARS.

YESTERDAY'S STATEMENT.

Although it was impossible to give exact figures at the moment of the damage the recent flood had caused in the Colony, said the Hon. Mr. W. T. Southorn; Colonial Secretary, at yesterday's meeting of the Finance Committee; it was anticipated that it would be in the neighbourhood of a million dollars.

As authority was needed for proceeding with the work of restoration it was proposed that a vote of $600,000 be passed at that meeting. A formal vote would be before the Committee at its next meeting when more accurate details would be available.

The tentative figures were.—Hongkong $510,000, Kowloon and New Kowloon $60,000 and New Territories $30,000, a total of six lakhs; but he was afraid they would not get off with that figure as it only represented what the P.W.D. thought they would want up to December 31.

The vote was approved.

All the votes, particulars of which were given yesterday, were approved. A mistake in the official figures was responsible for repair work on the Police launch No. 4 (which went aground) being given as $1,700. It was explained that the figure should be $17,000.

With regard to a vote of $75,000 for reclamation of Quarry Bay, Marine Lot No. 4, due to more expense being incurred than was anticipated in the construction of foundations to certain parts of the sea wall protecting the reclamation, the Hon. Mr. Creasy said the original contract was made prior to 1923 and the whole contract was considerably changed owing to an exchange with Messrs. Butterfield and Swire. It was connected with the 100 foot road at that point.

The Government took over from Messrs. Butterfield and Swire a row of buildings and in exchange they were to reclaim a part of Quarry Bay and put up a retaining wall to protect the reclamation. The work was still in progress. The original contract was a small one and the contractor failed in 1923 and when the Netherlands company arrived they took over the quarry from the contractor and used it for their block yard.

The Chairman: Do you think it will be completed for the $176,000?

The Hon. Mr. Creasy replied in the affirmative and went on to explain that the recent storms had damaged the wall to some extent.

## NEW WARSHIPS

## EIGHT SPECIALIST OFFICERS APPOINTED

The principal British men-of-war, due to be completed during the current financial year are the battleships Nelson and Rodney, and in anticipation of their entry into service, about eight specialist officers have now been selected. These include for the Nelson Lieut.-Commander G. B. Middleton, who in 1923-25 was in the Hawkins, and Fleet Torpedo Officer for the China Station.

Submarine L.9, which has been paid off at Hongkong and placed on the disposal list for sale, is one of the 31 bouts of a similar type built under the emergency war programme of 1917-22.

These submarines, which are to be gradually eliminated from our underwater service, are improved or enlarged "E's" and generally follow the lines of that class; but, in addition to an increased number of torpedo tubes—four 21-inch bow tubes—they carry a 4-inch gun. The L.9 was built at Denny's, avian equipped with two sets of Diesel engines of 2,400 h.p., giving a surface speed of 17.5 knots, while the electrically driven machinery of 1,600 h.p. gave a submerged speed of 10.5 knots.

The L type gave the best result; of any submarine during the war period, their displacement of 1,070 tons making them more comfortable as sea boats than their pre-accessor.

## JUST PAINT

DODGE TO EVADE CHINESE AND MALAY PIRATES.

As the centuries slip by the part played by paint in a vessel's appearance steadily diminishes. The Tudor warships were absolutely dazzling in their paint and gilt work and the humbler merchantmen were made as festive as stern commercialism would allow. Even in recent years it was customary to go to some trouble and expense to paint on the sides of a ship a white streak with squares of black to represent gun ports, which gave them, as instance the "Loch Torridon," a very handsome appearance. One reason why this originally was done is said to have been that they appeared at a distance as men-of-war and therefore were let alone by Chinese and Malay pirates.

But to-day the warship has been made as invisible as possible, and economy has resulted in much the same standardisation for the Mercantile Marine, the funnels being the only remaining space where the colour artist is given

There are two well-known exceptions to the prevalent black hulls, namely, the Aberdeen White Star and the Union-Castle. The former still cling to the sea green, similar to what their famous clipper Thermopylae was painted, while the Union-Castle ships are easily recognisable by their slate coloured hulls.

Most companies favour white for deckwork, but the Peninsular and Oriental and the Nippon Yusen Kaisha have buff, which colour is selected as it is more durable and considerably less tiresome to the eyes under the intense tropical glare.

## STRIKES

## FORTY IN SHANGHAI LAST MONTH

There were forty strikes during June, which is the highest number ever recorded in a month in normal times. A desire on the part of the workers affected to show their sympathy with other members of their trade who were on strike accounted for thirteen of these disputes, economic discontent was the pretext put forward for thirteen more of them, and various forms of internal friction produced the remaining fourteen. The employees affected comprised 23,138 cotton mill workers, 23,920 silk flature operatives, 2,910 hemp sack factory employees, 1,150 Chinese and foreign-style tailors, 600 leather goods workers, 560 tobacco factory employees, 300 engineers and mechanics, 200 transport workers and 528 members of miscellaneous other trades. The strikes resulted in a loss of 71,233 days labour to co-creams in the Settlement, and 97,850 days to establishments in Chinese territory. Considerable loss also resulted from sabotage of which three cases occurred in the Settlement. The disputes furthermore involved a great amount of extra work for the Municipal Police who, in all cases, were obliged to make special arrangements for the maintenance of law and order.

It was remarked that the strikes in the Settlement occurred in areas close to Chinese territory where the vast majority of the workers affected have their homes.
