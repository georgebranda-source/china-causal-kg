## AFTER 36 YEARS

THROUGH PLAGUE AND TYPHOON.

MN. J. KERR, A.S.P., RETIRES:

Thirty-six years' residence in any part of the globe, even with a stable community, would of necessity entail the witnessing of many changes, but to few places would this apply with such force as to Hongkong where the development has been so comparatively quick and Remarkable.

Mr. J. Kerr, Assistant Superintendent of Police, who retires at the end of this month with that amount of service here to his credit, will leave a very different Hongkong and Kowloon behind him than those in which he first set foot in 1890 when he arrived as a member of the Hongkong Police Force.

This naturally applies to Kowloon more particularly, which for some time after Mr. Kerr arrived, was practically all hills with a road as far as Austin Road. From here to any other spot in that direction one had to clamber over rocks and boulders. Kimberly Road led one then to the waterfront only. The New Territories were not then ceded, and Mr. Kerr remembers the bamboo border fence stretching from Kowloon City to Shamshuipo with Chinese "braves" on the other side attired in picturesque garb, their coats bearing Chinese characters. Their duties were connected with the Customs. The main ferry ran in those days from a point somewhere in the neighbourhood of the new main office buildings of the Wharf and Godown Company and there being no Salisbury Road in those days, loose boulders extended about as far as the, now, railway station.

One of Mr. Kerr's earliest and most trying experience was connected with the first outbreak of plague in Hongkong when deaths were taking place at the rate of 112 a day with some 3,000 in all. Public servants, the military and civilians combined in combating this terrible scourge and times were doubly trying for the police, for there was a certain amount of unrest. The plague was so virulent in its deadly effects that numbers were dying almost immediately on, admittance to hospitals and ignorance and superstition associated with foreign "devilry," the use of ice to bring down the temperatures of those affected being cited and there being much talk of open rebellion and violence. "Tung Wah hospital officials were, in fact, mobbed."

Mr. Kerr has still in his possession a medal awarded for services at this time. On one side of it are a patient on a stretcher, a nurse and doctor warding off the angel of death, a bucket of whitewash and brush and on the other side are Queen Victoria's head and an inscription. The whitewashing reference is to the method of stamping out the disease—the burning of all belongings of infected persons and the whitewashing of their premises.

Work of superintendence in connection with the removal of the dead in the typhoons of 1900 and 1906 also fell to Mr. Kerr's lot. The typhoon of 1900 was on November 10, the latest in the year one is known to have taken place here, and many craft were caught. The havoc wrought in that of 1906 was terrible, some 10,000 people, it is thought having lost their lives inside of two hours.

Mr Kerr's rise in the force has been steady; his appointment as Sergeant was in 1899, 3rd class Inspector in 1908, 2nd class in 1906 and 1st class in 1910. In 1915 Mr Kerr was appointed Chief Inspector and in 1922 he was appointed to the rank at which he is retiring.

Many years of active life should await Mr. Kerr who by no stretch of the word could be termed old although, "old" in the service of Hongkong. He intends settling down in Australia, his Home with Mrs. Kerr and his son. He had always been a "home bird." He confessed to a "China Mail" represals at various. ## THREE MONTHS' JAIL

A Chinese was charged before Mr. R. A. D. Forrest at the Central Magistracy this morning with the theft of $2 from H. Lesser, seaman of the U.S.S. "Helena," at Queen's Road Central on Saturday evening.

The complainant stated in evidence that he was walking near the Queen's Theatre in company with two shipmates when the defendant bumped into him and ran away with two dollar notes he had in the jacket pocket. He gave chase and caught the man, who threw the money on the road.

Mr. H. A. P. Meik, chief officer on a local boat, correlated a part of the complainant's evidence.

In spite of defendant's protests that he was innocent and a victim of mistaken identity, the Magistrate held him guilty and sentenced him to three months' hard labour.

## TRADE LOAN

AN OPEN LETTER TO THE COMMITTEE.

THE CHINESE VIEWPOINT

Gentlemen, I offer no apology for addressing you anonymously through the medium of the Press on the subject of the Trade Loan for this subject vitally concerns the public, and I feel that you will be interested to hear about the hopes and expectations of the Chinese regarding this matter about their anxieties and disappetments. But I do offer you my assurance that I am not being activated by any ulterior motive that I have no "axe to grind," and that, personally, I am in no way interested in the Loan.

Now, what was the financial situation of the Colony prior to the announcement of the loan? It is, I think, no exaggeration to say that it was grave. The Banks—doubtless for good reasons, which I do not profess to know—refused to lend one money, irrespective of the amount or quality of the securities offered. Creditors were pressing debtors for repayment. The vitality of credit was gone. Cash appeared to have completely vanished from the market. The Chinese were driven to appeal to the Government for assistance. A Chinese Delegation, headed by the Chinese members on the Legislative Council, waived on the Government, and the whole outlook was exhaustively and impressively stated by their spokesman. Thanks to his masterly presentation of the case, and to the sympathy of Sir Edward Stubbs, the Government was, within the space of only a few days, able to announce that the Home Government would at once place at the disposal of the Colonial Government a sum of $23,000,000, which was the figure suggested by the

The effect of this announcement was marvellous and instantaneous. The whole tone of the financial situation at once improved. Faith in the Colony immediately revived. All hoped that with $3,000,000 available to be lent on good securities, of which there were ample, the nightmare of enforced sales and wholesale insolvencies would exist no more. Surely the money would be for the coming within one week? Two weeks? Three weeks? One month?

Then as the weeks passed and still not one dollar came out from the loan to relieve the financial pension, people began to get uneasy, anxious and even in spedulous. As usual rumour, succeeded rumour. The Government would lend only to finance, business " not to ward off depreciative and rulorous sales. In any case, the ridiculous margins demanded by the Government would render the loan commercially useless. Indeed, was the money really available at all?

And so, as the rumours increased, the situation gradually drifted back to the old days of despondence and financial stagnation.
