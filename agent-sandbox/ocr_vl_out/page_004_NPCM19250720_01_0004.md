PLUCK AND HEROISM.

When the official history of the rainstorm disaster of Friday comes to be written the tragic and pathetic circumstances of the calamity, which has evoked the utmost concern and sympathy of the whole Colony, non-Chinese as well as Chinese, cannot fail to be relieved by the unadorned tale of pluck and heroism of the Europeans who worked incessantly at the grim and risky work of rescue. The average Briton knows neither fear nor danger when confronted with the relief of suffering humanity. The greater the risk the more pronounced is his daring. There is not the slightest thought of making of himself a hero. All that is in his mind is the effort to save life.



That trait has been brought out to the utmost degree in connection with Friday's disaster. A casual onlooker at the work of rescue on Friday and Saturday could not but be visibly moved at the superhuman manner in which the noble band of rescuers persevered in what might have seemed a forlorn hope. To many it might have appeared a sheer waste of energy—at least after Friday evening—to endeavour to rescue any more of the unfortunate victims alive. But the old adage that "where there is life there is hope" was never more fitly illustrated.

## Enquiry Needed

A Coroner's inquest will no doubt be held to enquire into the deaths caused by the collapse at Po Hing Fong. The pen of criticism must therefore be held until such times as the inquest is held and a verdict given. But it is permissible to suggest that at the close of the enquiry there should be instituted the most searching investigation as to the building of retaining walls and the oversight exercised in general building matters by the Public Works Department. Why, for instance, should a number of shopkeepers be put to worry, inconvenience, and the possibility of loss, each time there is an excessive fall of rain? Queen's Road was raised several inches, making certain shop fronts very much below level. No effort has been made to prevent water entering shops and doing damage to stocks. It is admitted on all hands that the excuse offered for the almost continual flooding of Happy Valley and the danger occasioned to the villagers of Wong Nei Cheong, are, in the main weak, unbusiness-like, and unworthy of experts. It has been alleged that there is no cohesion between the departments comprising the P.W.D. To assure public confidence there should be a commission of enquiry, and the same holds good regarding the building and supervision of retaining walls.

## An Arctic Epic

The world paid more attention to the return of Amundsen and his party from the Arctic regions than to the European security pact and the ferment in this part of the world. The knowledge that the explorers had returned to life out of the very maw of death stirred the imagination of mankind. Just as all the world loves a lover, so all the world loves a hero, because heroism is something apart from considerations of gain, a passage upon heights which all men see and most yearn to conquer. Some realise their vision. For nearly all the vision is lost in the fog of the commonplace until some deed blows away the mist and reveals for one brief hour a shining and a splendour. Amundsen and his men have reminded us of the constant glory. But they have brought back with them more than a message to the soul and spirit of men. Denied the Pole, they observed, we now learn, much of scientific value. From their aerial heights the explorers recorded extensive masses of mist in the polar regions hitherto believed to have been local phenomena only. Ocean soundings, too, established the existence of a wide ocean between Spitzbergen, the North Pole and North Greenland, "The expedition has observed a total of 160,000 square kilometres of hitherto unexplored areas," states Amundsen. That is the terse summary of the expedition's achievements. Further positive and negative results will be made manifest; no doubt, when the observations are thoroughly sifted. More details of the epic struggle with the ice, when the party seemed "caught like rats in a trap," will be awaited eagerly. For the present the tale stands in its broad outlines, the record of a magnificent failure.

## EASTERN EXTENSION TELEGRAPH

Presiding at the annual meeting of this company, held at Electra House, E.C., the chairman (Sir John Denison-Pender, G.B.E., K.C.M.G.) said that he was sure the shareholders would agree with him that the result of their working had been satisfactory.

From a comparison of this, with the previous year's message and other receipts, there was an increase of over £77,000. This was attributable to a slight, but very slight, improvement in Far Eastern and Australian trade conditions noticeable in the latter part of 1924, but he was afraid they would have to wait for some time yet before these conditions really showed any material improvement.

Although he was not at all pessimistic as regarded the current year, it was too early to forecast the effect of the reduction of tariffs to Australasia from 3s. to 2s. 6d., as that took effect only from December 1, 1924. Although they could not look forward as in former years to a rapid increase in the volume of traffic to counterbalance the loss of revenue caused by this reduction of rate, as to day the public enjoyed other classes of traffic at reduced rates, such as deferred and daily letter telegrams, which they did not in former years, the board considered it necessary, having the future in view, and perhaps more particularly to provide as far as was possible against interruptions of their main route to Australia—two of which occurred in 1924 through faults in the Perth-Cocos cable—to duplicate this section, and they had placed a provisional order with the Telegraph Construction and Maintenance Co. for manufacturing and laying a cable of the new "loaded" type.

Turning to the expenditure side, while there was a slight increase in working expenses at stations, almost wholly due to annual increments granted to staff, there was an actual decrease in expenditure of £66,000. This was mainly accounted for by the reduced amount allocated to the maintenance ships' reserve fund. Taxation, also, payable in England was lowered by £21,300. With the recent reduction of income-tax this item should show a further decrease in their next report.

The balance they had to deal with was £846,896, plus the carry-forward from 1923 of £412,244, making a total of £1,259,140. From this sum, £450,000 had been placed to, the general reserve fund, against £250,000 for 1923, but they must not overlook the £100,000 they used in that year to pay for their new ship, therefore, the actual addition to reserve was really only £100,000 more than for 1923; £400,000 paid in dividends; and £409,140 had been carried forward.

Thanks to the good offices of the Netherlands Government, the licence to land a duplicate cable between Singapore and Batavia was granted by the Netherlands Indian Government on November 25, 1924, and that the cable was immediately laid and opened for traffic on January 29 last. Their new repairing steamer, The Cable, was taken over from the builders on October 20, 1924. Leaving London soon afterwards, she arrived at Singapore on December 1, and had been in commission at that station since December 12. Her machinery had worked from the first without a hitch, she had already carried out several cable operations, and she had in all respects proved herself a most efficient and useful vessel. Their new offices at Singapore had been completed at a net cost of £91,108.

He regretted to state that negotiations conducted throughout the past year with the Chinese Telegraph Administration by the Great Northern Telegraph Co. and themselves had not come to any satisfactory conclusions. There was, however, no good, now some prospect of an agreement being reached before long.

When he last addressed the shareholders he referred to the financial conditions ruling in China. These showed no signs of improvement, but as it was expected that the Treaty between the Powers, dated Washington, February 6, 1922, relating to the Chinese Customs Tariff, would shortly be ratified, there was a hope that China's finances would be placed on a satisfactory basis. A combined meeting of the Chinese Chamber of Commerce and representatives of Chinese commercial organisations, labour guilds, and industrial organisations was held on Saturday afternoon at the premises of the Chinese Chamber of Commerce in Connaught Road "for the purpose of discussing a settlement of the present situation." There was a very large and representative attendance, which was presided over by Mr. Li Yau-chun, who was supported by the leaders of commerce in the Chinese community.

The proceedings were naturally conducted in Chinese, but it was evident that the majority attended with a real desire to do something to end the present position of stalemate and thus effect a change for the better in the trade of the Colony with which is interwoven the success and prosperity of loyal Chinese who have a large stake here and are determined to do their utmost to bring about a return to normality as quickly as possible.

The discussion was of a protracted nature, various points being mentioned as likely to ease the present position. It was stated by one gentleman that the mechanics at Taikoo, Kowloon Dock and the Naval Yard had already returned to work, and there were indications that others would return as soon as employment could be found for them.

It was ultimately decided, it is understood, to ask the support of the members of the Chamber of Commerce and of masters and men belonging to the various local/guilds for the recently formed Chinese Trading Trade Maintenance Association, of which Mr. Ho Sai-kwong is chairman. The latter body will in turn seek the co-operation of the Chinese members of the Legislative Council in influencing the Government to extend its protection to any measures that may be adopted with a view to carrying on the business life of the Colony independent of the strikers in Canton.

The opinion was naturally expressed that the meeting had no power or influence in the direction of actually ending the strike—this resting not with the loyal Chinese in Hongkong but with Canton—but it was hoped that by doing all possible to return to normal conditions as speedily as possible, a powerful moral effect would be exercised over Canton.
