## FIGHT FOR RUBBER

In America the anti-British rubber faction has not been getting its own way. With typical frankness American agitators, including Mr. Herbert Hoover, Secretary of Commerce, himself, have been told by their own compatriots that, if the control of rubber cultivation outside American soil savours of commercial morality, Americans are not guiltless by controlling the production of other products when deemed necessary. Moreover, whilst rubber manufacturers are growing at the alleged effect of control on prices—which must surely hit the rubber industry in Britain as well—a timely exposure is made by American politicians and writers of the mammoth profits being made by American motor and tyre companies last year.

That the outlook for the plantation rubber industry is of the brightest cannot be denied. There is, or will be, a demand for all the rubber that can be grown both within and without the British Possessions. Without in any way impinging on British preserves there are ample acres for cultivation in other parts of the globe by Americans and all others who look with pardonable envy on the growth of the rubber planting industry in Malaya, Ceylon, Burma, and India.

One fact stands out in the rubber controversy in America, and that is the remarkable lack of enterprise on the part of usually enterprising Americans in not awakening to the vital importance of rubber cultivation years ago. The pioneers from America in this field can be counted on one hand, those from Britain, would require a whole volume to record their names. Hence the unrivalled position of the British to day.

The more the demand grows for a thorough investigation into the profits made in America from rubber, the greater will be seen the hollowness and the sham of developing British rubber control. The dream of the public rubber cultivation has been outlawed by British Operations.

## THE MAKING OF WORDS

Despite the million-odd entries in the latest dictionary, there are not nearly enough words to allow us to say what we mean. In a certain way speech does for verbal expression what the tempered scale does for musical expression. Though G flat and F sharp are etenally different, the tempered scale arbitrarily sets up something which is neither and declares it to be both. So, when you thump the first of the three black keys on the piano, you thump a vicious compromise that says a thing no sensitive musician really means. A complaint shopper, you accept not what you demand, but something alleged to be "just as good."

A sort of word was christened by Lewis Carroll the "portmanteau." In his famous introduction to that pioneer of portmanteau poems, "Jabberwocky," the bard explains that this characteristic effect arises when its maker's mind is attuned with such equal intensity to the expression of two related ideas that they fuse and issue from his mouth as one. For example, if you equally want to admonish the charwoman not to crinkle up your manuscript and not to squash it, you tell her not to "squishle" it.

Thus are obtained delicate hilt-tones and between shades of meaning, which correspond to the results of a painter's mixing primary colours on his palette, or a composer's blanding of various orchestral instruments.
