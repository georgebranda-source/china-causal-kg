Hongkong, Saturday, May 1, 1926.

Of many a dull and uninteresting week locally of late the current one will be hard to beat. Its record can be written down in small type and small compass. If we take our pleasures seriously assuredly do we not indulge over much in mirth when a wet spell is too much protracted. The only really outstanding event of the week has been a conference of business men with His Excellency the Governor regarc.ng an effort to bring the Volunteer Corps up to the maximum of efficiency. This by no means insinuates that the Corps has been of inferior quality in the immediate past. In fact the services it rendered to the community since the critical days of the outbreak of the strike up to the date of demobilisation earned for it permanent gratitude. What it has done before it will be found able and ready to do again. The local branch of the Navy League has held its annual meeting, when a membership of 120 was reported and the finances disclosed to be better even than those of a year ago. Still, a membership of 120 is very poor. There ought to be at least 400 members. The Portuguese Consul-General, Senhor Cerveira de Albuquerque, has been congratulated on the elevation by his Government to the rank of a first-class Consul-General. The relations between the Consul-General and the Government and people of Hongkong have ever been of the happiest, and we re-echo the wish that he will be long spared to fulfill his Consular duties in this Colony. In the world of sport it was hoped to have an exhibition of tennis during the brief visit to Hongkong of the Philippines. Davis Cup team, but unfortunately, the weather ruled it out of the question. The Kowloon Football Club are to be congratulated on bringing off a "double" by winning both the Shield and the

League championship—a new record in local football. Details of the scheme of modern flats on the Peak were reviewed at the first annual meeting of shareholders of the Rural Land Investment Company. Unfortunately much of the work was delayed by rain whilst development on three of the lots has been deferred until local conditions show some sign of improvement.

At Home the most absorbing topic has naturally been the coal crisis and the certainty now of the pits closing down owing to the failure of the owners and the miners to agree on the report of the Royal Commission. The Prime Minister has been exercising himself to the utmost in the interests of industrial peace, but the miners have rejected the owners' amended offer and the strike began at midnight yesterday. The London engineers have decided to strike for an extra £1 a week wages, so that the "merry month of May" is likely to be a misnomer in this year of grace. The features of Mr. Winston Churchill's Budget speech, which he delivered in the House of Commons on Monday, were that there would have been a profit this year but for the Coal Subsidy, that a new betting tax will be introduced, that a considerable sum of money from motor taxes will be diverted from the Road Fund to the general funds of the exchequer, and that the Trade Facilities Act, excepting where it safeguards "key" industries, will be repealed. The greatest controversy has arisen in regard to the betting tax, which has been assailed both by bookmakers and church folk, whilst an authority like Lord Lonsdale is of opinion that the revenue to be secured will not be worth the trouble of collecting it. The Labourites in the House of Commons received an accession to their strength in the person of Miss Susan Lawrence, who won East Ham North-by-election and fills the place of a Conservative.

In India racial feuds have again been a disquieting feature of the daily cables, but drastic measures have been put into operation, including raids on roughs, and these have done much to clear the air. The Governor of Bengal, Lord Lytton, has appeared on the scene and interviewed the leaders of the Moslem and Hindu communities, with it is to be hoped, good results.

In the world of aviation, the Danish airmen should by now be in Peking. The two Spanish aviators were last reported to be at Hanol. It is now doubtful whether Hongkong or even Macao will be touched at. It all depends on weather conditions on the China coast.

Affairs in North China have not changed greatly during the week, and the only real "tit-bit" is that the notorious "Christian General is speeding to Moscow as fast as the train can carry him. The position in Cartor is still a stajernate, with however onlimism the keynote in this Colony

CLUB'S PROGRESS

ENGINEERS INSTITUTE

ANNUAL MEETING

OFFICERS ELECTED

Mr. J. Ormiston, the incoming President of the Club, presided at the annual meeting of the Institution of Engineers and Shipbuilders, held at the Club premises in King's Building yesterday.

After the annual report, already published in the "China Mail," had been read, the Chairman expressed regret at the small attendance.

The question of the distribution of funds to certain charities was raised, and Mr. Ormiston said he was quite sure that the members were of the opinion that the usual donations should be continued.

The meeting voted the usual contributions to charities. These were:

British Legion, £50; St. Dustan's £50; London Hospital £20; Glasgow Royal Infirmary £20; Dr. Barnardo's £20; Orphan Homes of Scotland £10; Shaftesbury Home and "Arethusa" Training Ship £10; Royal National Lifeboat Inst. £10; Seamen's Hospital £10; and Hongkong Seamen's Institute $500.

## President's Address

The president, Mr. B. L. Frost, said,—I think you will agree with me that, taking the adverse circumstances into consideration, caused by the strike and boycott, we can consider this our thirty-fifth annual report a highly satisfactory one. Our profits for the years' working only amount to $4,084.69 against nearly $8,000 in 1924.

Curiously enough the first and last quarters of the year were the worst and the third quarter, when we were going through the worst of the strike, was the best one.

Our membership stands at about the same number as in 1924, but I regret to say that we have lost. By death no fewer than 14 members. Our sympathies go out to the friends who have been left to mourn their loss.

Our worthy friend and Hon. Librarian, Mr. Stokes, took a well earned holiday during the year and I am afraid that members must have noticed a difference in the working of the library during his absence. I am pleased to say that he has returned to the post with renewed energies and when the alterations are completed and he has the library and reading room arranged to his and, I am sure, every other member's satisfaction, there will be few places of its kind which will be better than ours.

It has been a pleasure to see that the interest in billiards has increased and that the competitions are so keenly contested. I am sorry that the prizes are not ready for distribution this evening.

As retiring president and as a member who is leaving the Colony after nearly 22 years and as a member of this institution for some 14 years, it is a great pleasure to me to know that the Institution has at last expanded and that as soon as the alterations are completed will be the second social club in the Colony, in size, but first in popularity.

This, I think, is due to the fact that we have no cliques and that the committee extends the hand of fellowship to whatever stranger may happen to become a member of the Institution.

I wish the Institution all success and trust that with the extension given a trial, and with the co-operation of all the members to make the new venture a huge success, that at the next annual general meeting you may be able to revert to the old rate of subscription.

Proposing, the election of Mr. J. Ormiston as president, Mr. Frost said he had taken a keen interest in the welfare and work of the Institution, and he was quite sure that they could have no one more capable of carrying out the onerous duties of president. Mr. Ormiston thanked the members for the honour, and said he had taken a keen interest in the Institution since 1909. He referred to the offices he had held since that time, and said he would do his utmost to further the interests of the Institution and the members.

Mr. Frost, proposing the election of Messrs. L. J. Blackburn and E. W. Blackmore, as vice-presidents, said that the members who were there during the strike knew that these two gentlemen helped them to carry on. He did not know where they would have been; were it not for their assistance?

THE GREAT PYRAMID

SUBJECT OF UNIVERSITY LECTURE

BIBLE REFERENCE



The Great Pyramid, one of the group known as the nine pyramids of Glach and situated ten miles to the west of the modern city of Glach on the edge of the Saharn, was the subject of a lunar illustrated lecture at the University last night by Mr. D. O. de Silva. Over a hundred coloured slides were shown.

The lecturer said that although it was certain that the pyramid was by far the oldest building in the world, it was not so old as 5,000 or 6,000 years, as was sometimes claimed. Professor C. Piazzi Smith, Astronomer Royal for Scotland, had demonstrated that the building contained within itself the evidence which established the date of its erection as 2,140 B.C. The Great Pyramid was therefore a little over 4,000 years old.

The�ur, that far from being designed by Cheops, the reigning Pharaoh of the time Cheops and his subjects were compelled to engage in the work, the purpose of which they did not understand, by a mysterious people named Hykscs, or Shepherd Kings who invaded Egypt from the East, subjected the people and in addition to closing their idolatrous temples compelled them to build the pyramid for a certain purpose, leaving it when they evacuated as an object of reverential wonder, an inscrutable mystery for forty long centuries. The ancient Sphinx, which lies close to the Great Pyramid, is sometimes depicted, said the lecturer, to appear to be almost as huge as the Great Pyramid, but this was due to a false impression of perspective. Brief reference was made to the absence of authentic knowledge about the Sphinx and the attempt which had been made at much expense to find out if chambers were contained inside by piercing its immense bulk with a boring rod.

Although the people of Egypt, imagining the Great Pyramid to be built as a tomb, constructed others which were used for this purpose, the lecturer claimed that the designer of it was inspired by higher motives than the construction of a last resting place of a dead monarch. The lecturer claimed that the motive behind its construction was that it should symbolise the Christian life and the way of salvation, a theory first voiced by a young Scotsman of Leith, Robert Monzies. Each passage and chamber had its own symbolical meaning, claimed the lecturer; it could be likened to a Bible in stone. Biblical reference to the Pyramid was also claimed by the lecturer, references in the book of Job being given.

The passage ways of the Great Pyramid symbolised the various coyenants of the Divine Plan, stated the lecturer, illustrating his analogy by means of slides and description. The whole of the time features of the Bible were corroborated by the Pyramid-inch measurements and the correspondence were so exact that he, with others who had made a study of it, were convinced that they were “intended by the Divine Architect to confirm their faith in the times and seasons of the Scriptures.” The Pyramid measurements corroborated the Bible chronology and the fact that “this monument foretold by means of its symbolism and time measurements that the autumn of the year 1914 would witness the beginning of the great time of trouble now in progress, should prove of absorbing interest to all.”

The Great Pyramid, stated the lecturer, was God's sign and witness to the Christian now and to the world in the coming Age. The Great Pyramid was truly a "miracle in stone."

CONSTABLES DEATI

ENQUIRY INTO KING'S PAR MYSTERY.

FORMAL EVIDENCE



Further evidence was given yesterday as to the death of a Weihaiwei constable who was found in King's Park with wounds in this throat, Mr. J. H. B. Nihill holding an enquiry at the Kowloon Magistracy.

The Coroner sat with a jury, comprising Messrs. J. T. Humphreys (foreman), I. F. da Rocha and Chung Ying-chiu.

Dr. T. Smalley said that the wounds in the dead constable's neck were exceedingly serious; while there were also wounds on the little finger of the right hand. There were no wounds to suggest that deceased had struggled before his death. The wound in the neck reached right down the vertebrae column, severing all the vessels and structures on its way. The whole wound was composed of seven separate incisions, which ran into each other. The cause of death was an incised wound of the throat and haemorrhage.

In answer to the Coroner, witness said the wound could not have been self-inflicted. The man had been dead for about 36 hours when witness saw him.

Detective Sergeant Kennedy gave evidence of finding the body after receiving information as to its location. He said that he found it lying in a hollow of the ground near a rock.

Further evidence was given by a Chinese married woman who stated the deceased and another man had visited her from time to time, and that on one occasion she had heard them conversing together.

At this juncture the Coroner adjourned the inquiry, which it is expected will last for some time, as there are a considerable number of witnesses to give evidence.
