## ABNORMAL FANATICS

One feels convinced after a few weeks in Russia that only a small minority of the Communists believe in anything but their own power and advancement, writes a correspondent of the "Daily Telegraph." Many of the leaders are, no doubt, genuine Communists. Of the sincerity of Chicherin, for instance, there can be no doubt: he refused a great inheritance in his early years for the sake of his principles, and at that fateful meeting in London, where Lenin split the Socialists into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks, he voted with the former. But not one, looking into his pale eyes with that strange glitter in them, could regard him as normal, Dzerhinsky, too, produces the impression of a cold-blooded fanatic. "You cannot make revolutions in white kid gloves," he answered a more squeamish acquaintance, and the same spirit has carried him through ruthless seas of blood as through fifteen years in a dungeon for the sake of his convictions.

The “screwed-up” expression of the great Bolsheviks, especially of Lenin and Trotsky, has often been commented on, and the galleries of portraits with which Moscow abounds show this phenomenon to be characteristic. Rykoff is almost the only prominent Communist who has the face of an ordinary human being. For this very reason, perhaps, and because of his moderation, his influence is small, and since he gave the Third International some very sound advice on the folly of persecuting the nephew, or private traders, out of existence, he has made himself disliked by the extremists. It would seem that the screwed-up face and glinty eyes are symptoms of abnormality, and no real Bolshevik leader can be regarded as quite normal. Lenin was certainly a great man, of tremendous driving power; yet in the official bulletin of his autopsy it was announced that, instead of grey matter in his brain, they found green slime.

SWARMS OF SPIES.

The extremists now in power have taken the bit between their teeth, and miss the controlling master-hand. None of the moderate men appear to be strong enough to check them, and they are capable of overriding any reasonable arrangement made with a, foreign country by the Government on the grounds of political expediency. Yet the officials with whom foreigners come into contact seem not only reasonable, but civil and courteous; their hands, however, are so entwined in the meshes of an endless tangle of red tape that business is as tedious as it is difficult. Yet this very red tape is necessary as a check, for they cannot trust one another; all are thinking of lining their nests against a rainy day, and survivors of pre-Communistic days are always on the look-out for a chance to recover, regardless of means, some of their lost property.

In Russia the individual dares very little. Spies swarm everywhere, and the more prominent and more responsible the man, the more his actions are watched and recorded. The leaders spy upon each other, and the powerful "Ogpu" spies on everybody. Foreigners are watched, and the inquisitive spoon-fed, so that it is very difficult to form a genuine opinion. In the hotels thecomings and goings are registered, as well as interesting conversations. As a rule this is done without attracting attention, but occasionally a clumsy fellow, when eavesdropping, will betray himself. Natives dare not visit foreigners, and if foreigners visit Russian friends it is certain to bring suspicion upon them. The postal censorship is an old Russian institution, and it is certain that the Bolsheviks have continued this legacy of the Tsars, with many other of the worst features of the old regime, and it is unwise to write from abroad to friends in Russia.

WORSE THAN TSARIST TYRANNY. But the tyranny of the Tsars was liberty, indeed, compared with the tyranny of the Bolsheviks. Then at least one was allowed to live and trade and work, so long as politics were not touched. Russian broadmindedness, the child of their boundless plains, allowed greater freedom of action than does public opinion in Britain. To-day everyone lives in constant dread. Many people never go to bed before two a.m., so as to be dressed when the agents come, for that is their favourite hour. It is characteristic of Russia that the old saying, when a chance silence falls upon a party, it is not that an angel is passing over, but "a policeman has been born."

Intolerance is the keynote of the Communist system, which might be defined as the very anti-podes of the policy of "live and let live." They loathe Democracy, for the Dictatorship of the Proletariat is incompatible with the greatest good of the greatest number, and means exclusive power in the hands of the minority—another characteristic in common with the old régime. They openly treat as enemies all who are not members of the party; they make use of them so far as it suits their purpose, but once they have ceased to be useful they are better dead.

It is told that in 1923 Moscow was a gay city, but in 1924 the swing to the left and the economic distress made themselves felt. Seats at the Bolshoi Theatre, opera, and ballet cost too much. There are few people in Moscow to-day who can afford ten, or even fifteen, gold roubles for stalls, which are patronised chiefly by an occasional member of some foreign mission, a concession-hunter from capitalistic Europe, or nephew who has not yet received the usual short notice of expulsion. Yet, as during the terror in the French Revolution we read that the theatres were crammed, so to-day during the terror which few see, but everybody feels in the air in Russia, the harassed and nervous public flocks to the theatre and the cinemas to forget for a moment their anxieties and fears.

Still, the Russian temperament shows itself often enough in laughing faces in the streets; yet this laugh has a hollow ring about it; it is the spirit of "let us eat, drink, and be merry, for to-morrow we die," that spirit which is truer of the Russian than of any other nationality in Europe, for everybody lives from hand to mouth, nothing knowing and little caring what to-morrow may bring forth. The only man I saw in Moscow who seemed genuinely happy had no legs. Both had been amputated above the knee. He came stumping cheerfully into a grocer's shop, with a healthy glow and a cheerful smile on his bronzed and bearded face. I instinctively drew out a few kopecks, when to my surprise he fished out of a capacious pocket a huge pile of small change, both paper and that not unpleasing "hard valuta" now in circulation, which was evidently his day's takings. He changed it for five and ten rouble notes, for he found begging a lucrative business, he cracked jokes with the salesmen, laughed uproariously, and after a vigorous discussion about vintages, bought a bottle of "port-vin" for three roubles, stuffed it into his pocket, and stumped out into the street, whistling a cheerful air.

## FORLORN CHILDREN

If the other beggars in Moscow were as merry, it would be a cheerful city, indeed, for scarcely even on the Galata Bridge at Stamboul can one see so many maimed and distorted beggars as in the capital of Bolshevism; and not all are cripples; ragged boys are a perfect nuisance, and it is impossible to buy a packet of cigarettes at a kiosk or come out of a restaurant without being persecuted by a batch of these important young ruffians. I was surprised at this phenomenon, and made inquiries, as I thought that under the proletarian regime all the poor were provided for. I was told that many found it a more paying game than working, while others could not produce the indispensable "dokument" which is so dear to the Russian official. Another characteristic feature is afforded by the swarms of forlorn children. The years of social disturbance, mortality among parents, and general life.

moralisation, especially among the youth of both sexes, have produced a big crop of parentless children, who live God knows how, begging a bit, stealing a bit, sleeping huddled up together among ruined and uncompleted buildings. To their credit, the Soviet authorities are endeavouring to cope with this evil, and among the very numerous appeals to one's purse when sitting in cafés and restaurants the one to which one gives with least reluctance is the fund for these poor waifs.

The concentration of the government in Moscow has led to a shortage of lodgings which is probably more acute than in any other city in Europe. Directly the frost breaks building proceeds vigorously. One huge skeleton structure near the Arbat was condemned as unsafe in the Tsar's days, and a lengthy lawsuit ensued. Now the Bolsheviks regardless of the architects' verdict, are hurrying on its completion with all possible speed. The only comment I heard was: "Thank God, only Communists will live there, so it will be a good thing if it does collapse." Rooms are almost impossible to secure, and few are worth having. A limited definite space called "ploshchad" is allotted to every person and heavily taxed. No body is permitted to stay in the city unless he can give a good reason, and many, a family has been deported at a few hours' notice to remote villages in the Arctic Circle. Few flats or lodgings remind one of civilised Europe. The "paradni," or front entrance, is invariably closed, bolted, and barred. No one can afford to keep a dvornik, or hall porter, and all use the "black" entrance, or back door. When calling upon an acquaintance it is necessary to wander about a courtyard, usually immense and always untidy, to find the tradesmen's entrance, which admits to a dark and gloomy kitchen, and then through dingy passages and corridors littered with boxes, stacks of old furniture, broken statues, and musty pictures, all recking and never ventilated, to the single room that is all that most families can boast it. This is usually over-furnished, and always depressing; an old rug may conceal a bed and make it look like a sofa, but a washstand, especially a Russian one, is never an elegant object. ## POSITION OF WOMEN

The Municipal Soviet exercises a fatherly supervision over its citizens; as in the old days, it is forbidden to enter or leave a tram when in motion, and the fine of a gold rouble for so doing is enforced; penalties, too are exacted for throwing about those long cardboard cigarette ends with which Russians are apt to strew the country, and big receptacles, labelled "dlia okurok," are placed in all public buildings and in the streets. But the latter are defaced by the shrieking propaganda, which gets on one's nerves until they are dulled; the effect produced by this incessant appeal to the eye is one of insincerity.

A rather surprisingly puritanical feature is the "struggle with prostitution," for which an active propaganda is maintained, visible in great posters. This seems odd, when they have tried to abolish Christianity, and Soviet marriage is a mere formula of registration; as easy and inexpensive to undo as to enter. But woman's position in Soviet Russia is in no way inferior to man's; she serves upon the General Staff of the Red army, and the tripe-woman of Marseilles has found her modern counterpart in the female executioners of the Che-Ka. The Communist women endeavour to distinguish themselves from their bourgeoisie sisters by eccentricities of dress or affection of masculinity, which completely destroys their attractiveness to the eye.
