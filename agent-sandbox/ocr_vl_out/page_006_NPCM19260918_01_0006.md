A SATURDAY CAUSERIE.

Although information in official circles early in the week was to the effect that it was anticipated that the two British-owned steamers forcibly detained at Wanhsien by General Yang Sen would be handed over in the course of a day or two, nothing definite has so far transpired. There has been considerable speculation with regard to the object of what Reuter termed "the second naval expedition to Wanhsien" consisting of British gunboats and a naval detachment of about 70 men but the only possible conclusion to be drawn is that the British Government has realised the grave danger to which mercantile vessels would be liable if incidents of this kind were to be allowed to take place with impunity. There is no logical reason why the gunboats should be needed for any other purpose than to act as escort to the vessels when released but if General Yang Sen hesitates to fulfil his promise to hand over the vessels at Ichang, where he is reported to have sent a commission to negotiate with General Liu Tsō-lung, the presence of naval forces of considerable strength will strengthen the hands of those who are conducting the negotiations for their release.

There is no legal ground whatever for the detention of the British ships and, so far as can be gathered, the only excuse offered is an allegation that cer-

tain. Chinese boats were "deliberately" run into. In view of the fact that the boats "whilst proceeding with the peaceful and legitimate object of conveying merchandise were surrounded by sampans containing Chinese troops endeavouring to get on board it would have been rather extraordinary if the vessels had managed to avoid them all in spite of the fact that they slowed down especially for this purpose. It is to be hoped that General Yang will not persist in an attitude which can have no other sequel than a repetition of an incident the terrible nature of which it has only been possible to appreciate since the arrival yesterday of eye witnesses' accounts of events at Wanhsen on September 5. The report that the Wanhsen "military are holding three British missionaries captives as a reprisal" is not a hopeful augury.

The need for a greater appreciation at Home of the critical situation developing in China was the subject of a cable which the local branch of the Navy League despatched Home this week. Whilst an efficient Volunteer Naval Reserve in Hong Kong could release from service in these waters sufficient regular navul vessels to make up the deficiency which has hitherto existed in the China waters, such an organisation could assist greatly by carrying out patrols in local waters. A cable was despatched from Hong Kong this week to enquire into the latest position with regard to the scheme which was sent. Home for approval and setting out the lines along which it was proposed that the Reserve should be worked in Hong Kong. The delay, it appears, is due to the financial considerations involved but provision is likely to be made for it in next year's estimates and by the beginning of the year a scheme will be in working operation. The Admiralty is understood to have indicated willingness to provide small gunboats or sloops for the use of such a Reserve.

## Colony's Walks

A significance other than what is conveyed to the European mind appears to the average Chinese resident in his conception of places like the Botanical Gardens. Medical practitioners, when handling cases of the poorer class of native, often prescribe an early morning stroll to the Gardens. This breath of fresh air is easily obtainable and there are many who live in crowded areas and have suffered accordingly. Careful observation has shown that each morning there are regular visitors to the different spots in these Gardens. Some are in the throes of consumption and go, fully believing that some mysterious benefit will accrue from patience. There are a few who use secluded parts as a rendezvous for a daily discussion, followed by a leisurely stroll back for "chow." Even a walk through these beautiful gardens is a luxury to a number. True, there is no admittance fee. But in the endeavour to make a living, a sufferer may have to forego the pleasure to make up for time. Of the Europeans who visit the Gardens, there must be only a small number of nature lovers. Children of the upper and mid-levels

taken there by amahs can hardly come within this category. Years ago when life in Hong Kong had fewer amenities, there may have been more 'non-Chinese adults' who went up for the view. Even amateur photographers seem to shirk the place, feeling that the studios have ample supplies on hand, thereby making the job not worth while. Until the advent of motor buses, brisk walks along Bowen Road were not disfavored. Then of course, there were the strenuous climbs up hill and down dale on both sides of the harbour which enthusiastic walkers have described for the benefit of others.

## "End" of Boycott?

Says the "Times" of Ceylon" of August 31, in an editorial:—

While the China hostilities are in progress the Hong Kong Government is engaged in negotiations at Canton for the termination of the boycott on foreign imports. The deputation from Hong Kong has not, however, to deal with the Red Government. The real power at Canton is not that Government, but General Chang Kai-shek's, who controls the "so-called "serikers." The boycott has led to the formation of a huge vested interest which is battening on the proceeds of the levies on imported goods. General Chang Kai-shek has an armed force of 3,000 men. [Does this refer to his Army or the pickets?] He is quite independent of the Canton Government, and it is he who will have to be bought off if the boycott is to be liftea, but how a system of spollation which has become the sole means of subsistence of thousands of people and supports a large number of officials in luxury can be brought to an end it is very "difficult to see.

Albert Guetat, proprietor of the Albert Cafe, was sued by his landlord in respect of rent for his dwelling house and counterclaimed in respect of gas fittings for which the landlord was liable under an agreement and which he (defendant) had caused to be installed himself, was again before His Honour Mr. Justice Wood in the Summary Court yesterday.

Mr. M. K. Lo, on behalf of the landlord, applied for a re-scission of the previous decision under which His Honour had found for the defendant on claim and counter claim. Mr. Lo said that in his judgment His Honour had been unable to find as a fact whether or not the defendant had paid, the sum of $210. If this was so then the counterclaim should have been dismissed. The whole Basis of the counter claim was that this money had been paid.

Mr. L. D. Turner, for the defendant, said that the plaintiff's own witness had said that he gave the receipt as a discharge for the rent and plaintiff was therefore stopped from denying that the receipt was a discharge.

His Honour reserved his decision, saying that if he found there was a stopper then he thought his judgment as given should stand.

## NATURAL RESOURCES MUST BE GUARDID

HE "WORLD."

. New York City, September 10.— An editorial in this morning's New York "World" map the opposing of independence for the Philippines on the ground that the natural resources of the Islands must be protected from the hands of foreign countries.

"It is particularly unfair for the correspondents with the Thompson mission," says the editorial, "to declare that Philippine coal and iron are an additional reason for not granting independence since Japan urgently needs these commodities. This statement has been duly filed from Colonel Thompson's ship, yet nobody knows how much iron and coal the Philippines have, or how valuable the deposits are."

The World concludes: "Under the four power treaty arrangement, suitably extended, the Philippines can be protected by that pact which the United States thinks real enough first to have proposed it and then to have signed it."

### Eager To Know P. I

Ashland, Oregon, September 1, West coast Americans are eager to know all the truth about the Philippines, the development and educational progress there, according to N. M. Rambs, who is travelling through the Northwest and writing his experiences for the Independent Philippines News of Salinas.

Mr. Ramos on his arrival here was invited to give a number of talks before the public bodies and was visited at his hotel by towns-people eager to obtain special information about the Islands.

This has been his experiences in most of the places he has visited and he believes that well-informed Filipinos should be sent out by the insular government to reveal the Philippines in America.

Mr. Ramos believes that plain talks, giving facts that American people want, are fully as valuable as more formal propaganda. This tour is primarily for pleasure but Ramos has been compelled by general request to make it something of a preaching tour.—"United Press."

## SHINGLED HAIR

## TABOO IN ITALIAN COURT

Rome, Sept. 1.—Bobbed, or shingled hair is to be taboo at all functions of the forthcoming Italian court which will open in the autumn. Queen Helen has set her face against the prevailing fashion and in matters of intimate court etiquette her word is law.

Queen Helen is an honorary patron of the Catholic Women's league, which has been one of the most vigorous opponents of the present day feminine fashions of bare arms and abbreviated shirts.

During the Lenten season, when Rome was dancing gaily night after night through the season of penance, Queen Helen forbade all balls and dances at court.

The ban on bobbed and shingled hair will doubtless cause some heart-burning in feminine bosoms, for the number of the bobbed and shingled in Rome is legion.—"United Press"

The 45th annual meeting of Hong Kong St. Andrew's Society is to be held at the City Hall, on September 24, at 6 p.m.

The balance at the credit of the Society now stands at "H.K. $1,125.88 to which must be added the sum of Straits $6,700 invested in 5% per cent. Straits War Loan and £500 five per cent. War Loan 1929-47.

The Annual Ball was to have been held in the City Hall on November 27, St. Andrew's Day being a Monday, but owing to the death of M.M. Queen Alexandra it was postponed until February 26.

A Robert Burns Birthday dinner was held on January 23, at the Hong Kong Hotel.

The sum of $1,046.50 was expended during the year in assisting distressed Scotsmen.

The "J. R. M. Smith" Scholarship Was won by Master John Laing of the Central British School.

Your Committee deeply regret to record the deaths of Messrs. W. "Murray.Scott, D.K. Blair, J. H. Kynoch, T. A. Barclay, A. Findlay-Smith, Sir James Cantlie, Dr. Dalmahoy. Allan and W. Shewan.

#### LATE MRS. LAMMERT. FUNERAL TO TAKE PLACE TO-DAY

The late Mrs. Lammert, wife of Mr. George P. Lammert, Senior, whose death we reported yesterday, had lived in Hong Kong for the greater part of her life, (she was born here) and although failing health had prevented her from taking an active part in charitable and social undertakings she was well known to a large circle of acquaintances by whom she was held in great esteem.

Mrs.' Lammert was the daughter of the late Mr. and Mrs. Atkin (her father was at one time associated with the Hong Kong and Whampoa Dock Company).

She was 49 years of age...

In addition to the bereaved husband she is survived by a son, Mr. G. P. Lammert, Junior, the well-known Rugby footballer and athlete and a daughter, Miss Lammert, also by two step-children by her husband's first wife—Mr. E. G. Lammert who is at Singapore, and Mrs. Gray, wife of an army officer at Calcutta, India.

Mr. Lammert, Senior, is brother of Mr. H. A. Lammert, broker, and Mr. L. E. Lammert, the auctioneer.

The funeral takes place this evening passing the Monument at five o'clock.

## BIG THEFT FROM SAFE

Au Iu-chi, accountant of the Tung Sing firm of No. 36, Wing Lok Street, reports that between 7.30 and 11.30 p.m. yesterday, his safe was opened by a duplicate key and $2,177 in Hong Kong banknotes were stolen.

A servant employed in the firm is suspected, he being alleged to be the only person besides Mr. Au who is supposed to know where the key was kept.
