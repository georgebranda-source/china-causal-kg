## COTTON TRADE

### (Reuter's Service.)

London, Julie 11.

In an address at the Manchester Chamber of Commerce, Sir Maneckji Dadabhoy, a member of the Council of State of India, and a prominent Indian industrialist, declared that the Japanese cotton trade competition was going to menace the world unless steps were taken in time both in England and in India. Britain must cheapen the cost of production. He suggested that the Indian import duty of 111 per cent. might be increased to 20 or 25 per cent. against foreign manufactures and reduced to say five per cent. in the case of England.

Mr. J. H. Rodier, chairman of the Irwin section of the Chamber declared that if in order to cheapen production the standard of living of the workers in England must be reduced to the standard of Japan. Britain would prefer to lose its preponderance in the cotton trade. He was of the opinion that the competitive power of Japan was increased by the inflation of the yen. The cheapness of Indian cotton last year and this was an advantage which might only be temporary.

Sir Edward Rhodes, who has just returned from the Far East said that he was convinced that Japan was a country that was making most rapid and most dangerous progress against us in India and the traders in the Far East, including China were most afraid of Japan even in regard to fine goods.

Berlin, June 11.—The obligations of the Stinnes concern are reported to amount to 155,000,000 marks of which 110,000,000 are due in June. Forty per cent of this amount is covered by goods etc., so there is roughly a balance of 90,000,000. Banking firms which attended the recent conference are prepared to advance 50,000,000 marks while the remaining forty will be guaranteed by a banking concern headed by the Darmstaedter Bank, the Reichs Bank being ready to give all assistance for bills of exchange issued by the Stinnes concern in agreement with that body.—Renter.

Vancouver, June 11.—Mr. Edward Babcox, editor of the "Indiarubber Review" published in Akron, Ohio, in an interview said that strained Anglo-American commercial relations were likely to eventuate owing to the British restriction of rubber export which resulted in the price of rubber rising during the past three years from eleven to 78 cents a pound, with the prospect of its reaching a dollar. The result will be a situation necessitation international correspondence and forcing United States interests into the rubber production business.

## SIR GUY GAUNT

## HONGKONG'S DISTINGUISHED VISITOR

An interesting visitor to the Colony who is renewing acquaintance with the Far East after a lapse of many years is Vice-Admiral Sir Guy Gaunt, K.C.M.G., C.B., C.M.G. M.P.

Sir Guy renews an acquaintance with the Far East which in his younger days must have provided him with many thrills. As lieutenant he took part in operations in the Philippines in 1807 and two years later he was in charge of the British Consulate at Apla, Samoa, when that place was attacked by rebel forces. As a result of his good work there (he raised what was known as Gaunt's Brigade) he was promoted to Commander.

In the capacity of Commander on H.M.S. Vengeance he saw service in the Russo-Japanese war being promoted to Captain in 1907. From 1914 to 1918 he acted as Naval Attache to H.B.M. Embassy in Washington and whilst acting as lason officer for United States with the rank of Commodore first class he was again mentioned in despatches in 1917. His promotion to Rear-Admiral in 1918 followed service with the Naval Intelligence Department and at the time of his retirement he was Vice-Admiral.

Since his retirement from the service Sir Guy has represented the Buckrose division of Yorkshire and has figured with distinction in all matters of administration of the Royal Navy and Colonial defence.
