SOME HEROES OF THE

## LIES: GOOD AND BAD

It happened to me the other day, while engaged on the rather sad task of reholding old letters, to come across one about golf, writes the golf correspondent of "The Times." The writer was praising the beautiful turf of a seaside course (the turf there is beautiful and delicate still, though this is 40 years on) and wrote:—"I think a professional could take his driver wherever he wanted to except in the bunkers."

There seems to me something pathetic and ingenious and out of date in that comment; it is a sentence that nobody would write to-day. First, there is the mention of a professional as if he were a god. A professional is a great man still, but not quite so great as when golf was first beginning to spread over England. Then he appeared a wizard or a conjurer. His very manner of waggling a club seemed unearthly to the band of clumsy middle-aged novices. He came, too, from a far country, he was a Scot and they were Englishmen; he had all that stark and formidable quality, that musky glamour, which belong to the North in Southern eyes.

That however, is by the way. What really strikes me as old and old-fashioned is the picture of the professional taking his driver nowadays except on the tea. He tears the ball away with his strong iron, and at long intervals takes his spoon. Once there really was wooden club play through the green. He wanted to use his driver even when playing his own ball. He wanted to use it very often, when retrieving the inept effects of his partner in a foursome. Alas! who takes out the professional to play in a foursome to-day?

And then how eloquent are those few words in the matter of lies, good lies and bad lies. No one to-day exclaims gladly that he has got a good life, nor grumbles at his enemy's luck in having one. We take our good lies for granted and only mention the subject of lies at all when railing against our fate. It is hard to say to what extent this is due to better and more expensive green keeping, and to what extent to the rubber-cored ball. Certainly if we do get a bad lie, the ball flies easily away from it; it also makes it possible to take all iron club with far less sacrifice of distance. The reward of the man who can nip the ball out and away with wood is not what it was. Of all the romantic and splendid things I can remember from my earliest reading in golf, one stands out.

It related to Lieutenant James Campbell Stewart, of the 72nd Highlanders, a great man who played level with Allan Robertson and, with Mr. George Glennie, won the Silver Claret Jug in the grand tournament at St. Andrews in 1858, "thereby," as the records of Blackheath so proudly tell, "constituting this club the Champion Golf Club of the World." Somewhere it is written of Mr. Stewart that he never troubled to see his ball, but threw it down upon the teeing ground; and before that simple statement all the stories of the Morrises and the Parks paled in my youthful eyes. DROTURB. CANOBGLED.

'The appearance of Mr. Arthur Henderson, M.P. for Burnley and

In Socialist ex-Minister, at the Second International had its effect when he was barred from lecturing to a group of business men at Hurbford, Connecticut, as a result of a protest made by the National Security League.

The secretary of the league, Mrs. C. M. In mid-week he addressed a large meeting in New York in support of the city's Labour candidate for the mayorship, and his speech was broadcast. He declared that the Treaty of Versailles was "economically (sound)" and was responsible for the unemployment of a million and a half British workers.

Mr. Henderson, who is on vacation, hoped to attend the Inter-Parliamentary Union's conference at Washington, but was too late. Now he plans to be present at the Canadian session at Ottawa.

### (Reuter's Service.)

eking; December 3.

A Chinese editor, after an interview with Feng Yu-haiang, states that Feng desires to keep Tian Chi-jai in office for the present. ## N HOLE OF DISTRESS

I am quite sure in my own mind which is the one hole in the world at which I am most apprehensive as to my lie. It is the 13th or Sea hole at Rye. No one can say that the lies for that second shot are bad. They are perfectly fair—fair with a colourless impartiality. The ball does not sit down in a hole, but neither does it sit up on ever so small a pinnacle. It just lies close, as close as it can, to that flat unhelpful plain, and there, in front, are the uncompromising hills, and there is no way round. I have said that the lies are not bad, and that is the truth; but heaven only knows—and may it forgive me—how many times I have said my lie was bad after topping my second. At that hole we long for just a touch of softness in the ground to help us, if only in fancy, and it is not there. It is hard hard hard without an inch of "give" or mercy in it, and oh! my goodness I how it does find out the bad bravery player.
