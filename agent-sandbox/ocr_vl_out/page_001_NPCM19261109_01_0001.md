## “NEW” BOYCOTT

## Transport Union's Word Obeyed

DEADLOCK IN CANTON.

Chinese Ships Not Taking Up

"Canton's "new" boycott has actually started, the order of the Transport Union being obeyed as from yesterday.

The result is that another deadlock—as far as merchandise is concerned—has set in at Canton, but passenger communication is still permitted.

Several forces of agitators are at work, each with different objects.

The Transport Union comprises cargo coolies and the employees of cargo junks. A resolution was passed a week ago, by over 1,000 delegates, when the handling of cargo to or from British (or Hong Kong) vessels was forbidden as from Nov. 8 (yesterday).

Hitherto the Steamboat Co.'s ships have carried little or no cargo down, except the usual quota of foodstuffs, shipped without recourse to members of the Transport Union, the respective shop fokis working the merchandise themselves.

No Shipments To-day.

However, the "outside" companies (i.e., the boats operated by Chinese owners, under the British flag) have done a good deal of business.

Yesterday evening, a report came from an authentic source that the S.S. "Tung On" (of the Tung On S.S. Co.) had been boy-cotted by the Transport Union, on arrival at Canton in the afternoon. Accordingly, the S.S. "Sai On" took up no cargo this morning.

As stated yesterday, the licences of the cargo junkes at Canton are issued by the Harbour authorities of the Chinese Maritime Customs and these licences contain a clause whereby they may be cancelled if the holders refused to work cargo.

## Hope From Government

"Native merchants in Canton are also looking to the Nationalist Government to devise a speedy "settlement" by ordering the men back to work.

The position at present is that passengers can travel without restriction between Canton and Hong Kong either by rail or by steamer. Those who prefer the Steamboat Co.'s vessels are still subject to molestation and veiled threats, in spite of armed police patrols sent out by the Canton Government.

On the other hand, cargo can be sent up to other West River ports where the trouble is not so much agitation as the examination by the Government inspection corps.

Two Chinese flags.

Another body of agitators—other than those picketing the Steamboat Co.'s wharves and the Transport Union—is the body pressing for the Chinese boats under British registry to change over to the Chinese flag. Others go still further. They are not satisfied with merely “the Chinese flag.” They insist that those at present under Chinese registry shall cease to fly the “Republican” five-barred ensign and hoist the “clear sky, white sun” banner of the Nationalist Government. One river steamboat has already been told that the use of the five-barred flag will be construed as “anti-Revolutionary” policy.

There is another dispute in which the rice merchants and some lightermen are concerned but this is not attributable to anti-British or anti-foreign agitation.

The Strike Committee talks of appointing a select committee to investigate into the fire on Saturday, when their headquarters at East Garden were burned out.

It is now practically definite that there were no casualties through the blaze but it is almost impossible to account for the prisoners, in the face of the attitude of the Strike Committee.

Native traders look on the fire as a blessing in disguise.

New headquarters will be found at Whampoo. Meanwhile, a few more of the rank and its have taken advantage of the opportunity to leave without even applying for the $10 demobilisation grant.

## Outline of Government's

The Miners' Executive considered for three hours this morning the compromise plan drafted by the Government for the settlement of the mining dispute. This afternoon they proceeded to Downing Street and submitted their views to the Premier and the Coal Committee of the Cabinet.

The coalowners were then summoned, and were interviewed separately. Conversations were still proceeding at half past seven this evening. From statements made by members of the Miners' Executive it would appear that important points are still in dispute.—British Wireless Service.

Five Points:

The Government's suggestions to end the coal dispute are believed to be on the following lines:—

(1) The minimum percentage in addition to the basis rates to be that provided in the 1921 agreement—the minimum to be paid whatever the hours worked.

(2) The ratio for the division of the proceeds of the industry between wages profits to vary between 87-13 and 86-15 according to the circumstances of each district.

(3) The present mode of ascertaining the proceeds to continue.

(4) The subsistence allowance of the low paid day wage men to be retained.

(5) In the absence of a national agreement, a guarantee to be given to the Prime Minister from each district.—Reuter.

## EARLIER CABLES

The Miners' Executive met this morning to consider proposals for the settlement of the coal dispute on a basis of district agreements. These proposals were drafted by the Government after conversations with both parties on Saturday. They are set out in a series of national principles to be embodied in district agreements.

In submitting their conditional offer to accept the district agreements, the miners laid down demands for firstly, a national minimum percentage, secondly, a district subsistence wage, thirdly a common method of ascertainment of proceeds with a common ratio of division between wages and profits, and fourthly, a form of national co-operating machinery. The Government put these suggestions before the coal owners in a more definite form designed to replace the national co-operating body, by safeguarding more closely the observance of national principles by the district without recourse to arbitration or a national body set up by legislation.

The owners, it is stated, have fallen in with the Government's proposals to the extent of agreeing to ask their district associations to revise their terms, and reframe them on a common plan in any district agreements that may be negotiated. The Government's present idea appears to be that when it is obtained from the owners the promise will, if passed on to the miners with the full responsibility of the Government behind it, constitutes a sufficient guarantees for the miners that they will be treated fairly.
