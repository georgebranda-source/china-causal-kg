## A SATURDAY CAUSERIE

Although the Colonial Government of Hong Kong and the Embassy at Peking are answerable to different departments of Empire administration, the need is none the less apparent for the fullest possible co-operation in the matter of safeguarding British interests in North and South China. Mr. Miles Lampson, the new Minister to Peking, showed his appreciation of the need for such cooperation when, at some considerable personal inconvenience, he transhipped at Hong Kong in order that he might hold informal conversations with H.E. the Governor. This visit should enable him to grasp the peculiar problems with which British administration is faced in its relations with South China and to understand the patience and sacrifices of the British communities in the south in the endeavours that have been made to overcome these difficulties. Mr. Lampson will leave Hong Kong today with the best wishes of all sections of the community for a successful term of office. He has already voiced at Home the Government's deep sympathy with the difficulties of the Chinese people; its impartiality towards the contending factions; and its duty to protect the lawful rights of British subjects. Pending the establishing of a national government the British Government is bound to accord its nationals the fullest possible protection and exact reparation where no Chinese authority exists, a policy which cannot be misinterpreted as indicating an attitude of unfriendliness or a desire to interfere with internal politics of China.

That society is made up of a number of units, mutually dependent, and that the inceptive to labour is enjoyment of the fruits of the labour—individual effort and individual reward—is being gradually recognised by the Soviet rulers, who may be devoid of common humanity and justice but are certainly no fools. Hence the New Economic Policy, the change in the law relating to inheritance, and the effort to secure outside aid in repairing the ravages of the past eight or nine years of destruction. The last mentioned they will not get, we hope, until guarantees are given that they recognise the law of commerce and reliance can be put on their promises. The world in general recognises this and laughs at the Communist-inspired fables about the purchase of millions and millions of pounds worth of machinery, payment to be deferred, the security being the promises of the Communist minority. Later on, perhaps, ready cash will be offered for machinery and goods, but only as a way of lining the pockets of the 0.08 per cent. of the Russians that rule. There are thus two causes at work which give some hope for the future of Russia, one internal which may result in the formation of real national Soviets, when the power of the minority will be dispersed and weakened among the local people who form national Soviets; and the external force of circumstances resting on the fact that no other nations will trust the Soviet rulers for that international commerce and intercourse which is essential to every country, however apparently self-contained. ## CORRESPONDENCE

## SCOTLAND'S DESTINY

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"]

Sir,—May I crave the hospitality of your columns briefly to outline the chief reasons why it has been thought necessary to found the Scottish National Movement, the principal aim of which is to set up an independent Parliament in Scotland which would, of course, remain within the circles of the British Nations?

The present is unquestionably Scotland's hour of destiny. She must assert herself now as a nation, or linger on in the slough of provincialism for generations to come. It is not the duty of anyone man or body of men to recognise this necessity or take action to make it pass from the region of the ideal to the actual, but of everyone of Scottish birth gentle as well as simple, high as well as humble. Let us recollect that we are not asking a favour but demanding a right.

The grand error that our opponents and many of our well-wishers make is in thinking that in striving to further the cause of Scottish independence we are animated by commercial, political or similar reasons alone. On the contrary, our ideals are wholly and absolutely Nationalist in character. To put the matter more crudely, we desire to see Scotland lead her own national existence, to fulfil her national destiny in her own way. Our outlook, our manners, thoughts, and hopes, are not those of our neighbours. Our case rests on the circumstance that we are an entirely distinct people, with a record of separate national progress reaching back nearly 1,500 years and possessing indefeasible national rights of our own. All the argument in the world cannot gainsay this salient fact, and for its complete vindication we shall continue to strive until success crowns our efforts.

The Scottish National Movement now demands that a separate Scottish Parliament be erected, the destinies of Scotland to remain in the hands of that Parliament as elected by the Scottish people. That is a clear call, a definite objective towards which to work, a cause which men and women of all classes and parties can understand and join in striving for.

If other incentives be needed, the spectacle of our population gradually being diverted to Canada, the influx of countless thousands of people of alien races, the usurpation of the land, for sporting purposes and the continued neglect of public business, should surely suffice to cause the dullest of Scotsmen to protest against conditions which would be tolerated no where else in the world.

Scottish nationalism must remain an ideal founded on deep and sacred sentiment; but if it is to be practically successful it must extend its activities out of the region of sentiment and vision into those of action and sacrifice. Consciousness of race-feeling! That is our sheet-anchor. Let all Scotsmen and women join the Scottish National Movement, which has now 4,000 members, and help to accelerate its advance from a slow march to a victorious charge! Will all persons in favour of this object communicate at once with the Organiser, Scottish National Movement, 4 Stafford Street, Edinburgh?

Yours, etc.,

LEWIS SPENCE.

President, Scottish National Movement

## Student Invasion

The "North China Daily News" observes that it is a common saying among those who live in American university towns that "you can tell a group of university students as far as you can see them because of the funny-dressed men and ugly women." Shanghai, not being an American university town, critics of our student visitors, who walked along the maloo and adjacent roads yesterday, voiced their opinion of the young ladies as follows: "They are sweet little things, some of whom are entirely too far away from home." With regard to the young men, the general opinion in Shanghai was that their clothes were peculiar. Loud vested youths, coloured like something that Shanghai has seldom seen, paraded through the hotel lobbies. "Other peacocks in plus-fours and latest model collars and ties gave all an ample opportunity to visualise the more sombre variety of clothing.

## The Rugger Season

Rugby football commences in earnest this week-end, when practice games will be played to-day and to-morrow. On Sunday afternoon the Shanghai Scottish (S.V.C.) are playing H.M.S. With these new players the Rugby Football Association are hoping to get together one of the best teams that have ever represented Shanghai.

## The Shipping Strike

The latest move of the strikers is characteristic of Canton. Yesterday afternoon, a number of pickets, arriving in motor-cars, boarded a China Merchants' steamer lying at the French Bund and attempted to stop work by violence. Police investigations show that the cars came from the Chapel district. At the time these steamers are loaded, there are hundreds of people about the wharf, and therefore the pickets had little difficulty in getting aboard the vessel. A squad of Annamite policemen was sent to the wharf, but in the meantime the efforts of the police had proved sufficient to put an end to the trouble.

## When He Will Pay

Mr. L. K. Kentwell, the local British barrister, who has been somewhat prominent during recent weeks in connection with a case brought against him by a Chinese in connection with the registration of a company at the Spanish Consulate, and which resulted in a move being made for his disbarment, has created something in the nature of a sensation by writing to the Municipal Council's solicitor and stating that he declined to pay his rates until "Shanghai is handed back to the Chinese people whose Sovereignty the Imperialistic Countries of Europe have continually infringed." Mr. Kentwell, who was born at Hong Kong, was educated at Oxford prior to being called to the Bar at the Inner Temple, and it is difficult to understand his attitudes towards the "Imperialistic Countries of Europe" for not so long ago, when in the witness box, he admitted having a most lucrative practice adding, "apart from life insurance money, I should be worth $100,000 were I to die today." His British nationality and English education seem to have been very useful.
