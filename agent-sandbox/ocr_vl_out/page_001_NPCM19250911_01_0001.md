## WHAT NEWS IS GOOD

## ——

Interesting developments have followed, in quick succession, the acts of violence on the Chinese s.s. "Wah Shan" which was bringing treasure, livestock and a general cargo from Kwong Chow Wan to Hongkong.

Seen this morning, a representative of the Sui Lee "Co., owners, told a "China Mail" man that he had received "good news" regarding the "Wah Shan." On learning that the steamer had been taken to Shekki, his master had proceeded to Macao, en route for Chinshan, where negotiations had been opened with agents of the authorities directing the strike. The speaker was not in a position to say whether the cargo would be returned—and he gave the impression that beyond being interested in the safety of the ship, the cargo being, perhaps, more a matter for the shippers and/or consignees—but his information was that there was little anxiety as to the ultimate fate of the "Wah Shan."

## "Hoi Nam" A Marked Ship!

Yesterday an unconfirmed report was mentioned in the "China Mail," that the s.s. "Hoi Nam," flying in the same trade, had also been menaced by armed Bolshevik vessels.

This morning, the local office of the owners said that they had not been advised accordingly. They recognised the fact that the "Hoi Nam"—although she flew the Portuguese flag—was a marked ship, having incurred the hostility of the Canton Strike. Executive because she had never ceased running since the strike began. For this reason, and because threats had been received warning them against transporting foodstuffs to Hongkong, they had given definite instructions to the Kwong Chow Wan agents to telegraph as soon as the ship arrived there.

Under normal conditions, it took the "Hoi Nam" thirty hours to make the direct trip from this Colony to the French port. ## “HONAM'S” TRIPS

PASSENGERS NOT ALLOWED

TO LAND.

On the three previous trips rampans were allowed to come alongside the ship off Shameen and take the passengers' ashore, but on Thursday afternoon it was seen that the strike pickets had determined to prevent any of the passengers landing in Canton. Sampans were kept back at a distance from the ship and a strict watch was kept by the pickets to carry out their purpose. That the sampan crews had been threatened of the consequences was apparent from the fact that the few that came near the boat. disregarded the appeals of the passengers to take them away. A strike picket on a motor boat was overheard to say that if they wanted to return to Canton they must come by train.

PICKETS' STRONG CORDGN.

A surprise awaited the 700 odd Chinese passengers who travelled to Canton by the fourth trip of the s.s. "Honam" on Wednesday morning.

## STRIKE EPISODE

CHIVALROUS PICKET AND CORPSE.

HONGKONG WOMAN'S STORY.

## //

That Canton strike agents are openly working in Macao is one of the allegations made by a woman who has just returned to Hongkong from an up-country village.

Taking her children with her, she left Hongkong by ordinary steamer at the beginning of the strike. This week she decided that trouble would break out in Canton and that, after all, Hongkong was the best place. One reason for her thinking that disorder would break out in Canton was the fact that several imposing bridges of boats had been thrown across her part of the Canton River delta—this being one of the Bolshevik preparations to take Honam. These bridges were not taken down after the danger to Honam blew over, but Li Fook-lam's troops still remained in her village and their proximity was threatening.

## IMMENSE CLEARING HOUSE

She went by the regular tow-boat to Canton. From Canton she proceeded by other tow-boats to Heungshan, to Shekki and then to Chinshan which is outside the Macao border. Here the strike pickets have established an immense clearing house. People arriving there, to get into Macao, are closely scrutinised and questioned. If it is admitted that Hongkong is the ultimate destination the person is immediately sent back. Those who have the appearance of workers—of either sex—are also frustrated, being detained in large boats until passage is secured.

People wishing to go on to Macao must give an address in that Colony from which a (shop's) chopped guarantee will be forthcoming to the effect that the person concerned is a bona fide resident of Macao. This woman did not know a soul there, but she concocted an address on the spur of the moment. "In vestigators' left that same evening for Macao to look up the place mentioned as all others given by travellers on the same tow-boat. Some of the pickets were, so she told a "China Mail" reporter, chivalrous to the extreme. They pointed out that she had two very young children and that accommodation could not be found for them nor provision made for them. Some wanted to let her go as she did not look like an amah but others were adamant, and she had to pay 14 cents per head for a meal.

## BOUND WITH ROPES

Next morning the “investigators” returned. Those who were lucky enough to have had guarantees found for them were released. The address she gave could not be found and she had to stay another day.

Later in the morning she saw a country woman sneak up close to the pickets' headquarters where she turned over a man's corpse to see if it was that of her husband. Four villagers had run the gauntlet in getting vegetables down to Macao that morning. They carried firearms and one striker was killed. The smugglers' casualties were not known. This country woman had turned over the striker's dead body by mistake and she was instantly bound with ropes and imprisoned for being a spy.

The next morning it was decided to let the Hongkong woman go. Her money had been taken from her—she knew not where—and so a sampan was ordered to give her a free passage to Macao, whence she came over to Hongkong.
