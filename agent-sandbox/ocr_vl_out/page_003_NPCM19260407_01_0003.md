## MISERY IN PEKING

## PEOPLE'S DRIVEN TO CRIME

On account of the high cost of living and increase of unemployment resultant from the prolongation of the war, a large number of the poor people have been driven to the adventurous trade of robbery and burglary to gain a living. During the last two months over 100 cases of highway robbery have been reported around the metropolitan suburban districts. The majority of the robbers, were only armed with clubs, knives, or spears, while things robbed were of little value, such as horses, donkeys, clothes and sundries. This shows clearly that these robbers are not professional and only driven to this under desperate conditions.—Kuo Wen.

## Pancakes in the Menu

Pannakes, as has so often been told, were in vogue in pre-Reformation days to use up the butter, cream, and eggs in the larder, these being forbidden foods during Lent. Recipes for pancakes are plenteous in the old cookery books. One edition I have of the famous Mrs. Hannah Glass's classic gives six recipes, four for those of "a fine sort." As the supposedly plain kind is generous of eggs and milk and contains a glass of brandy it was not exactly meagre (observes a writer in the "Daily Mail.") Another edition I have of Mrs. Glass's work contains nine pancake recipes, the last a "pink coloured one, a pretty corner dish for either dinner or supper." Bestroot juice is the colouring agent. Some of the old recipes meet the possibility of eggs being scarce. In that case the batter was made of flour, small beer, ginger, "etc." One I recall makes use of "clean snow." Sometimes the batter was fried in olive oil so that animal fat should not be used.

Sir Harry Baldwin, surgeon-gentist to the King, lecturing at the Institute of Hygiene, made an unfavourable comparison of the face of the modern woman with that of a woman living in the Tudor period. Sir Harry said:

An examination of the wonderful portraits and drawing of personages of the Tudor period by Holbein, an Windsor Castle, will reveal that not one shows evidence of the contracted jaw pinched nostrils, and blankly gazing eyes of the adenoid subject.

For a typical example of a woman of the denoid diathesis, look in the exhibition of works by the late John Surgent now on view at the Royal Academy.
