## $11,000 THEFT

SWEETHEART SHADOPED.

An assistant accountant of an import and export office at 49"Deau Vaux Road appeared this morning before Mr. R. E. Lindsell charged with larceny of $11,100 from his employer.

Sub-Detective Inspector Fallon, who prosecuted for the police, said that on November 16 Mr. Leung Lau-po (master of "the firm"), left his shop with defendant in charge. An hour later, complainant returned, and found missing from his drawer $1,000 in bank notes and a note receipt of $10,000 from a native bank. Inquiries were made at the bank who informed the police that the amount had already been withdrawn.

Defendant was constantly shadowed by the police who were later led to his sweetheart residing at Whitfield Street. The place was searched and $10,300 was found.

Defendant pleaded guilty when the charged was read to him and was sentenced to six months hard labour.

## CHINA HOSTILITIES NOW IMMINENT

CANTONESE V. FENGTIENITES.

A stubborn campaign is certain. In this latest phase of the nation-wide conflict there may lie a solution to the confusion of so many governments, but events in China have taken the least expected course in the past and a prediction is futile at the moment.

Both General Chang Kai-shek and Marshal Chang Tso-lin have won signal victories before. Each has in turn crushed formidable adversaries but not eliminated them.

No Unification Yet?

Wu Pel-fu and Sun Chuan-fang have been stripped of their power but they still have a following. They still count as adversaries of the Nationalists.

The Christian general was put to rout by the Fengtienites, but he still has many troops in the north-west.

Even if one of the two major factions is totally removed, when this threatened struggle is over, what is going to happen to the others now remaining in the background?

If history is repeated, one side will be trounced but not wiped out. And in this light I venture to think that the time is not ripe, or the field is too vast, for unification to be attained by military conquest.

Chang Tso-lin's Wealth. As to the chances of the rival camps, it is difficult hazard a guess.

Observers with "the Armies point to the Manchurian warlord as being the stronger in men, munitions and money.

To counteract Russian help for the Southerners, Chang Tsolin has his division of White Russians.

Within the last few years, the Fengtien party has amassed considerable wealth — estimated at $10,000,000—with which to equip their army to great advantage.

Against Wu Pei-fu and Sun Chuan-fang, the Cantonese had a telling superiority in a united command whereas these two Northern leaders were dependent on the goodwill of overgrown subordinates miscalled allies. No so with Fengtien.

The system that started from Mukden savours a good deal of feudalism, and each general is personally responsible to the Marshall. More than once subordinates have rebelled but never with success.

One point in favour of the Nationalists, even before the vanguards are at grips, is that some of the people are on their side.

This statement needs to be qualified. The middle and upper classes are partial to nobody, being weary of all disputes.

8he lower or artisan masses are more apt to declare, in their own roundabout way, for the Southerners than for Fengtien.

Very briefly the explanation lies in the Kuomintang policy of taxing merchants and property-owners heavily, while the wage-earner pays little in proportion. Not so, the Northerners. All is grist to their mill.

Fairly Evenly matched.

It also remains to be seen how far the Sovietised propaganda will serve Canton, in the third and biggest stage of their expedition.

So much as to conjecture. In territory, the adversaries are fairly evenly matched.

To start with, Chang Tso-lin had three provinces forming Manchuria (known as the three eastern provinces), Chihli and Shantung.

Although constantly offering aid to the Northerners, Fengtien maintained a policy of masterly inactivity against Canton and practically ceased war as soon as the other Nationalist Army that of the Christian general (Feng Yu-hsiang) was removed to a safe distance.

Meanwhile, the Southerners wrested Hunan and Hupeh provinces from Wu Pei-fu. Kiangsi was captured from Sun Chuanfang and Fukien is partly overrun.

To prevent losing everything, Sun Chuan-fang sought. Chang Tao-lin.

At this stage, a very recent incident

Starting with only Fukien and Chekiang provinces, Marshal Sun pushed the Fengtlenites out of Kiangsu and Anhui. These two provinces had been occupied by Chang Tso-lin when the Christian general rebelled against and brought about the downfall of his master, Wu Pei-fu.

Using the thin end of the wedge, Chang Tso-lin had pushed southwards along the coast, across the lower Yangtse valley and into Shanghai.



Last year, Sun Chuan-fang exerted pressure and the Manchurian army retreated northwards into Shantung, fighting a rearguard action with success.

This check by Sun has ranked in Chang Tso-lin's breast. And

Sun must have drained the bitterest of dregs when he begged Chang Tso-lin to take back those very same provinces because he himself had been defeated by the Cantonese and was unable to stem the tide any longer.

Simply by letting Sun Chuan-ang fight the "Southerners, and without interfering" to preserve he balance of power, Chang Tson has recovered precious territory which he coveted.

The result is that Sun is left with less than what he started with, i.e., Chekiang and the northern half of Fukien. But area smaller in scope is easier to defend with limited resources.

"Loyalty in Death."

Writers in the vernacular papers have been eloquent in picturing the scene at the Tientsin conference, when Sun had to apologise for his ambition. — Not the man that he was; he still counts. That his leadership is still valued is evident from a declaration by one of his generals who came under suspicion, that he “never had an opportunity to show his gratitude for the kindnesses he” had received and he therefore welcomed a chance for his body and bones to be smashed and grounded, so that he may prove his loyalty.

Not so much can be said for Wu Pei-fu. Holding only a small part of Honan province (to the North of Hankow) he has torned down offers of help from Chang Tso-lin. Perhaps he suspected that what little remained to him would be taken from him as the primary condition for fighting his battle with Canton. One of his generals wanted $1,000,000 from Fengtien when he would undertake a counter-offensive himself.

The Christian general (Feng Yu-hsiang) can render signal service to his allies at Canton by coming out and again fighting Fengtien. But he prefers to bide his time and conserve his strength.

On the face of things, Chang Tso-lin is "not taking up the lines directly facing the Cantonese camp merely to ease Sun Chuan-fang's burden.

Inducement comes in the shape of two provinces and the control of both "banks at the mouth of the Yangtse River.

Still, this will mean that his men will be blocking the progress of General Chang Kai-shek. The latter may await a more favourable time for launching his next

Since they are then to fight in any event, the Fengtien army can do worse than start immediately, now that they have got the territory. These men are used to the rigours of winter which may inflict hardships on the Cantonese soldiers. That is why it is thought that once sufficient troops have been poured down from the North—and this is only a matter of days—hostilities will break out in the biggest war the Republic has seen.

Canton will endeavour to cross the Yangtse and Fengtien's task will be to get south of that great waterway. The length of this river alone is sufficient to entail a long drawn-out war.
