# LOCAL AND GENERAL

The Exchange Banks will be closed to the transaction of public business on Monday, October 12.

For cheap living Timor is to be recommended since a man is passing rich on £50 a year there, according to a recently returned traveller.

The Chinese Government is about to publish a de luxe edition of the Chinese classics. There will be a few copies for sale at $6,000 (Chinese currency) each, and the rest will be donated to libraries in China and abroad.

Admission prices for the 9.15 performance of Harold Lloyd's great comedy "Hot Water" at the Queen's Theatre to-night are $1, $1.50 and $2 (not $2.50 as erroneously stated in one announcement).

"We offer a cordial welcome to the claw of the British Eastern squadron,"—so reads the legend on a packet in which picture postcards are being sold at Amanhashidate. It is rather doubtful whether the honoured crew would recognise itself as the "claw"—presumably of the British lion, says the "Japan Chronicle."

A thirsty tourist drank copiously from a roadside spring in Oregon. A sign that read, "The water is contaminated," had been placed beside the spring. An official of the Oregon Highway Department, driving by, saw the drinker and stopped to warn him; only to be met by the question, "What sort of mineral water is this? I never heard of it before." The authorities have now replaced the sign with one reading: "This water is not fit to drink."

Jacob Epstein's eighteen sculptures on the building of the British Medical Association, which opened his career as the stormy petrel of contemporary British art, will not be transferred to the association's new building, now nearing completion. These figures nearly a generation ago caused a furore in staid England and were the reproach of all academicians and were even important enough to bring down the condemnation of the churches. The doctors, apparently, do not wish to mix up with controversies over art. They will not lose on their investment, as they paid $10,000 for the sculptures and already have received offers of $30,000 for them.

The news that one of the new cruisers is to be built at Devonport has been received with great joy, for although the work is unlikely to involve the taking on of any additional men, it guarantees the constant employment for several years of the existing staff, now numbering about 12,000. It is expected that the new cruiser will be up to the Washington limit of 10,000 tons, and that she will be laid down upon the slip at present occupied by the cruiser "Cornwall." The latter vessel in all probability will be ready to leave the slip in February next, in which event the keel of the new one will be laid down in the following month.

# SOCIAL AND PERSONAL.

Bulletin No. 200 of the Canadian Pacific contains the following: — Canadian Pacific—As Others See Us.

Mr. and Mrs. A. G. Hewitt are due to leave England for Hongkong on November 8.

Dr. William Peek, the Managing Director of the London Congress of 700 American doctors, says: "The whole tragedy of modern life is that all the best citizens, the greatest men, are killing themselves before their time." He denounces the mania for overwork, overplay, and the indulgence in night life. The modern hustle is just suicide, and a return to the simple life would do away with the necessity for many doctors.

The forthcoming weeding is announced of Mr. John Walter Harris, of the Kowloon Docks, to Mrs. Adola Camino, widow, of Jordan Road, Kowloon.

The scantiness of women's costumes at the recent Ascot meeting was widely commented on. One smartly dressed woman made the admission that the hat which she wore at Ascot as a debutante fifteen years ago weighed only an ounce more than the entire outfit she wore to day. The woman who was the first held by an evening newspaper held the recall of weighing the hat at the time because it gave her a leadsche. She put her chiffon-gown silk underwear, hat shoes, stockings and handbag of the sales and found they weighed quite an ounce more than the hat.

The Australian-Oriental Line, Ltd., Captain Gambrill and owners of the S.s. "Changte" sent out invitations for a reception on board that ship from five to seven p.m. on Friday last at Manila.

The Liberal Party, which is widely regarded as almost extinct, won a striking success in a debate at the Union Society of Cambridge University recently. The motion before the house was "that this house, rejecting Conservatism, wishes to express its unqualified approval of the Liberal Party and its aims." The motion, of course, was one upon which both Conservatives and Labourites could unite against the Liberals. In spite of this, the Liberals won through by 146 votes to 152. Lord Hugh Cecil, brother of the more famous Lord Robert—now Viscount Chelwood—championed the cause of Conservatism, while the Liberal stalwart was Ramsay Muir, the historian, who edits the "Weekly Westminster."

A good story about that man of astonishing memory, “Datas.” Some ten years ago a friend asked him if he liked eggs to which “Datas” readily responded “Yes.” The other who frankly disbelieved in his powers, thinking his marvellous answers were a mere trick, made a careful note of the question. Then, some time ago, he met “Datas” again, and, before anything else was said, hurled at him “How?” Without the least hesitation “Datas” answered “Fried.”

A Consulate-General for the Argentine Republic was opened in Kobe on October 1, at 100, Yedo-machi. Mr. Francisco Ortis has been appointed Consul-General.

Apparently the fame of our popular songs is reaching to as far as the West Coast of Africa. An “educated” native writes to a Midland firm: “I want be happy, make me happy so too. Popula song say well. Do it now. Me, big, black bess man, want plenty size gold ring for beloved. Send so long an valuable present you go.”

If Ken Wood had been in China, the King would not have had to make his moving appeal to the public to avoid littering our parks with paper and other rubbish. The Chinese regard paper as one of the "four precious things," the other three being pens, ink, and ink-slabs. They pick up every loose scrap of paper they see if it bears printing or writing upon it, and the remnants are solemnly burned.—Candide, in "The Sunday Pictorial."

Sir Philip Cunliffe-Lister has now withdrawn his resignation as President of the Board of Trade as a result of pressure from the Premier. Sir Philip tendered his resignation over the Government's subvention to the coal industry, thinking it the proper thing to do, because of his wife's large interests as a coalowner. Mr. Baldwin and his Ministers felt strongly that there was no reason whatever in the circumstances why he should resign, and Sir Philip has now come round to their point of view.

Irene Castle's baby is "worth all the thrill there is in success; all the excitement there is in Paris and London and Rome; all the dancing in the world," she told a reporter who interviewed her in her new role of wife of Major Frederick McLaughlin, coffee merchant, and mother of a blue-eyed daughter now four and one-half months old. "Sixteen pounds and only four and half months old," the mother said. "I never used to know anything about babies, but now I'm like an encyclopedia on them. She makes me humble."

The hands of English women are growing bigger. A London jeweller of thirty years' experience is authority for the statement. "Twenty-five years ago to receive a request by a woman to have her ring enlarged was considered an extremely rare occurrence," he declared. "Beginning about 20 years ago I began to get perhaps one such request a year. But last year I received nine requests for enlarging rings while in the twelve month just ending my books show 57 orders for enlarging rings." Tennis and golf and women's wider interest in general athletics I believe is the cause of the sudden tendency for women's hands to break larger.

Sir William Joynson-Hicks, British Home Secretary, in an interview with the London “Morning Post” last month, characterised as “premature” the report that a special constabulary was to be recruited to safeguard the public against unrest fostered by the Communists. He admitted, however, that such a plan was under consideration, together with other “safety measures.”

During his stay in Chile, the Prince of Wales received many reminders of his grandfather's association with the Republic. Nominally King Edward acted as arbitrator between Chile and the Argentine, and his name is greatly honoured throughout the 38 degrees of latitude over which the State extends. Conventional boundaries only exist in the south, where by the award of King Edward in 1902, as a result of the examination of the disputed territories by the Commission, led by Sir Thomas Holdie, limits were established to lands where the ownership was not otherwise definable.
