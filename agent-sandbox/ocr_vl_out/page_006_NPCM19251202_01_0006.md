## A GREAT NEED

The chief argument used against Hongkong rejoining the China Association was that the China Association had done nothing for Hongkong. The correctness of the argument was disputed and it was pointed out that while Hongkong had done nothing for the China Association, the Association had not been idle in giving publicity to the facts of the China situation and valuable information on such things as the treaty rights already in existence. Hongkong is now a member of the parent body, and although the fact may not be patent, the future should show the wisdom of the step taken to link up with the parent body. The Association is admirably served by its committees in various parts of China. It is fed with facts and is in a pos

tion to broadcast them. People at Home should be as expert in Chinese affairs as we should be in Hongkong. There has probably been more ignorance in Hongkong of the situation in China, than there has been elsewhere. It should be the principal work of the local committee to publish, from time to time, information which comes to its notice. One thing to be aimed at is the distribution of consular reports where such are available and of sufficient value. In the early days of the trouble, Home papers of the more serious type, attempted seriously to understand the situation and to throw light on the dark places thereof, Correspondents—those especially who had knowledge of China and the Chinese—performed valuable service in contributing facts from their own experience. More often than not there were references to, and quotations from Consular reports. Those given to suggest that certain labour conditions in China were part of a British policy, were readily refuted. There is one particular. Blue Book under the title of "Papers respecting Labour Conditions in China," from which no less an authority than Mr. J. O. P. Blund suggests it is possible to understand "Why Chinese merchants are trustworthy and Chinese officials corrupt." It would do many—including Chinese—a considerable amount of good to have such knowledge—knowledge based on undeniable facts and as Consular reports doubtless give both sides of a case and are not one-sided documents, we might expect to read of the better side of official effort. In other words, the public should get a bird's-eye view of the Chinese situation, a view which

would help it to come to the perplexing Chinese question fully prepared to do it justice.....The matter is one for the local branch of the China Association.

## EUROPE'S NEW CHAPTER

It was fitting that such a great day as the one on which was signed a Treaty transforming the whole European situation should have been made the occasion of honouring a statesman whose skill and patience have contributed in no small measure to a successful outcome of the negotiations. Mr. Austen Chamberlain's reception when he returned to London from Locurno provided signal instance of the national gratitude felt to the Foreign Secretary—A.C., as he is familiarly known—for his triumph over difficulties and discouragement. In his own words, the treaties "threaten no one, are directed against no nation, secure the safety of many and ought to be welcomed by all". The most hopeful sign of the new spirit which is beginning to brood over Europe is the fact that the transformation was made possible by the act of Germany herself who indicated the way by which France and her eastern allies might obtain the guarantees they considered necessary for the creation of a spirit of security. General Von Hindenburg has thus falsified the predictions of those prophets who saw in his election to the presidency an error of sabre rattling and displaying of the mailed fist, for this gesture of Germany is entitled to be termed a generous one when it is considered that Alsace and Lorraine are definitely renounced, also whatever else the Peace Treaties gave to France and Belgium. In return, the other parties to the Treaty agree to an interpretation acceptable to Germany of the clause which had proved one of the greatest difficulties to her entry into the League of Nations. This was concerned with the Article under which the Powers agree to allow passage across their territory of the troops of any other member of the League when these troops are on their way to attack a common foe who has violated the covenant. The contention of Germany had been that as she is disarmed amongst armed neighbours she has not a status of equality with the other Powers. The document which has now emerged commits all parties to peaceful settlement of all possible disputes concerning the Eastern frontier of France or of rupture in the relations between France and Germany, and Belgium and Germany. There are also treaties of arbitration between Germany and Poland and Germany and Czecho-Slovakia and treaties of mutual guarantee between France and Poland, and France and Czecho-Slovakia. Germany's argument is thus met in this respect and guarantee given against such a contingency as she had feared, of Germany being prohibited from invading France under any circumstances and France being permitted to invade Germany whenever she could claim that her ally, Poland, was in danger. What is more, with her admission to the League, Germany will become a fellow labourer with her former enemies in the work of removing dangers to peace. It would really seem at last that the haunting menace of an advance one way or the other across the Rhine is in a fair way to be dissolved and that a newer and brighter page has been opened in the history of Europe.

The flower of the early summer is the iris. The Japanese take a large area of a public park and plant pools with iris. Over these they spread two houses with broad balconies that hang within reach of the voluptuous beauties. They raise a mound in the centre of the pond and build a small pavilion thereon. They curve airy bridges across the arm of the irregular pool, they make footpath through the enpurled marsh, so that you can see with ease the gorgeous tapestry of dark purples of all shades of blue, of pale violet and of velvety white. And in one corner of the garden you find a potter ensconced, fashioning cups and bowls and vases on which you may yourself paint your favourite flower or write your name and he will put it in his kiln and in a few minutes hand it to you—a souvenir of the iris season and the iris garden. ## JAPANESE FLOWERS AND FETE

Next comes the lotus, which might be called the emblem of the religion of the Orient.

It was four o'clock in the morning one day in late July that we stood on a bridge spanning the most famous lotus pond in Tokyo—in Ueno Park. We were there at this early hour only that we might hear the great blossoms open. They burst forth in all their loveliness with a gentle popping sound that had brought to the bridge a few of the most aesthetic of the Japanese men. A pastime not for the rabble is this listening to lotus opening, but it is for the cultured few whose tastes have been cultivated to a needle point of refinement. To the Occidental, the fascination of this lotus opening is for the eye rather than for the ear, as this flower of surpassing loveliness slowly opens like a living hand.

Then comes the great summer fete on the Sumida River in Tokyo.

"If the weather is fine to-night there will be something worth seeing near the Ryogoku Bridge on the Sumida River," said my particular Japanese friend; the first day of the fourth week in July. "It is the Kawabiraki, or Summer Festival of Tokyo, and it celebrates the opening of a bridge across the river over a century and a half ago."

At eight o'clock that evening we found ourselves breathing a sea of humanity that surged back and forth on the bridge, the stone and iron successor of the original wooden bridge responsible for the fete. The bridge and the shore were a moving forest, with each leaf a person. Once in this jungle there was no leaving it, for hundreds, my thousands, of policemen had been provided by the municipality to persuade the mass to keep moving, according to a regular programme. And there were babies in that struggling crowd whose mothers had not feared to bring them on their backs, and they were as safe as if in their own homes, for the baby in Japan is each a King—or at least a Crown Prince.



We might have embarked on any of the countless craft that slowly moved along the river in a crush that threatened any moment to reduce them to derelicts. Each bount sent forth bursts of song and laughter and snatches of samisen music, and streaked the river with blazing ribbons of radiancy from its red and white lanterns. Or we might have hired a room in any of the famous restaurants along the shore, filled with squabting figures so close that their knees and elbows touched, and there viewed a scene as bright and spirited as anyone could wish.

But we did not. We were in part of that great sea of human beings, no agitated as it was kept moving, directed by the lanterns bearing the double red stripes of the police, now quiet as it gazed upward toward the blazing sky and finding its voice in a prolonged Ab—Ah—A—A—as the ball of light binst into a fountain of dancing stars. And surely the art of pyrotechnics could produce nothing more spectacular than these which blazed for an instant against the black sky. A sharp detonation, a ball of fire sprang into the sky and a dragon dailed across the heavens, or the flags of the nations unfuried in the sky, or a peacock spread his tail in fiery splendour, or a great crimson fish of paper released in a shower of sparks leaped down into the boats below.

The next day we quite forgot the discomfort of the evening before and went forth to buy copies of the prints which the old Japanese masters had made in commemoration of the event. Hiroshige has left a number of prints showing the fete as it was celebrated in his time; Utama, given to gaiety himself, painted the scene in which he must often have taken part; Toyokuni I and II both used the Kawabiraki us subjects for their prints. And indeed even to day this bales of night scene is a suitable subject for artists. — Edith de Garcia, in Japan.
