## NOTE TO PEKING

British Minister on Wanhsen.

YANG SEN'S ACT OF PIRACY.

The Note of His Majesty's Minister states that he has the honor to acknowledge the receipt of the Walchitopu's "memorandio" on September 10 and 14 on the subject of the seizure of the British merchant steamers at Wanhien, in which four complaints were made against the British steamers in connection with the sinking of junks of the Upper Yangtse as follows: Firstly, "the steamer "Tienkuang" on June 13 in the district of Wanhien, secondly the "Wanliu" on June 8 in the district of Fengju; thirdly, the "Chiall" on August 2 at Foutan; and fourthly, the "Wanliu" on August 29 at Yungyang.

Owing to the Interruption of regular communications, due to civil war conditions on the Yangtse, and the fact that His Majesty's Consul at Chungking is at present at Ichang, the British Minister hitherto has only been able to obtain telegraphic reports regarding these cases, but the British Consul at Chungking states that of the four cases alleged the first was never reported to him, the second was completely refuted, and the third was referred to the Steamship Company concerned for observation.

As regards the fourth the British Consul; on the day after the occurrence, telegraphed to General Yang Sen that any claim against the "Wanliu" should be brought through his Consulate, when it would be promptly and equitably dealt with, and in view of Yang Sen's high-handed detention of two British ships, which were not connected with the Yungyang incident he forthwith proceeded to Wanhsien in order to deal with the matter personally.

The British Minister has already discussed this last Wanhsien case and the grave incidents arising therefrom, and now places on record the following outline of the facts as they have hitherto reached him:—

The "Wanliu" on August 29 was discharging passengers at Yung-yang when she was boarded by sixteen armed soldiers seeking to commandeer accommodation for the transport of Chinese troops in violation of the regulations prohibiting British vessels, in the interests of strict neutrality in Chinese civil wars, from carrying armed Chinese forces, whilst simultaneously sampans containing troops approached the ship.

Proper Legal Inquiry.

The “Wanliu” proceeded slowly in order to avoid the sampan when fire was opened from both banks of the Yangtse River and the troops on board rushed the bridge and engine-room, but fortunately were driven off by the ship's officers. Sampans endeavouring to come alongside the vessel fouled one another and eventually sank. On arrival at Wanhsien the troops were removed from the “Wanliu” by H.M.S. “Cockchafer” and the “Wanliu” proceeded on her voyage. On August 29 Yang Sen, apparently, as a reprisal, seized the “Wanhsien” and “Wantung,” putting at least 300 soldiers aboard. Yang Sen declined to discuss the matter with the commander of the “Cockchafer” whereupon the Consul hurried from Chungking to Wanhsien to take charge of the negotiations. Yang Sen refused to listen to his representations or release the steamers or the officers until the matter of “Wanliu” was settled. The Consul, acting under instructions from the Minister, in Peking, exhausted every possible conciliatory effort, including, naturally, an offer of a proper legal inquiry. Yang Sen declined to agree to any terms which did not permit him to exact compensation from the Company without regard to the actual facts of the case.

On September 5, H.M.S. "Widgeon" and the "Kiawo" the latter manned by four naval officers and 60 men, proceeded to Wanhsien to assist the "Cockchafer" in effecting the release of the ships and officers.

Strict British Neutrality.

## NO POWER?

## Local Law & Chinese Pirates

VITAL POINT RAISED.

Does Our Jurisdiction Extend to China?

In connection with the charge of piracy against three Chinese at the Criminal Sessions there was an interesting development this morning when Mr. C. G. Alebaster K.C., submitted that the court had no jurisdiction over Chinese subjects alleged to be implicated in piracy in Chinese waters.

The alleged piracy occurred off Cheung Chau Island, four miles outside the limits of British waters.

Mr. Alabaster submitted that the prisoners had no case to answer. (They have not yet given their evidence).

It had been proved beyond contest that the spot where the attack by the “white sail” sampant took place was within Chinese waters, said Mr. Alabaster.

For the purpose of his own argument, continued counsel, he would not contend that beyond the ultimate coasts of China there was a territorial jurisdiction extending over three or four miles. It would be sufficient for his purposes that the alleged piracy had taken place in Chinese territory without the additional mileage.

The spot in question was as much within the body of the county or district of China, continued counsel, as the Solent was within the body of Southampton and the Bristol Channel within the county of Glamorgan.

## JURY PRIVACY

### NEED FOR REFORM LOCALLY. READER'S COMPLAINT

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"] Sir,—As a member of the public interested in the murder trial which concluded yesterday I attended the court throughout the morning and was extremely surprised at the lack of adequate jury facilities.

In the first place, the jury when retiring to consider its verdict, passes through the public corridor and yesterday I observed a Chinese woman stop and address a member of the jury as he was on his way, with the others, to consider the verdict.

The staring crowd outside the windows of the room yesterday grew to such dimensions and members of it were so persistent in their attentions that, quite properly, the jury sent out a message asking that the verandah in question should be cleared.

This was in fact, done at the order of the usher but as the usher's duty is to remain at the door of the room, some distance away and out of sight of the verandah windows, the clearance was only temporary.

As a member of a former jury which also experienced considerable annoyance as a result of the attentions of a curious crowd in our deliberations on an equally grave occasion, I consider that the least the law which calls on them to adjudicate in an issue of life and death can do for them is to provide privacy during their deliberations.

In courts at Home the jury have a special entrance and exit which ensures that in leaving the court they are unmolested and the room with which they are provided is secure against all would-be evas-droppers or observers.

Hong Kong, September 22.

[The above letter was submitted by the “China Mail” this morning to the Hon. Mr. J. H. Kemp, Attorney General. Mr. Kemp said that the matter merited serious consideration and he would bring it to the attention of the Chief Justice. ## WAR ZONE SHIFTS

CHANG KAI-SHEK'S STRATEGIC ADVANTAGE:

WU CHANGES HIS MIND.

[By Our Special Correspondent.]

Latest movements in the central China war portend the shifting of the main scene of hostilities from Hupeh province to Kiangsi.

The lull in the Nationalists v. Sun Chuan-fang zone continues with the southerners moving up troops as though preparatory to striking. Marshal Sun Chuan-fang's "five provinces" are still rushing up reinforcements towards the more flexible parts of their line.

Despatches from Kiangsi point to General Chang Kai-shek having taken up the position of a semi-circle, the middle of which threatens Nanchang. the capital of Kiangsi province.

The southern main force is in the centre of a large salient, having “bent back” the Kiangsi garrison to an extent whereby fresh divisions can be pushed forward rapidly to any part of the semi-circle.

Previous hesitation on the part of Marshal Sun Chuan-fang is blamed for having given the Nationalists this big advantage.

Military experts predict that Sun Chuan-fang will not fight seriously at least till another week

has passed by, when he will have put sufficient troops in the field to ensure numerical superiority over the southerners.

He is still waiting for Marshal Chang Tso-lin of Fengtien to give evidence of good faith as a professed ally, by taking the field in the north, on Wu Pei-fu's side. Native peace missions are still relying on Sun Chuan-fang's suspicions of a "back door" attack by Chang Tso-lin, and on the remote possibility of an understanding with Chang Kai-shek.

No outbreak has been reported from the Fukien-Kwangtung border.

To the north of Hankow, General Tang Sung-chee, commanding the 8th army, seems to be starting another offensive against Wu Pei-flu, who is now on the Hupeh-Honan boundary.

The Nationalists have already nominated a council of government for Hupeh province—on the lines of that in Canton—together with other officials who include Mr. Chan Kung-pok was Foreign Commissioner and Superintendent of Customs. Mr. Chan was head of the Labour and Agricultural organisation in Canton. His place on the Boycott Conference was taken by another at the last moment owing to his being ordered to the north.

At the eleventh hour, Wu Peifu seems to have changed his mind as to a counter-offensive to relieve the pressure on Wuchang. There is no official news regarding the besieged city.

A telegram from Hankow, dated September 19, states that missionaries of all denominations in Hankow and their property are safe, but that some fighting has taken place at Chikungshan, where there are missionary and Alliance missionaries from Wuchang. Bombs have been dropped on buildings adjacent to the Alliance Mission property.—Reuter.
