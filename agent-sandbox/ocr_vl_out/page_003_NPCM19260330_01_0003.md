America's Advance:

An increase in America's naval anti-admiralty armament has been made quickly, and secretly, says the London "Evening News."

"Five out of her 18 battleships, including her newest craft, the "Maryland" "West Virginia," and "Colorado," will be fitted with eight of the most powerful anti-aircraft guns, allont. Hitherto, it is believed, they carried only eight 3-inchers.

Even the anti-aircraft armament of Britain's new ships, the "Nelson" and the "Rodney," which is secret, is believed not to exceed ten 4-inch guns. Otherwise the British ships are still comparatively weak against aerial attack.

## World's Swiftest

The naval correspondent of the Daily News writes: "While the 'Rodney' and 'Nelson' are still hush battleships, the Admiralty discloses that the cruiser and mine-layer "Adventure," which was launched in 1924, is an oil-burner of 6,740 tons, with genred turbines that generate 40,000 horsepower. It is understood that she possesses Diesel motors for cruising, but the fact has been suppressed. Four 4.7-inch guns and 16 smaller ones comprise an exceptionally light armament for a ship of her size, but the weight thus saved is available for numerous mines.

"H.M.S. "Adventure" is the Navy's first high-speed mine-layer. The new destroyers launched in January were the "Amazon" (1,330 tons) and the "Ambusendoc" (1,210 tons). Both can travel at 37 knots. No previous destroyer was capable of such tremendous speed, and they are easily the world's, swiftest war-craft.

"Secrecy is still maintained regarding the speed and armament of the giant submarine XI, whose tonnage is 2,525. She is completing her sea trials. A striking disclosure is France's and Japan's activity in building submarines."

HOLSTEIN'S LIFE PASSION

One by one the big reputations of Wilhelmian Germany are ruthlessly blown to smithereens. The latest to go is that of Baron Holstein, the man who from Bismarck's fall until his own retirement in 1906, dictated the policy of the Wilhelmstrasse, and who, by deliberately traversing the attempts in the opening years of this century to bring about an Anglo-German alliance, burdened his memory with almost as much responsibility for the outbreak of the Great War as must be borne by Admiral Tirpitz. In its way the exposure of Baron Holstein is the most astounding of all the post-war revelations in Germany. Many detested his policy, but no one questioned his personal probity. His admirers said that with his patriotism was an obsession, and politics a fanaticism. To his enemies he was one of those perverse creatures who from behind the scenes pull the strings that move the chief actors on the political stage, but are content to gloat over their power in secret as the miser does over his hoard of gold. That he was seriously influenced by other motives than those of his office not even those most closely associated with his work ever imagined for one moment.

To-day Germany learns with amazement that the real passion of Holstein's life was gambling on the Stock Exchange, and that from day to day, and even from hour to hour, he systematically made speculative use of diplomatic secrets, of which he probably knew more than any other man in Europe. It is no vague, unsubstantiated charge that is raised against the "Grey Eminence," as he was called among his colleagues, after the Capucin Father Joseph. Richelieu's collaborator and inspirer. Never was a sudden, belated, and unexpected accusation more overswhelmingly proved. In its issue of this morning the "Berliner Tageblatt" prints as an opening installment two whole pages of his bank from the Foreign Office with instructions to take advantage of this or that piece of confudential official information for the purpose of this or that investment.

## AMAZING DISCLOSURE

Baron Holstein's peering, spectacled eyes kept an equally alert watch in both homes and foreign politics. Now it would be the assassination of President Carnot, now the impending re-signation of a Prussian Minister, now a confidential report of Professor Koch on the outbreak of cholera in Hamburg to which he directed his broker's attention, with corresponding suggestions that these events should be utilised on the Course. For thirty years hardly a day passed without one of these letters being written. Sometimes there were two or three of them in one day. As appears from the correspondence, they were always sent by ordinary or pneumatic post. Messengers were avoided as potential witnesses to the illicit traffic. Holstein was also extraordinarily cautious in his personal meetings with his banker. On one occasion he wrote that he had intended to visit him, but had turned back on getting the impression that he was being watched. In fact, he observed all the precautions of a practised conspirator, and observed them very effectively, for he was never detected.

These disclosures are all the more astonishing because Holstein was believed to have lived quietly, and to have died poor. In his rumpish old clothes, the thin, grey-beared little bachelor looked like a seedy bookkeeper, desiccated by long years of poring over ledgers. He lived in three sparsely furnished, oil-lighted rooms in a mean street of a not over-reputable quarter of Berlin, where he was looked after by a white-haired housekeeper. Society never saw him in its halls, and his one indulgence was the German midday dinner at a first-class restaurant—he was reputed to be a fastidious epicure. So that any fortune he may have accumulated by his long misuse of his official position was not spent with the profusion that generally accompanies the gambling mania.

EFFECTS OF CIVIL WAR

According to information from the Ministry of Communications, the four Government trunk railways are in a deplorable condition. This is chiefly due, of course, to the civil wars and the declaration of provincial independence by the militants.

The Tientsin Pukow Railway is under three directors; namely: Chang Tso-ju, Chu Yao and Sung Chang-chi, who have been appointed by the Kuominchun, the Shantung Tupan and the Kiansu Tupan respectively, with or without the approval of the Chiaotungpu. From Tientsin trains can proceed as far as Machang only. In Shantung trains can go between Tehehow and Linghang but passengers must change cars at Hang-chuang for Pukow.

Regarding the Peking-Mukden Railway, owing to the recent raids of the Fengtien warships at Taku and Tangku, the bridge at Peitang was destroyed, but it was subsequently repaired by the Kuominchan. From Tientsin to Lanchow, the line is controlled by Mr. Ting, appointed by the first Kuominchan, while the Mukden-Lanchow section belongs to Marshall Chang Tso-lin and has its administrative office at Chingchow, outside Shankalhuan.

The Peking-Hankow Railway has probably suffered more than any other Government line from the civil wars. It is now controlled by Wu Pei-fu and Feng Yu-hsiang. Trains can only reach Chang's intien from Peking and the situation beyond Chang's intien is indescribable. It is stated that Wu Pei-fu has appointed the former Vice-Minister of Communications, Mr. Lao Chichang, as director of the Chang Han Railway, and that an office will be opened at Hankow to control the Hupeh-Hunan section. The Shichiaochuang-Kantanhsien section is being controlled by Shansi troops.

The Peking-Shuiyuan Kowai has been probably, the least disturbed of the railways of China, but now on account of heavy army transportation and the lack of engines, commercial transportation is also impossible, though the line is sound from Peking to Paotouchen.

The receipts of the above-mentioned railways are all misappropriated by the commanders for military purposes and consequently, the Chiaotungpu, which was once the richest Ministry in Peking, cannot now pay its staff regularly, not to mention the repayment of foreign and native loans and obligations.

## GOOD PRICES

Three property sales were held yesterday afternoon, one being at the China Auction Rooms and the other two being at Messrs. Lammert Bros. sales rooms.

At the China Auction Rooms, by order of the mortgagee, Mr. E. V. M. R. de Sousa disposed of two dwelling houses at No. 4, Wing On Street, and No. 3, Gilman Street. This property contains an area of 749 square feet.

The upset price was $16,000, and this was advanced by some very brisk bids of $1,000 and $2,000 until the sum of $37,000 was reached, at which figure the property was knocked down to Mr. Wong Chau.

Another property, situated at No. 13, Man Tsung Terrace (known as No. 29, Wong Nel Chong Road), was also offered for sale at the China Auction Room, but as Mr. de Sousa was unable to inform those present of the area of the property, its sale was postponed until further notice, in order that the area might be ascertained and advertised. At Messrs. Lammert Bros. sales rooms, two lots of leaschold property at Kowloon City were sold by order of the mortgagee.

No. 60, Sal Kung Road, Kowloon City, with an area of 1,238 square feet, and a Crown rental of $5,67, was started at an upset price of $3,500, and with $100 bids acceptable, was knocked down to Mrs. Chan Ying for $4,600.

No. 61, Sai Kung Road, Kowloon City, with an area of 874 square feet, and a Crown rental of $4 was next put up. The upset price was $3,000, and this was advanced by one bid of $100, the property being purchased by Mr. Chung Wing-kui for $3,100.

## SEDITION LAWS

BRITISH GOVERNMENT TO CONSIDER AMENDMENT.

That the Government is considering the question of amending the sedition laws was disclosed by Sir William Joynson Hicks (Home Secretary) when answering a question containing a big array of indictments against the recently-formed Young Communists' League, namely, that its object is to instill into children's minds disloyalty to the Throne and Constitution, in order to prepare for the coming "Red" revolt and to combat all patriotic organisations, like the Girl Guides and the Boy Scouts.

The questioner, Sir William Davison, asked whether legislation would be introduced to protect children from pernicious influences.

Sir William Joynson Hicks averred that he was well aware of the nature of the League's activities, and he was keeping close watch on them.

Labour Members ironically cheered Mr. E. Thurtle (Labour), who facetiously drew attention to the intense interest in the matter in Sir William Davison's West-End constituency, Kensington.

Sir William Joynson Hicks said he thought that the anxiety was not limited to Kensington.—Times of Ceylon.

EDNA MAY RICHMOND

On his deathbed, George W. Hazelting, of Los Angeles, decided to make a will. Lacking other material, he used a piece of his nurse's potticent on which to write the will, leaving the major portion of his estate to Edna May Richmond, his granddaughter.

There may be an election for the seat on the Sanitary Board held by Mr. C. G. Alabaster, whose term has just expired. Nominations close next week, but one is already in that of Dr. F. M. G. Ozorio, who is put up by Mr. J. M. D'Almada Remedios and seconded by Mr. C. A. da Roza. Dr. Ozorio was a member of the Board for nearly nine years, but resigned last year, it will be remembered, when he left the Colony for some months. Mr. Alabaster has not yet announced whether he will be a candidate. The electorate comprises those whose names are in the jury list.
