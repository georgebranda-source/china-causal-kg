## LONDON'S MEAT BILL

Interesting details about London's annual meat bill, which, as shown by supplies to the Central Markets, reached a value £36,500,000 last year, are contained in the report for 1924 of the clerk and superintendent of the Smithfield Markets, Mr. H. G. W. Millman. Last year was a record for the markets, the total supplies (including poultry and provisions) amounting to 480,520 tons, which exceeded anything hitherto reached in the previous fifty-six years' history of the market. The value of the weekly list of retail prices, the issue of which was commenced last November, is commented upon. The list (states the superintendent) forms a price indicator, not only for London, but other parts of the country. It only embraces best quality meats—ewe mutton is not included—and if the people pay more, then they either lack the shopping or marketing sense, are indifferent, or require extra services which justify the retailer charging higher prices. Sections of the retail trade, who were critical, now help in a task which is generally conceded to be in the interests of all sections of the public from producer to consumer. The issue of the retail list should continue, for it affords the public information, and any appreciable fall in wholesale values, or rise, as the case may be, can be adjusted weekly in favour of or against the bearer of all charges—the consumer.

The average of the wholesale prices of Scotch sides of beef during 1924 was about 19.2 per cent above the average prices of other classes of fresh killed beef. Scotch and English mutton prices were lower than the year before, with the advantage in favour of the former, and realised 13.4 per cent higher values than other fresh mutton supplies—mostly Dutch, 72.3 per cent more than New Zealand mutton, and 100 per cent more than mutton from Australia and Argentina. Scotch and English lamb prices for 1924 were somewhat in favour of the home country. Chilled and frozen meat supplies from all sources totalled 317,994 tons. Of the total imports—900,808 tons—of beef, mutton, and lamb into the United Kingdom during the year 36.3 per cent. passed through the market for distribution, this being an increase of 1.2 per cent on 1923.

## MARINE INSURANCE RESULTS

The earlier underwriting accounts of the year gave, it seems, a pretty good index of the state of affairs in marine insurance. The others are merely following on the same track. Of those published in the last week or two the London Assurance, it is true, transfers to profit an amount of £64,000, of which under £40,000 was earned in interest on funds. But that leaves in reserve only £1,000,000, against premiums last year totalling £1,122,000. The premium income of the London Assurance is particularly high, since before the war the annual premiums amounted only to around £400,000.

None of the other companies is so ambitious either as regards premiums or transfers to profit. The Alliance Assurance have put their interest receipts of £45,000 to profit, and on a premium income last year of £510,000 find their reserve a little less than at the end of 1923 at £1,008,000. The Royal Exchange Assurance are keeping their interest receipts in the underwriting account, and even so, on an increased premium income of £635,500, have only managed to increase their reserve by £40,000. It now stands at £792,000. The Phoenix Assurance is another company writing over £1,000,000 in premiums. The account is remarkable for the depletion of the reserve by over £400,000, notwithstanding that the first-year claims settlements for 1924 are rather lower than the average. The claims settled for previous years, totalling nearly £900,000, are very high, and, it would seem, another such year would seriously disturb the account.

## FIRST DIVDEND SINCE 1912

After going dividendless since August 1912, holders of the 6 percent. Cumulative Preference shares of the La Plata Electric Tramways are at last to receive a small return on their investment, the company now announcing the payment of 3 per cent.

In December 1919 the Debenture stockholders agreed to a postponement of the payment of £10,421, representing interest outstanding on January 1, 1920, until January 1, 1923, and Deferred Interest warrants were issued in respect of this amount.

Those warrants were paid off at their due date, thus paving the way for a resumption of Preference dividends.

## THE LANCASHIRE COTTON

It is interesting to learn that Mr. Elmsille, H.M. Trade Commissioner in New Zealand, considers that the Lancashire cotton trade is doing as well as ever it did so far as New Zealand is concerned, and, in fact, is showing gradual signs of development. He avers that the enthusiasm for British goods is such that purchasers will give up to 20 per cent more in order to obtain British-made in preference to foreign products. America and Japan, it is true, are doing their utmost to exploit the market, but Mr. Elmsille believes they are not making any headway, and in the matter of motor-cars and agricultural and dairying machinery in particular there is still considerable scope for the exporter in Great Britain.

## BRITISH SOAP

Not only is the soap consumed in the United Kingdom lower in price and higher in quality than that of any other country, but as a natural result the United Kingdom enjoys a larger export trade in soap to neutral markets of the world that of the total exports of soap of any other three nations added together," said Lord Leverhulme at the annual meeting of Lever Brothers.

Speaking of the present high taxation of Great Britain, he said that against this inequality the British taxpayer gained the advantage of the high standard of exchange. British credit had been maintained, and of the two evils high taxation or low rate of exchange we had chosen wisely in preferring high taxation. But, all the same, he added, British trade and commerce were staggering under this heavy burden.

## THE PHILIPPINES

With Great Britain, the Philippines overseas trade for April, 1925, was valued at P2,902,877, of which P1,916,829 represented the value of exports. Trade with China went over the two-million peso mark, but the Philippine exports for last month amounted to only a little over P500,000. ## ANGLO-PERSIAN IN TH2

Sir Charles Greenway, who has recently returned from Buenos Aires, is reported to have said that he was greatly impressed with the markable progress of that country, but was disappointed by the attitude adopted by the Argentine Government towards foreign capital engaged in the oil fields. The trouble is that some of the lands in the Comodoro Rivadavia field which the Anglo-Persian had acquired, and on which a producing well had been brought in have been included within the limits of the enlarged national oil reserve formed by the Argentine Government. The claims of the Anglo-Persian, which would appear to be strong, have been ignored. The Argentine Government has committed itself to a policy of intensive oil development by the State. The Budget for 1925 includes an appropriation for an increased drilling campaign the construction of further storage tanks, and the purchase of another tank steamship. On its oil operations in 1924 the Government claims to have made a surplus of three million pesos. This has probably counted against Sir Charles Greenway.
