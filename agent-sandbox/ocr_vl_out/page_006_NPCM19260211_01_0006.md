## AWAITING A NEW CHINA

On a former occasion the opportunity was taken in this paper to single out for comment some pertinent utterances by Mr. Silas H. Strawn, the American delegate to the Conferences on Tariff and Extraterritoriality — utterances that rise far above the popular conception of representative American opinions on Chinese topics. Less than a month ago Mr. Strawn delivered a striking address to the students of Tsing Hua College, Peking, in the course of which he spoke with the engaging frankness characteristic of his countrymen. His words formed excellent propaganda material in every sense of the term. He told his hearers, for instance, that he has "thus far seen no convincing evidence that China's present-day troubles are in any degree attributable to the so-called unequal Treaties or to the Imperialistic attitude of the foreign Powers". On the contrary, Mr. Strawn stated that "the evidence seems to be overwhelming that the troubles of China are internal rather than external, and that unequal Treaties, extraterritoriality, tariff autonomy, and Imperialism are political slogans which are availed of by the agitators to excite the people of China into a frenzy of criticism and unrest."

Tactfully, Mr. Strawn proceeded to warn the students against accepting any of these slogans without a most careful study of the facts, and next referred to the part that America is prepared to play, quoting the U.S. Secretary of State's Note to the Chinese Foreign Office on September 8, 1925, which expressed a desire to assist China to realise her aspiration, but emphasised the necessity of giving concrete evidence of China's ability and willingness to enforce respect for the safety of foreign lives and property and to suppress disorders and anti-foreign activation which embittens

## EMPIRE OVERSEAS

It is, declares a writer in the Contemporary Review, no extravagant dream to visualise the Commonwealth as being at some future day, the centre of power of the British Empire.....No such area of land in the history of the world has ever been held by a group of people united by blood ties, and by domestic and national ideals, and among whom there has never been any real discord.....To this group of people small in numbers when they of the Commonwealth are taken into consideration—numbere are to be added. No "hangers-on," but stock of the right sort with a training to equip them for the vast possibilities to be found in Australia. The Prince of Wales's activities since he returned from his last tour, are not without significance. Following on his stirring appeal for sojourners in different parts of the Empire, came the announcement of the establishment of a big Agricultural College—somewhat on the lines of the Hawkesbury College of Australia, to be directed by a former head of that institution—where young men are to be trained for life "down under." The Prince, we are told, "is taking a keen interest in emigration." He has visited the training centre referred to and made himself thoroughly acquainted with the course of instruction to be given. The Prince's interest in this important matter foreshadows an intensive campaign—not merely in the interest of the Dominions concerned and vast numbers of young men—but in the interests of the Empire itself. The policy of a "white" Australia has been rigorously maintained, but it is open to question how long such a policy can be maintained if aggressive measures are not taken to people the place with British people. The Prince's interest will be found to be catching and the good that will result will prove again that H.R.H., like his illustrations, grandfather, is Britain's best ambassador. A thriving Australia is not merely desirable in the interest of Australia itself. There is a growing trade between China and Australia, in which Hongkong takes part, not to be idly passed over. Australia now produces cotton and of course this has been famous for her wool. The sugar cane is grown, fruit abounds. Cattle rearing is but another of the many things carried on in the Commonwealth. The progress of the scheme of training settlers of all classes will be watched with interest as a step in the consolidation of the Empire.

AMERICA AND FAR EAST.

### (Rentor's Service.)

In the House of Lords, Baron Parmoor drew attention to the British subsidy under the Trade Facilities Act to the Silver Line. He said that its ships were undoubtedly employed in the trade between America and Singapore (Straits Settlements). There was a very large carrying trade in the four British lines between these points, besides Japanese ships and ships subsidised by the American Government, and at present there was more than the necessary shipping accommodation.

Earl Plymouth, in replying, said that the Silver Line was given a loan of £117,000 of which the British Government guaranteed the principal and interest. The loan was granted upon the condition that almost the whole proceeds must be utilised in building the necessary ships in the United Kingdom for service between New York and the Far East. The orders had formed a great boon to shipbuilding yards. He pointed out that there was a divergence between shipping and the shipbuilding industry's interests, but the authorities who granted the guarantee had carefully considered the effect of such upon shipping. The Government did not consider that these new ships were likely to interfere with British vessels.

## GOLD SALVAGE

DIVERS WORK ON WRECKED LINER

(British Wireless Service.)

Rugby, Feb. 10.

An interesting statement was made in the House of Commons by Mr. F. C. C. Davidson, Parliamentary Under-Secretary to the Admiralty, that nearly five millions sterling of gold have been recovered by Admiralty divers from the wreck of the White Star liner Laurentic, which was for- pedoed by a German submarine-off the west coast of Ireland in 1917.

The exact value of the gold was 24,958,000, and the cost of salvage was £138,000. This included a bonus of £6,739 paid to the officers and men, who had carried out this task without loss of life or material.

## NEW AIR SERVICES

## CAIRO TO KARACHI ROUTE:

(Reuter's Service.) Amsterdam, Feb.

Sir Sefton Brancker has passed through The Hague on his return from Sweden. In an interview he explained the details of the proposed Cairo-Karachi air route, adding that the service from Karachi to Rangoon would be undertaken by the Government of India.

Sir S. Brancker also discussed with Mr. Mesman, manager of the Dutch Aerial Navigation Company, the possibility of running a service from Singapore to Australia via the Dutch East Indies which, however, would be conditional on the result of negotiations with the Dutch and Dutch East Indies governments.

Later Sir S. Brancker sailed from Rotterdam for America, where he hopes incidentally to meet Fokker, the famous airman.

## AFRICAN CAR TOUR

JOURNEY OF SIXTEEN MONTHS.

(British Wireless Service.)

Rugby, Feb. 10:

The arrival in London yesterday of Major and Mrs. Courtreat, who recently completed the journey from Cape Town to Cairo in Crossley motor-cars, was the occasion of a demonstration of welcome at the Royal Automobile Club.

The expedition, which started in Autumn, 1924 took sixteen months to complete, and it is the first time that the journey has been made by motor.

Although many adventures are recorded by the tourists, and in some parts Mrs. Courtreat was the first white woman to enter them, no great difficulties were encountered during the journey; and, apart from being on one occasion submerged in a river, there were no insuperable mechanical troubles.

Aside from being president of the 'Empress of Scotland' Eastern Star club, Mrs. Edith E. Gattis, Seattle Wash., made a bid for having fame at Algiers when she was plunged into the lay waters of its harbour by an Arab who snatched her purse, then gave her a push. Two Arab boys reached her, but no trace of her purser or her assistant ever was.

## SNOW-BOUND

## UNEVIABLE EXPERIENCES

WILD WEATHER AT SEA.

(Router's American Service.)

The city is snow-bound, the snow being a foot deep in some places. A thin string of motor vehicles is only able to crawl at a snail's pace along the roads, whilst pedestrians are walking in single file along the pavements between the banks of snow five feet high. Many of the main roads outside the city are closed to traffic. Trains on the Eastern States Railway are hours late.

Gangs of unemployed are trying to clear the show, which continues to fall.

Wild weather prevails at sea, but only one wreck has so far been reported, a schooner being driven on the rocks of the Massachusetts coast, when several of the crew were drowned.

## A BRITISH HERO

MEDAL FOR "BRAVEST DEED OF THE YEAR"

(British Wireless Service.)

The Stanhope Gold Medal award by the Royal Humane Society or the "bravest deed of the year," has been given for 1925 to a rimsby fisherman, named Duncan Souter.

He is a member of the crew of the steam trawler "Honoria," which was engaged in fishing 90 miles off Iceland in half a gale with heavy cross seas running. The mate was washed overboard, and was soon far astern of the vessel. He failed to reach the Hefbuoy, and Souter plunged overboard, reached the lifebuoy, swam with it to his unconscious mate, and supported him until the vessel manoeuvred into a position from which it was possible to throw a line, by which both men were eventually got on board.

The Captain states that if the first attempt to throw the line had been unsuccessful it would have been impossible, owing to the wind and heavy seas, for a second attempt to have been in a position to effect a rescue. Extreme risk was incurred, it being impossible to launch a boat.

## SILK IMPORTS

### RUSH TO EVADE THE NEW DUTIES. NO REPETITION

### (Reuter's Service.)

The rush of imports of silk goods last year after the impending imposition of the new tax had been announced in the Budget was the apparent reason for a question put by Sir Frederick Wise (Conservative, Iford) in the House of Commons as to whether such action would in future be circumvented.

The Prime Minister, Mr. Stanley Baldwin, in replying, said: "When the time comes it will be found that we have not overlooked the important matter of dumping."

SPAIN COMES INTO LINE WITH TREATY.

(Reuter's American Service.)

Washington, Feb. 10.

A Treaty with Spain to prevent liquor smuggling has been signed by Mr. Frank A. Kellogg, Secretary of State, and the Spanish Ambassador. It is similar to the Treaties with Great Britain, Italy, and other countries.

Mrs. Salome R. F. Shallcrods of Philadelphia, while taking a carayan trip on the Sahara on New Year's Eve, was left stranded on the desert when her camel became suddenly ill. She was rescued by Harry Pollard, staff photographer of the "Empress of Scotland," when he took a long shot and went back to investigate a dark spot on the horizon.
