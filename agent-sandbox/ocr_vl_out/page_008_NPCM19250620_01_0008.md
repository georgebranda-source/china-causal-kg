## UNAMBIGUOUS

Because the British Government has said little about the growing chaos in China during the past few years, most people have assumed that the Home authorities had lost touch with events in this part of the world. The great speech which Mr. Austen Chamberlain, Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs, delivered in the House of Commons, as cabled in full yesterday, must finally dispel any such feeling. Mr. Chamberlain showed not only that the British Government has been following happenings in China but has been following them very closely. There is no need, with his speech still fresh in the public mind, to recapitulate the main headings. Suffice it to say Mr. Chamberlain established beyond doubt that the present troubles are due to the internal dissensions of the country as carefully exploited by outside mischief-makers, who have turned the discontent of the populace into anti-foreign-feeling in order to further their own evil ends. That much, of course, has been obvious all along to impartial observers in the Far East. Now that Mr. Chamberlain has driven it into the public mind at Home, we may expect to see the nation stand behind the Government in its determination to protect British lives and property in China as much as any non-war

Mr. Chamberlain was explicit upon the point. "There can be," he said, "no weakness, no hesitation, no doubt." The protecting of British lives and property was the fundamental duty of every British Government and the present British Government would discharge that duty. In taking this stand, Mr. Chamberlain soon found that he had the support of the entire House of Commons, the Labour leaders losing no time in announcing their approval of this part of his speech. After this stern warning, delivered with a united voice, it is to be hoped that the irresponsible elements in China who are allowing themselves to be used as the tools of outside mischief-makers will pause to ponder the possible consequences of their reckless folly. Britain is the staunch friend of China—would she, for instance, forego the Boxer Indemnity were it otherwise?—and every effort that she can make to put the country on its feet will be made. Therefore it is for the real China, the practical, hard-working, labourious and industrious China of Mr. Chamberlain's speech, to see that a handful of malicious agitators furthering the ends of outside mischief-makers are not permitted to estrange and embitter two nations who have so much in common for the good of the world.

## A Strike-Moral

The general recognition of the greater benefit to be derived from close personal contact between pupils and those who have devoted their lives to the training of the youth and adolescent is shown by the increasing growth of public boarding schools throughout the world and the, even greater demand for the amenities they provide. The parent who made his own way in life looks back on a youth devoid of friendship with others older and more experienced than himself and is actuated by the resolve that his children shall have the opportunity of availing themselves of what he missed; while the parent who experienced it himself realises its value too well to think of denying to his children a privilege to which he owes the formation of habits of mental exercise which are an ever increasing source of enjoyment to himself. There are boarding schools in Hongkong modelled on English public school lines and there are in these and other schools here as the type of master as the staffs of the best public schools in England are made up of. It is more than likely that it is the feeling of loyalty to these masters which has resulted in so much comparative restraint having been exercised among the students of Hongkong during the present crisis and it is significant

that the only trouble of any magnitude has been at the chief Government School where there is no hostel, and where the application of the authorities for the provision of boarding accommodation has been persistently refused. It is no reflection on a staff which is well known to be as fine a mentally equipped body of educational enthusiasts as one could hope to meet in a college of its kind to state that it is not surprising that the seeds of disaffection should have found richer soil in the minds of youths who are free (so far as college supervision is concerned) to roam the street at any hour of the day or night when they are not actually engaged in their studies. No great harm has been done up to the present, however, and as they have not received the support from other schools they are believed to have been led to expect it is probable that a few days will see the main body of students back at their studies; if not there would be little difficulty in making up the numbers of even as large a body of the students as are affected judging from the waiting lists. The trouble should not have been without its lesson, however, and for the reasons stated above the Government might well reconsider their decision regarding the provision of a hostel for Queen's College.

## Public Baths

The first care of the Municipal Council of Hongkong—which will be the first evidence of the fruits of reform—will undoubtedly be the provision of public baths in the city as well as wash-houses. Room will have to be found for them and the razing of slums to that end will be a step in the right direction. This measure of necessity will result in a saving. It will save water—at any rate so far as the "washing of clothes is concerned. Study a person cleaning garments at a street fountain and agree. There will be a saving in the matter of discomfort. The ordinary Chinese has no facilities for the daily scrub which is the order of most healthy-minded, and bodied people here. Glance at a row of houses or flats, try and imagine the cubicles into which they are divided, the number of people occupying them and the lack of decent facilities, and agreement will be reached. The Government, it is alleged, is getting out of its educational work very cheaply. We have yet to hear the Government's point of view and the amount it has given in building grants and valuable sites practically free of charge and at nominal crown rents. But no one questions that on certain matters of ordinary municipal routine it gets out of doing what it should do at no cost at all. Think of public baths and washhouses at Home, or to come a little nearer, those of Japan—and again reach agreement.

## TO GOLF OR NOT TO GOLF

They do not even try to think.

For some time past serious persons have objected to the game of golf, notes the "Daily Telegraph" (London.) Mr. Leo Maxse, who has just matched himself against the Earl of Balfour to denounce it, is not original or lonely in that occupation. Men may be heard at it any day and upon any links, and they are far more fervent and free than Mr. Maxse's courtesy permitted to him. But he argued his case, which they do not. Britons, he urged, should never be slaves to golf, first, because it was made in Germany; secondly, because it has a debilitating influence upon the younger generation. The evidence which Mr. Marse has been so fortunate as to discover that the Teutonic Knights played golf in the intervals of breaking the heathen Prussians we shall be glad to see. Meantime, we propose to continue to blame Scotland. The second part of the indictment lacks the element of surprise. Five hundred years ago it was discovered that golf was doing Scotsmen a lot of harm. The state of that unfortunate nation must by this time be deplorable. In 1457 the Scottish Parliament "decreted and ordained that the fute-bal and the golfe be vtterly cryit downe and's noet vsyt." It was desired that the youth of the country should give their time to military training and particularly shooting. For Scotsmen have always, with strange modesty, held that the English were superior to them in the use of the long bow. But the ancient Parliament was no more convincing than Mr. Marse. Nobody seems to have taken any notice of its decree. It tried again, and "ordت in that in no place of the realme there be usit fute-ball, golfe, or uthar sik unprofitabil sports." Thus early was the horrible seductive power of the game made manifest. Mr. Maxse thinks that its essential vice is proceed by the kind of men who are attracted to it, "politicians, journalists, international financiers, mugwumps, mandarins, bureaucrats, and highbrows." We could go on in this strain for years together, "dinners and suppers and sleeping times excepted." What game was it that Mary of Scots was found playing after her husband had been in suspicious circumstances, blown up? Golf, of course. Who brought golf to England? Not Lord Balfour, but that "wlest fool in Christendom," James I. They were great golfers in that unhappy House of Stuart. Obviously that accounts for their misfortunes, and perhaps also for their fatal charm.

But there is, after all, something serious in Mr. Maxse's charges. Golf, he contends, is a good game for the elderly and the sedentary, but it is not good enough for anyone else. Boys and young men have taken it up, and thus surrendered our old English supremacy in sport. To this Lord Balfour replies that golf is not largely played by our public schools, and that so far as we have ceased to be supreme in games it is because the other nations have now at length learnt to play. The answer is not wholly adequate. Though golf is not played by a large number of boys and young men who are perfectly fit to play something faster and more strenuous. Most people who know anything of the training of physique and character would allow that young fellows ought to have a course of some hard team game. No other sport, however good for muscular development, for adroitness of body, and quickness of eye, will supply the place of vigorous team work. We have, of course, many current examples that golf and other games are not, mutually exclusive. A man may, and, in fact does, get his blue for "Rugger" or "Soccer," and play golf for his University. But probably there is some truth in the suggestion that golf tempts a good many boys to desert "the flannelled fools at the wicket and the muddied oafs at the goals." Why should a lad not play the game which he likes best, which, in general, will be that most suited to him? We fear that it is vain to call, as Mr. Maxse would, all the young golfers back to cricket in order that we may recover the ashes. It is confusion of thought to assume that we play games in order to beat other nations. The object of playing a game is not "international supremacy," but amusement, exercise, health of body and mind. We send our best men to make a match with the best of other nations in order to keep the game strenuous and skilful. It is surely plain that a nation which gets about the winning of international games as if that were a chief object of sport may easily achieve supremacy without a high general standard of skill or physique or sportsmanship. A concentration of interest on a few matches and a few players is not necessarily good for a game or a nation. What we should rather aim at is that every body should be playing something, and playing it for sport, not as a prescription or a task or a business. It does not seem likely that for the years in which one is a trifle slower or foot and stiffer of shoulder and shorter of wind a better game will be invented than golf.
