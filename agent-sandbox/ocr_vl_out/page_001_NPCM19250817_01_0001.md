## END IN SIGHT?

FOREIGNERS IN CANTON

AGAIN.

AMEEN'S GATES.

News reached Hongkong on Saturday that Shameen's gates had been re-opened and that intercourse between the French and British Concessions and Canton city had been resumed.

Shameen is, of course, connected to the mainland by two bridges. The “east” bridge is that leading into the French Concession and the “west” bridge links up the British Concession with Shakee.

It was stated that the decision had been arrived at to allow Chinese who had, at the time of the Yunnanese siege, deposited valuables and other property in Shameen for safe keeping, to withdraw whatever they wished. Another part of the report to hand was that domestic and other Chinese employees, formerly of Shameen, had indirectly signified their intention to return as opportunity occurred.

On inquiry from the best informed source in the Colony, it was learned by the “China Mail” this, morning that Shameen's gates are still closed. Their reopening had not even been discussed, nor would such a step be contemplated until adequate protection was given by the Chinese authorities on the spot. Shameen was still, virtually, in a state of siege. Nothing had been done to change conditions.

However, Canton's "gates," meaning the right of entry into the city, were opened on Saturday. It is surmised that the report referred to mistook Shameen for Canton. Foreigners, with the exception of British, are now allowed in Canton for purposes of ordinary intercourse. The supposition is that a beginning has been made to restore trade.

Trustworthy Chinese business circles express the opinion that the trouble, as far as Canton was concerned, would "blow over" in less than three weeks from to-day. A Chinese merchant with large interests in Canton predicted to a "China Mail" reporter yesterday that most of the Chinese-owned river steamers, if no others, would be running between Canton and Hongkong again, by the 15th of the 7th moon if not earlier, the equivalent of "that date" being September 2. He had reason to think that the huge army of unemployed in Canton were getting restless, that the "professional labour bosses" were becoming alarmed as to the outcome, and that negotiations had been made by those indirectly interested but not directly concerned, for river shipping to move again, with the object of facilitating a gradual return to normalcy.

Macao Steamer Back.

This afternoon, the s.s. Chuen Chow (Captain Jones) put in at Hongkong again after having tied up at Macao for the second or third time since the strike began. For a reason which has not been given, the crew have not made the voyage, lying up and then making a fresh start again.

Poultry and Livestock.

Housewives were pleasantly surprised yesterday morning when hawke s offered fowls in the street at 65 cents per catty, which is about equal to 50 cents per pound, for live chickens, and capons at a slightly lower figure. Later in the day the price went down to 58 cents per catty which is about the figure in ordinary times. This sudden drop is attributed to a very large shipment having arrived. The source has not been divulged, but the Chinese are particularly grateful for this bit of enterprise.

Arrivals of cattle, pigs and other livestock have also been reported, but the market prices for meat are still prohibitive.

## CANTON WARFARE

OFFENSIVE AGAINST WAICHOW.

TROOPS MOVING RAPIDLY.

From Our Own Correspondent.)

(Received via Shekki junk and

## Macao)

Canton is despatching a strong expedition to quell Waichow. The force which is to carry out the offensive consists partly of Whampoa Cadets and partly of General Hsu Shung-chi's divisions. This punitive army might have been greater had not the necessity arisen of keeping an eye on recalcitrant generals nearer to Canton.

Waichow city, far up the East River, is in the hands of Commander Yeung Kwan-yu who has several thousand bayonets in his command. Owing to the complexity of Cantonese politics he has, within the last year, been a subordinate of Chan Kwing-ming, the Yunnanese and Hsu Shung-chi, three factions which are opposed to one another. Latterly he has held Waichow, nominally under the Kuomintang. But when Canton sent a delegate to control his actions he rebelled. It is with the object, either of bringing Yeung to reason, or disarming his following, that the punitive force has been sent. This force, it is stated, has reached Sheklung.

En route, the Canton army may encounter the guerilla troops of Yuen Ha-kau ("Coffin Sampan"). Yuen now roaming the hills near Tsang Shing and a fugitive from the Whampoa Cadets.

Should Canton take Waichow, as they did in the last campaign when they bought over a Lieut.-General and Commander Yeung fled, then the other leaders of Chan Kwing-ming's army, in and around Swatow, may also be embroiled but it is safe to assume that they will not take to the field unless they are threatened.

## NO BOYCOTT

## HOIHOW SHIPPING UNFETTERED

RESIDENT'S REPORT.

The reports as to a trade boycott of British and Japanese goods being prosecuted with vigour at Hoihow would appear to be without foundation for a resident of Hoihow who arrived in Hongkong by the Talkwa Maru (leaving Hoihow on Friday morning), reports that a full cargo was brought to Hongkong, this consisting of 230 head of cows, over 200 hogs and large numbers of chickens.

This resident reports that there is absolutely no attempt on the part of the students to prevent shipment of freight either to Hongkong or Hoihow and states that when the boat left shipments were arriving regularly from Haiphong, Hongkong and Bangkok.

There had been a boy in the town of Hali-man Island, according to our informant, is determined that none shall occur. He has arrested and executed four or five students who were known to be Reds from Canton and were attempting to stir up trouble.

There is absolutely no anti-foreign-feeling at Holhow, according to this same source, and the women and children who left for Holhow were all now returned. The women and children of question set a banana cell on their own in Canton and no one could have brought them to the home.

## TRAMWAY COMPANY

## SOCIAL GATHERING AT THE DEPOT

VOLUNTEERS THANKED.

A volunteer worker of the Tramway Company during the strike writes as follows:—

Eleven o'clock yesterday morning saw the disbandment of the volunteer car drivers and conductors when a group photograph was taken, including the tramway officials.

In a short speech, just after the photograph was taken, Mr. L. C. F. Bellamy, the General Manager, on behalf of the Tramway Company, said that they were very sorry to lose such a fine body of men whose loyal co-operation helped them to see the strike through thick and thin, besides contributing in no small measure towards organising and training new men to undertake permanent work. It was, he said, with mixed feelings that he parted with the volunteers, and he thought—although he hoped nothing untoward will happen to have recourse to their services again—it would be much more appropriate to say "Au revoir" than "Goodbye." He also paid a tribute to the very efficient manner in which the volunteers carried out their duties. Mr. Bellamy's speech was received with much applause and hand clapping, after which the gathering dispersed.

I understand that the Tramway Company intends to present the volunteers with autographed copies of the group photo, and I think I voice the sentiments of all the volunteers when I say that we shall cherish the same as a memento of an unusual and extraordinary incident in our lives.

In closing, I beg, on behalf of my co-workers, to tender the tramway officials our very hearty thanks for the gentlemanly and gallant treatment, accorded to us during our term of service.

## LIBEL ACTION

CHINESE PAPER TO PAY

UNRESERVED WITHDRAWAL.

Allegations against the morals and honesty of the Chinese manager of the Kwangtung Industrial Bank are contained in an article appearing in a local Chinese newspaper on June 10, in respect of which legal proceedings were instituted this morning.

No defence was offered, but the printer—and publisher of the newspaper had already submitted a withdrawal and apology and agreed to pay $5,000 and costs, and this morning's proceedings were for an order to be made along those lines by the Chief Justice.

Mr. Elsley Zeitlyn (instructed by Mr. G. K. Hall Brutton) was for the plaintiff (Ching Pikkam), and Mr. Y. K. Mok (instructed by Messrs. Lo and Lo) for the defendants (Lai Ki-nam).

The newspaper in question was the “Tai Kwong Po,” and following is the article in question, which appeared in the issue of June 10:—

“Since Tang Pun-yan has had the intention of carrying into effect the ‘great eight districts’ policy he is making a display of the influence of the eight districts very actively. Recently he has organised the ‘King Ngai’ Bank as well. Banking is, in fact, a business for the benefit of the people, but to the eyes of the militarists it only serves as a means to suck the marrow of the people. Moreover, the chief manager of the Bank appointed by Tang is a fickle young man, Ching Pikkam. Ching had been a private secretary to Li Lit-kwan (Li Lieh-chun) and is a favourite of Yeung Yan-ping. In the second year of the Chinese Republic, 1913, through the recommendation of Yeung, he was employed as a proof reader in the “Kwok Man Yat Po” at Singapore. As Ching was addicted to the cup and woman he stole over $1,000 from the funds of the said newspaper and absconded to Hongkong with a female. For this reason the said newspaper nearly stopped publication. Now Tang has appointed this man as chief manager to the Bank and it may be inferred that they will help each other in the commission of evils by defrauding the people and causing a mess to the money market of the eight districts. At present on hearing the news the overseas Chinese residents who are natives of the eight districts are proposing to lodge a protest by sending telegrams to Tang.”

In submitting defendant's apology and the conditions contained in it which met with the plaintiff's requirements, Mr. Zejtlyn said that the stipulation with regard to publication in two English Hongkong papers was made because the Bank in respect of which the plaintiff was manager made its appeal to both European and Chinese clients. Following is the apology, signed by defendant and addressed to

"You have commenced an action against me, as printer and publisher of the "Tai Kwong" Newspaper, claiming damages for libel contained in an article published by the said "Tai Kwong" Newspaper on June 10, 1925, which article is set out in your statement of claim in the above action." I now confess that the statements relating to yourself contained in the said article are absolutely untrue and without the slightest substance in fact.

I hereby unreservedly withdraw every word in that article which in any way reflects on your character. I admit that there never was any foundation for any of the charges contained in the article. I deeply regret that they were ever made, and I tender you my sincere apologies therefor. I agree to pay the sum of $5,000 as damages and in addition your costs as between solicitor and client, and consent to judgment being entered therefor and to this apology being read out in open court and embodied in the judgment. I also undertake to publish this apology in the "Tai Kwong" Newspaper on three consecutives.

## HANKOW'S POSITION

## ANOTHER FRUITLESS TALK

### (Reuter's Service.)

A further discussion between the British Consul-General and the Chinese Commissioner for Foreign Affairs, regarding the defence of the Concession has had no result and was adjourned.

## HIS MASTER'S CAR

WAS HE JOY RIDING OR

TESTING IT?

JNDIAN CHAUFFEUR'S CASE.

Taking his master's cur out with his own friends in it and without permission was the reason given in the Summary Court this morning for the dismissal of an Indian chauffeur who claimed his wages for July up to the 23rd., the day on which he was dismissed, and a month's wages in lieu of notice ($106 in all).

The car was a 1925 model Buick seven seater, supplied to the plaintiff's master (Mr. Shewaran) by Mr. A. H. Rowe, managing director of the Hongkong and Kowloon Taxicub Company. It was the latter who had seen the chauffeur out with the car, and had informed the owner accordingly.

The plaintiff said that he had had to make minor adjustments in the mechanism of the car and had gone out on the occasion in question to test whether they held good or not. The man whom Mr. Rowe had told the Court he had seen sitting on the door of the car was there because he did not wish to dirty the car with his clothes.

Mr. Rowe, in evidence, said that the car being a new one with a six month's guarantee, he had told the chauffeur, whom he had personally introduced to the owner, that he was to come to him (witness): if anything was wrong and not to attempt to make repairs on his own account. This was done in accordance with the wishes of the owner.

Mr. Howe further said that the car was being driven very fast when he saw it in Stubbs Road on the day in question. It had to pull up near the Bowen Road turn and the back wheels skidded for some distance.

Mr. H. S. Fitzroy, for the chauffeur, suggested that the man on the footboard or sitting on the door might have been listening for some slight defect in the engine, but witness said that no one who knew anything about motoring would suggest such a thing. The wind would be whistling past the ears of a person in that position. On being informed that the chauffeur had said that he paid witness commission for introducing him to the buyer of the car Mr. Rowe stated that this was an absolute fabrication. The case is proceeding.
