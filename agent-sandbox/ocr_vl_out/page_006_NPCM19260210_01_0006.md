### Hongkong, Wednesday, Feb. 10, 1926

With the arrival of the mammoth liner “Empress of Scotland” on a cruise round the world Hongkong is once again definitely placed on the map in spite of certain fears some months ago that the little dot at the east of South China had been inadvertently erased by the “brass hats” at the Foreign Office and the Colonial Office. Our Colony has no pretensions to be a miniature or secondary Palm Beach, nor can it possess the attraction of, say, Blackpool in the Summer. Yet, there are things to be seen here that are the envy of Tourist Bureaux all over the world. Our harbour is sufficient even to make the teeth of a Sydney native water. The Peak, with its unique tramway, would be an asset worth millions of dollars were it only possible for our go-ahead American cousins to transport it bodily to one of their great holiday resorts. Our roads, both on the Island and the mainland, cause visitors from Australia, where are some of the worst highways of traffic in the whole world, to wonder how they were made and what is the secret of their marvellous maintenance. And in few other places in an Orient full of curios and antiques can so many pleasant hours be passed admiring and bargaining for those little things that speak for ever of their association with “foreign parts”. For these and many other reasons Hongkong well deserves a place on the map —and a place, too, considerably larger than the impression conveyed by that little dot on the China Coast!

Hongkong, of course, has been "discovered" long before this by the geniuses who arrange world tours by huge liners. The hundreds and thousands of passengers who have been brought hither from almost every corner of the globe have done propaganda work almost as valuable in their way as the Wembley Exhibition in adver-

tising Hongkong. They have sent on delightful heralds in the shape of picture post cards; they have despatched glowing epistles of their sight-seeing here; and ever afterward they have cherished a warm spot in their hearts for this particular gem of the British Empire's outposts.

The experiences that the passengers of the "Empress of Scotland" pass through whilst they are in the Colony may not be dissimilar to those brought hither by previous tourist liners, but there will have the charm of novelty to them, and hence they may be expected to go on their way rejoicing that the enterprise of the Canadian "Pacific Company" enabled them not only to see Hongkong from the vantage point of the liner's decks but to step ashore and spend four full days drinking in for themselves all the delights that are offering. The old phrase "See Venice and die" may well be paraphrased "See Hongkong and your impressions will never die."

Signor Mussolini trailing his coat is not an 'edifying spectacle. Some day, some one will stand on it, and the consequent rent will be great. Let not this obscure the saner side of Italian politics or desires. There is a story that Italy was considering suggesting to the League of Nations that certain cities be considered as cosmopolitan monuments of art, and as such be declared immune from bombing attack in war. Italy would present as such cities Venice, which suffered the loss of historical edifices and frescoes by Titian and Trepolo in the late war, Florence, Pisa and Rome. Here is a sensible suggestion. It should appeal to every art lover, every student of history, every admirer of the ancient and the beautiful. If adopted, the idea would seriously affect the practice of war. In war your object is to do as much damage and destruction as possible. The chasteness — if we may so describe it— of our own immaculate City Hall would have no effect or appeal on an invader. It would probably go. St. Paul's Cathedral, the Abbey, and other historical monuments also would disappear in the application of a programme of frightfulness. The last war demonstrated that and there is no need to enlarge on it. The suggestion is not likely to have a universal appeal. Florence is a small town packed with art treasures, while Paris is a great city with quite as much to arouse the reverence of the art lover. If one is given immunity, so should the other. If the plan for granting immunity to some is adopted, it is likely to spread immensably—and war-makers would not desire that. Common sense is not usually allied with war. ## CORRESPONDENCE

A SANITARY MATTER.

(To the Editor of the China Mail).

Sir,—I ask your pardon for broaching a matter such as the disposal of nightsoil in the public press but as a resident of some years' standing. I must confess that it was with considerable surprise that I learnt the other day, my own arrangements in connection with the matter having failed, that it is possible for householders or business firms to have the services of the Government contractor if they get into touch with the Sanitary Department.

I have myself fixed up with the latter and his letter is intended in no way as a slight or grumble as to anything, but simply to ask you if for the benefit of householders, and business houses for that matter, it would not be possible for a public announcement to be made as to the liabilities of such in the matter. The arranging for coolies to do the work had proved itself a nuisance to me and if it is possible to get the thing done efficiently for one, it is as well that everybody should be informed exactly where they stand.

## Yours, etc

### Hongkong Feb. 10

From enquiries, it is learned that there is a clause in the contract of the Government's contractor which makes him liable, if called upon, to attend any building and perform the work at certain rates. This can be arranged through the Sanitary Department. The reason why the matter is usually attended to by the householders themselves is that rates cheaper than those fixed for the Government contractor are quoted by the "ring" of smaller contractors with whom it is easy to get into touch.

As regards the Peak, the Government contractor is responsible for the disposal of excreta and the only charge on householders there are certain incidentals in connection with its collection.

The rates charged by the Government contractor for the lower levels vary with the type of building, European or Chinese, domestic, tenement, offices, hospitals, hotels, etc. Above the level of Bonham Road, Gaine Road, Albert Road and Kennedy Road, the charge is $2 a month for European houses and at or below the same level it is $1.50 a month, provisos applying to places where bathrooms exceed four in number. This includes servants' quarters. —Ed. "China Mail."

## GERMANY'S ENTRY

## CLAIM TO ASSIST LEAGUE'S PROBLEMS

(Reuter's Service.)

Berlin, Feb. 9.

A semi-official note, with reference to Germany's application for admission to the League of Nations depreciates cherishing illusory hopes of what Germany can attain through membership, but declares that Germany can work within the League on international questions like the revision of impracticable treaties, whose maintenance endangers the peace of the world, and co-operate in universal disarmament.

Furthermore, the League has taken over a number of special problems which are vitally important to Germany like the administration of the Saar, the security of Danzig, racial minorities, and colonial mandates, a share in which the German people justifiably claims.

## FLOWER SHOW

## ENTIRELY NEW FEATURE ANNOUNCED

## AMATEUR CUP

An entirely new feature in the Flower Show to be held on March 11 will be a small cup presented by Mr. J. A. Plummer which is to be known as the "Amateur" Cup.

The following notice will shortly be sent out to all members of the Horticultural Society setting forth the conditions under which this cup is to be competed for, but there may be many small but enthusiastic gardeners in the Colony who are members of the Society, and whose attention is likewise invited. The notice reads:

Mr. J. A. Plummer is very kindly offering a small cup for the best exhibit of pot plants entered in any of the open or Peak classes by members who have not exhibited before or who have never won a prize at any Flower Show in Hongkong.

In offering the prize Mr. Plummer wishes to encourage small gardeners, particularly those who tend to their own flowers or keep only one gardener.

Special tickets will be issued on application to those wishing to compete for this cup on which they will be asked to make the following declaration:—

In the event of there being not more than two entries, no award will be made.

It is hoped that this special prize will encourage the small and hitherto unsuccessful exhibitor to compete regularly at the annual Shows.

Mr. E. B. O. Hornell, Hon. Secretary, will be pleased to supply any further information that may be required by intending exhibitors for the Amateur Cup.
