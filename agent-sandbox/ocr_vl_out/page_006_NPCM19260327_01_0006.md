## DEATH

TAGGART.—On March 26, 1926, at sea, Lilian M. Taggart, the wife of Mr. J. H. Taggart.

Hongkong, Saturday, March 27, 1926.

Rimful of interest and of fond expectations have been the local events of the past week. Premier place must, of course, be given to the important intimation by His Excellency the Governor in regard to the willingness of the Canton Government to meet three officials from Hongkong to resume the negotiations in regard to ending the strike and the boycott. The question may properly be regarded now as sub-judice, and therefore it might be imprudent or at least impolitic to indulge in comment for the present. This remark may also apply with equal force to the retrospect of the boycott contained in the General Chamber of Commerce annual report and likewise to the disclosures made during the week of the brutal treatment meted out to two Indian watchmen who were captured and held in chains in the strikers' prison. In the same category of discreet silence may also be placed the coup effected by General Chang Kai-shek regarding which rumours have been as thick as leaves in Vallombrosa.

In purely domestic affairs the annual meeting of the General Chamber of Commerce has been the most important event of the week, particularly as His Excellency created a new departure on the part of local Governors by attending and delivering an excellent address. Earlier in the week His Excellency associated himself with the humanitarian work of the St. John Ambulanee Corps when he invested Mr. Ho Komtong with the insignia of a Knight of Grace of the Order of Saint John and presented a number of medals and shields. The investi

ture and the practical sympathy of His Excellency ought to prove a real stimulus to ambulance work in the Colony. Early in the week the appointment of a new Colonial Secretary to succeed Sir Claud Severa was announced, the gentleman to be selected being Mr. W. T. Southorn, of the Ceylon Service. The date of his probable arrival in the Colony has not been announced yet. Interest was attached to the annual meeting of the Alice Memorial Hospital, when a record of good work was disclosed, and an appeal made for further funds, to which it is to be hoped there will be a ready response. The enterprise of the Directors and General Manager of the Hongkong Tramways was exemplified by the trial trip of a new tramcar of modern design. Motoring also came into the limelight at the annual meeting of the Hongkong Automobile Association, a body which is making considerable headway and doing much toward making motor traffic safer alike for the motorist and the mere pedestrian. Three Company meetings were held—the Hongkong Engineering and Construction Company, the Hongkong Rope Company, and the Fire Insurance Association. The first named concern showed considerable losses on Government contracts; the Rope Company revealed reduced profits and the “passing of the dividend”; and at the Fire Insurance meeting strong criticism was levelled at the Fire Brigade, particularly in regard to the insufficient European personnel. It was announced to-day that the Rents Ordinance will be repealed on June 30. The sympathy of all residents will be extended to Mr. J. H. Taggart, of the Hongkong- and Shanghai Hotels, Ltd., on the occasion of the death of his wife on her way back to the Colony.

"Nothing doing" is becoming a hackneyed phrase in regard to Imperial questions. The fiscal bogey with raised in the House of Commons by one worthy Conservative member, who desired the Government to commit itself to the policy of preference for British goods from the Colonial Possessions. The Secretary of State for the Colonies was called upon to give him information that he might have procured for himself in any standard reference book, including the "intelligence" that, save for two articles, Hongkong is a free port.

Internationally, Geneva has again filled the bill to the almost utter exclusion of everything else, if an exception be made in regard to the effort of Senator Borah to revive the Americans' claims for alleged violations of neutrality during war time. before the United States decided to throw in her lot with the Allies. As regards Geneva, Sir Austen Chamberlain was able to vindicate himself before a critical Opposition in the House of Commons, although not exactly able to placate a still more hostile Press throughout the country.



In Northern China the Kuominchun has received a severe setback in the civil war-raging there. The presence of Russian cavalry seems to have helped them little so far, although it may be premature to predict the ultimate result in a warfare begotten of the spirit of militarism and not of the sentiment of real patriotism.

## A NEW REWARD

The Freedom of the City of London is rightly regarded as a great honour. Those on whom that honour has been conferred are to be numbered amongst the nation's great personalities. Other cities in Britain have a similar honour at their disposal. Generally it is the greatest distinction any city can confer. The suggestion is that Hongkong might very well institute an order of the Freedom of the Colony, to be given in meritorious cases. Honours in this part of the world are severely limited. Legislative Councillors, as a rule, live long and remain so in an honourable office. Justices of the Peace are big in number, but like the lifes of the field, they toil not, neither do they spin. The University — a semi-Government institution — confers honorary degrees. At this, the recital must stop. There is clearly need for a fresh distinction, to which all irrespective of creed or class would be eligible. And just, as the University does not confine its honorary graduates to those within the Colony, but honours the worker in different fields of endeavour, so the Colony would not necessarily restrict its "Freedom" to its own residents, although it is as a "home" honour that we think of it given not only to members of the foreign community, but to others who have done something in social or public service to merit the thanks and esteem of the community.

Strictly speaking, it would not be correct to say that the present outbreak of rabies in Hongkong is getting any worse as there have been no fresh developments of an alarming nature. On the other hand, the situation cannot be said to be better, as improvement can only be recorded when there are no more suspected cases.



As far as is known three persons have died. One was a Chinese boy, 14 years of age, whose mother took no action after he had told her that he had been attacked by a strange dog. As reported in the "China Mail," the boy was taken to hospital when he was in the last stages of hydrophobia, when little could be done for him.

One lesson to be emphasized is that there is much of which the ignorant classes, especially in a place like Hongkong, should be informed. A Chinese man, 40 years of age, was bitten by his own dog. Several days afterward, the animal died in strange and suspicious circumstances. Even then, the owner did nothing. When he had violent convulsions, and it was plain that he had contracted hydrophobia, he was taken to hospital. As was to be expected, he succumbed.

The third case concerned: the death of a 16-year-old Chinese boy. Altogether seven dogs have died of rabies during the present outbreak. Six belonged to the island of Hongkong, and the other was from Fanling.

Immediate Action Urged.

When a dog actually shows symptoms of rabies there is very little hope for it: Accordingly, the importance of owners—in their own and public interests—talking immediate action cannot be emphasised too strongly.

As soon as it is known that a dog has come in contact with a suspected canine, it should be kept under observation at Kennedy Town. If it has rabies it has to be destroyed.

regions are given wide discretionary powers in such matters, every dog is given the utmost chance.

There was a private kennel at Shatin in which one of the dogs was bitten by a strange wonk. It was suspected and ultimately died, at the Kennedy Town compound, of the disease. Other dogs that it had come into contact with were very valuable. They were kept in their own kennels at first. Then, when it was established that the other one had rabies, they were taken away and separated for six months. At the end of the period all cause for doubt had been removed, and the animals were handed back to the owners.

It is only by the loyal co-operation of the public that the authorities can hope to stamp out the disease. Even the ignorant would prefer to have daily injections given to their dogs for a number of days, rather than that fatal consequences should ensue.
