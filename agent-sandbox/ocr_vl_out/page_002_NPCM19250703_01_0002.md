## Hongkong, Friday, July 3, 1925

## JUSTICE AND FAIRPLAY

Sir Oracle hath spoken. That oracle is David Z.T. Yui, General Secretary of the National Committee of the Y.M.C.A. for China, who has the temerity to address an open letter to the public dealing with the situation in China and a possible way out of the tangle in which the Chinese students and rioters have got themselves and the Chinese nation.

The letter is not worth repulsion in full. Some of the points therein do not coincide with facts. Some of them raise issues which cannot be settled by means of an "open letter" to the public. Condensed, his plea is merely for a special commission of inquiry to be appointed jointly by the British and Chinese authorities. He praises the Chinese for their "love"

"love of fairplay," which should not stand in the way of Chinese being on the Commission. Lastly, he suggests an invitation to "some other fair-minded nationals" to join them on the Commission which he proposes to create.

Evidently Mr. David Z. T. Yui realises that the Chinese students, rioters, and strikers have done China the worst turn for many a long year, and that it is time to do something constructive to stay the rot. That is the impression gained from his penultimate paragraph in his "open letter," which reads as under:—

May we pause for a moment to think of the terrible hardships and sufferings which we are imposing upon ourselves, the amount of self-sacrifices to no good, the real dangers to peace and order not only in the Far East but in the entire world, and the tremendous damage to the international friendships involved? Do we not desire to free us from all these at the earliest possible moment? We believe without doubt that it is altogether within our power either to prolong and aggravate the tense enough situation or to clarify and end it within a very short time. Are we not wise, just, courageous, and large-hearted enough to choose to do the constructive and fundamental and therefore more difficult-ish?

That admission from an educated Chinese—“the terrible hardships and sufferings which we are imposing upon ourselves”—is worth noting in any discussion of the situation. Likewise the Chinese gentleman in question believes that it is “without doubt within the power of the Chinese either to prolong and aggravate the tense enough situation or to clarify it and end it within a very short time.” If Mr. David Z. T. Yui would go a step further and endeavour to inculrate some of these rational ideas into his compatriots in Shanghai and Peking, he would be performing a meritorious service for China. Propaganda of this kind is really more required in the big central of China than outside them. Let him get on with the good work in this direction alone, and leave international issues arising out of what he terms “the tragic event” or may go to be senior berwer China and the interested Power in the hours.

The Government in the Lord.

If the Prime Minister ever has occasion to demonstrate to one of his young men that none of us is indispensable he need only conduct the Minister concerned to the House of Lords. There, till his recent death, Lord Curzon held undisputed sway and few, if any, members of the Upper House looked for the emergence of a comparable party leader within a few months. The prevailing exception was that it would take two men to fill Lord Curzon's place —Lord Salisbury's almost aggressive virility coupled with the mellowing influence of Lord Balfour. Mr. Baldwin thought otherwise, however, and none who has listened to the Earl of Balfour leading the House of Lords can doubt that his appointment to the senior post, at the age of 77, has been fully justified. I have watched him digest a Foreign Office dossier on a question relating to Persia apparently oblivious of the debating questions addressed to him on the subject; then fold the bundle, grasp it with his coat lapel in characteristic fashion and deliver a reply delightful for the tracery of humour running through it. A day later Lord Balfour was speaking for nearly an hour, again without notes, on the complex problem of European and native in Africa, ending with the announcement of a scheme for the establishment of an Imperial Research Committee to examine questions of Empire development on the lines of the Committee of Imperial Defence with which he himself has had so much to do. Indeed, the new Committee is undoubtedly his own idea and was adopted by the Cabinet as soon as he brought it forward: The Government, therefore, remains well-equipped in the House of Lords.

## DELIBERATE ACT

## UNFAITHFUL CHINESE EMPLOYEE

## SNATCHER BIRCHED

A plea for leniency failed before Mr. R. A. D. Forrest at the Central Magistracy this morning as His Worship considered that a Chinese who had fraudulently converted money from his employers to his own use had acted according to a deliberate plan with intent to defraud and not on sudden impulse.

Defendant was employed by a Chinese firm. He pleaded guilty to obtaining $50 by a false chop from the Netherlands India Commercial Bank and to attempting to obtain a further sum of $340 in the same way. On the second occasion the Bank became suspicious, the Police were called in and defendant arrested. His plea was that he needed the money to send to his mother. Sentences of six and three months' hard labour, respectively, were imposed.

Sentence of two months and ten strokes with the birch was meted out to a Chinese, aged 27, who admitted snatching a gold hair pin from a Chinese lady.

## PORK HITCH

Plenty of beef is to be had today in the local markets but no pork. Upon enquiry of the Food Controller the “China Mail” was informed that the hitch as regarded the selling of pork had not yet been overcome. There was plenty of pork and there was no trouble about killing but the stalls refused to have anything to do with the retailing of it.

The objection to retailing does not, however, apply to hawkers, crowds of whom are to be seen selling meat and vegetables outside the very markets where the embargo seems to be in operation.

## COUNTY CRICKET

## LATEST RESULTS

Last year the Home country cricket season was spoiled to a considerable extent because the weather did not permit matches to be finished even in three days. This year it appears as if the fashion is being set to complete three-day matches in two days. This morning, more results come through and there was none that could be described as a real surprise.

For reasons which need hardly be explained, the championship table has not been published in the "China Mail" for the best part of a fortnight. To-day the table is given again.

It will be seen that Yorkshire have firmly entrenched themselves at the head of affairs and they will take some beating. The "Tykes" were successful in 1922, 1923 and 1924. This season they have exceeded all expectations, so far dropping only four points when Lancashire led them on the first innings. On the other hand, Lancashire—who were tipped by Pelham Warner as the winners this year—Surrey and Middlesex have been taking points from one another.

With Yorkshire's big lead interest is liable to decrease and they will have to lose at least two matches outright to make the competition exciting again.

Since the table was last published, there have been a few changes. Through Surrey losing badly to the champions and Middlesex bringing off a victory over Notts at Trent Bridge, the Oval team drop below the Lord's team. The failure to Middlesex and the loss of first innings points to Lancashire have cost Notts fifth place as Kent move above them.

## WORLD CRUISERS

A difficult section of an attempted all-round-the-world cruise in a thirty-foot cutter was completed by the arrival in Hong Kong of Captain Otto Prahl and Mr. Emil Euler who left Shanghai on June 7.

The boat, which flies the German flag, was bought and fitted out at Shanghai and Captain Prahl, who has spent on it much time and money is determined to accomplish the cruise if at all possible.

The occupants of the cutter have had an adventurous time since they left Shanghai and many repairs have had to be effected on the way owing to the ravages of the element. Anchor and chain were lost early in the voyage and when these were replaced a high wind carried away two booms and the chronometer ceased to function.
