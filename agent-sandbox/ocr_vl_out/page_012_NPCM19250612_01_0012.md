## ERRING WIVES

THE LAW AND THE HUSBAND.

The Government proposes to introduce legislation to remove the liability under which a husband is at present placed for his wife's wrongdoings. This announcement was made by Viscount Cave, the Lord Chancellor, in reply to a suggestion by Lord Danesfort that a committee should be appointed to consider the necessity for an alteration in the responsibility of husbands in view of the wide legislative changes that have been made in recent years in the status and rights of married women.

Lord Danesfort said that the law to-day was that a husband was responsible for his wife's wrong doings, even though committed without his knowledge or consent, and although his wife was separated from him at the time. It was the survival of some of the old common law doctrines which, according to modern ideas, were crude.

Lord Haldane was sympathetic with "the unfortunate husband," and agreed that it was monstrous that the principle referred to by Lord Danesfort should remain.

Lord Darling said that this was a matter of reform that ought to be urged by women themselves if they wanted to be considered as independent people. The law assumed that a married man and woman were only one person, and therefore if the woman committed a wrong it was committed by her husband. It had been laid down, in fact, that a man's wife was in a somewhat similar position to a man's pet monkey, and that he was responsible for the mischief she committed.

DOMESTIC CHASTISMENT.

The husband was, by the old law,

allowed to give his wife moderate

correction, on the ground that if

he had to answer for her mischievous behaviour the law thought

it reasonable to entrust him with

this power to restrain her by domestic chustisement. This power of correction began to be doubted in the reign of Charles II.

"If I were to put the woman's point of view," continued Lord Darling, "I am sure that they will not desire to be joined to their husbands more than is absolutely necessary. They will want to bring actions, get damages, and put them into their own pockets. The equality of the sexes demands this change in the law."

The Lord Chancellor, Lord Cave, replying for the Government, said that the old rule that a husband might beat his wife with a stick no thicker than his thumb had gradually disappeared, but another injustice had been caused by the retention of the liability of the husband. If the husband was liable for his wife's torts, at all events in times past he had her money, but to-day the law was that no part of the woman's property went to the husband on marriage. It was an unjust state of things.

"We have heard much of the rights of wives," concluded Lord Cave, "but I think in this case we ought to consider the rights of husbands." The Government proposed at a convenient time to introduce legislation, to remove this grievance, but it might not be possible to find time during the present session.
