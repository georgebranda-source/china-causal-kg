## PLANTS FROM TIBET

MR. KINGDON WARD'S RE CENT EXPEDITION.

## 一 

Mr. F. Kingdon Ward, F.R.G.S., the naturalist and explorer, who was just returned to England after a year's travelling in Eastern Tibet, brought back with him the seeds of a number of "fascinating new" hardy plants collected during his journey. Mr. Ward was accompanied on the expedition by Lord Cawdor. They had three permanent Bhutan servants and a number of porters. Exciting adventures were avoided, but the travellers broke new ground by traversing the full length of the tremendous gorge through which the Tsanpo river pierces the Himalayas.

Permission to make the journey was obtained at the beginning of last year from the Indian and Tibetan Governments, and the expedition was in part financed by the Government, Grant Committee of the Royal Society and by the Percy Sladen Memorial Fund.

start was made in March, and Tibet was entered from Darjeeling by the ordinary Lhasa route. When the Tsanpo was reached the party followed the river for 22 miles and then begun their botanical work in Eastern Tibet, May, June and July were spent in searching for new plants. Some unknown country was afterwards explored, and in October and November the seeds of the plants which had been specially noted were collected. The return was then made by the gorge of the Tsanpo, the Tibetan plateau, and Assam to Calcutta.

Speaking of the results of the expedition, Mr. Kingdon Ward said that he and all those interested in the venture were perfectly satisfied. The large collection of seeds brought home was now being gone through. Kew and Edinburgh had received a share, and others had been distributed privately throughout the country. Some of the seeds had gone to New Zealand and Africa. The scientific results would, he thought, be interesting. It had long been recognised that Tibet might provide the key to many problems of the distribution of flora. The difficulty was to get to the country and to carry out exploration in a very mountainous region. Much of the work last year was conducted at high altitudes, and at times the party climbed to 17,000 ft.; and 18,000 ft. When the seeds were collected there was a good deal of snow.

Among the discoveries was a magnificent hardy rhododendron with full orange flowers. The bushes grew to a height of about 10 ft. They were beautiful not only for their bloom, but also for their brilliant blue-green foliage, which could be seen miles away in the snow. Dwarf rhododendrons with real scarlet blossoms were another feature. Whole mountain slopes were covered ankle deep with this plant. An ivory-coloured Himalayan poppy was rather startling, and they had found some wonderful primulas. One specimen grew to a height of 4 ft., while 3 ft., was common. The flowers were remarkable not only for their size and colour, but for their fragrance. A golden yellow variety carried 100 flowers on one stem, and it grew so profusely as to clog up streams. A sulphur yellow bloom was extraordinarily fragrant and filled the air with perfume. Astonishing gentians 18 in. to 2 ft. high had each five or six spikes of sea blue trumpet-shaped blooms. They also had some good saxifrages. In addition to the seeds of the plants described they had brought back bulbs of a pink lily of the Martagon type, which bore 12 to 20 flowers on one stem and was delightfully fragrant. The seeds were germinating well now that they were here, and this was very satisfactory, as they had been three or four months on the journey and had passed through extremes of cold and heat. The seeds were kept alive on the way to Calcutta by placing them in thermos flasks and occasionally pumping in carbon dioxide.

Speaking of the exploration of the Tsampo Gorge, Mr. Kingdon Ward said that two Indian officers penetrated part of the gorge 13 years ago, but did not go right through. He and Lord Cawdor were the first Europeans to make the passage. With its windings the gorge would be nearly 100 miles in length. The river contracted from a normal breadth of 600 to 800 yards to a tumultuous torrent 50 to 100 yards wide. Cliffs rose abruptly to a height varying from 1,000 to 2,000 ft., above the water, and the full depth of the gorge would about 10,000 ft., where it cut through the Himalayas. It was only occasionally that the party could get down to the river, and mostly they were climbing up and down the cliffs. The scenery was superb, and at one point a glacier came right down to the water. Rumours had been heard of a great fall of the river somewhere in the gorge, but they discovered only two moderate waterfalls of about 40 ft. to 50 ft. The folings was very dense, and progress had accordingly to be slow. Nineteen days were occupied from entry to exit.

## NEW BUS CO

## ABERDEBN KAIFONGS' ENTERPRISE

AMBITIOUS SCHEMES

Four new Dennis' buses with curtain windows and spring seats, each to hold 23 passengers, are being placed on the Hongkong Aberdeen run by the Aberdeen Kalfong Bus Co., who, it is reported, have applied for permission to extend their service to Stanley with which there is now no direct communication.

An interesting history lies behind the placing on service of these new and up-to-date buses. Formerly Aberdeen was connected with the city only by launch. One such vessel was operated by the Kaifongs, i.e., the community of the village, but they ceased running when they failed because of keen competition. Their competitors then put up the rates and the Kaifongs commenced the 'bus service. The first company did not prove a success and the capital has nearly all gone. Now a new company is being floated with a capital of $200,000, in shares of $1 each so that nearly every inhabitant in the village can become a shareholder.

In the new concerns, shareholders will pay special rates, viz., 15 cents instead of 25 cents for first-class, and 12 cents instead of 15 cents for third-class. As there are English schools at Aberdeen, the company will issue monthly tickets at $2 to students proceeding to school in town and special buses will be run for them.

Because the Convent near Aberdeen is rendering good service to the community, it is also proposed to allow the sisters and staff to travel free as will all police officers in uniform or on duty.

## NO FRIEND IN NEED

LONELY

STILL COOLING HIS HEELS.

Chang Poh-loong ("Brilliant Chang) is still confined in the House of Detention awaiting the pleasure of the Governor-in-Council as to his destination.

It is two weeks since Chang was placed behind bars on his arrival as a deportee from England after serving a term of imprisonment for being concerned in the dope traffic. He was transferred to the House of Detention after his interview with the Secretary for Chinese Affairs a week ago. Although the authorities are prepared to allow him out on bail of about $500 pending the final decision, so far no friend of "Brilliant" has come forward to give him temporary freedom.

## HUTS ON FIRE

A fire occurred at Au Tin Chung village in the Castlepeak district at 4 o'clock yesterday afternoon and destroyed eleven grass huts. The damage is estimated at $1,140.

The cause of the outbreak has been traced to the act of a small girl in "burning grass" near the doorway of a hut A breeze carried the burning grass inside the hut and set alight a dry heap lying in a corner: The hut was soon ablaze and the fire spread to the others.

## TOLSTOY AT HIS DESK

### REMINISCENCES OF SURVIVING. DAUGHTER

After her previous lectures, Tolstoy's daughter, Tatyan. Lvovna Suchotin-Tolstoy, delivered an interesting lecture in German at the Russian Embassy in Vienna, thereby opening a series to be given within the scope of the Austrian Society for promoting cultural and economic relations with Russia.

## -

She stated that in his will Tolstoy provided that everybody should have the right to print his posthumous work, writings, and letters without paying royalties. He had fallen dangerously ill, and at that time said in a letter to his family: "The fact that my writings were sold for money has been one of the worst reflections for me during the last years." But he did not definitely dispose of his work before the year of his death. He then wanted his literary work to belong to the whole of mankind. Only when his legal adviser pointed out to him that that course was impossible according to the law did Tolstoy resolve to leave all his writings to his daughter Alexandra. In consideration of her illness, however, he ordered that, in the event of her demise, his posthumous work should pass over to his eldest daughter Tatyana. During his last years he could not be prevailed upon to publish anything, since he might give way to the desire for fame

Toitoy used to write until late at night. Every morning he would give the MS. to the members of his family to copy out; on the following morning the fair copy had to lie on his writing table. Then he would begin to alter, correct, I've done, and complete, which process he repeated on the next day. Thus a large number of chapters hitherto unknown, belonging to "My Childhood," "Resurrection," and the "Kreuzer-Sonata," and many versions of short stories and ideas, were found among his posthumous papers. It frequently occurred that he put some work aside and did not resume it a long time after; often he worked at the same M. S. for years before allowing it to be printed. As he was very economical in the use of writing paper, he would note alterations on old alps or on welopes dragged out of the waste paper basket, or he would conceive the margin of his abets with corrections and completions; so that the setting of his work in order after his death was rendered extremely difficult.

MR. R. MACDONALD AND HIS TROUSERS.

SHOULD THEY BE CREASED?

Mr. Ramsay MacDonald addressed a series of May Day demonstrations in his own constituency and in the adjoining division of Neath. Having been overnight the guest of Mr. J. D. Morgan at his villa near Cardiff, the ex-Prime Minister motored in the early morning to an old farmhouse, Plas Watford, near Cuerphilly, where he breakfasted at eight o'clock. A thirty-miles drive brought Mr. MacDonald to Cynmer, where he addressed a meeting of miners. "I have just come over the mountains," he replied to their greeting, "and everything is beautiful and inspiring except the slate roofs of the habitation of man."

Some people imagined, he said, that the Labour Movement was a sordid affair, but it was nothing of the kind. The significance of their May Day festivals was that the Labour Movement was filled with the spirit of spring. It represented the happy unison at which they were aiming among the workers of the world. Not the miner alone, not the engineer alone, not the Britisher alone, but the Frenchman, German, Czecho-Slovakian, Russian—all must be banded together in one constructive purpose if they were to win the workers' battle completely.

Newspapers were doing their best once again to raise the old Russian bogey. We had had our revolutions here; France had had them; blood had run; wickedness had been done. Where there were tyranny and oppression, revolution was inevitable, and instead of calm-minded men dispensing justice, they had ruthless men going straight to their goal not caring about the methods.

"That," he said, "is the nature of the beast. Do not let us deceive ourselves, but understand that when masses of young virile men and women are pressed down by Czars and rulers, then the inevitable consequence is brutality in order to bring freedom more within the practical reach of the people.

That is not justifying anything. Sooner or later we have got to get into relations with Russia. The trade unions here have got to get into relations with trade unions there. The one thing I warn you about is that you must see to it that in getting into friendly relations with Russian trade unions, you do not cut yourselves off from French and German trade unionists.

Speaking of what he described as the present Government's "barren record," he said their international policy was one of negativism. Egypt, if handled properly, he was convinced, could be settled now in a way satisfactory to both.

Referring to the election of Marshall Hindenburg, he asked, "Did anyone expect that anyone else would be returned?" The Allies, he said, had been strengthening Nationalism in Germany by their own policy.

GETTING TOO RESPECTABLE:

Prompted by a reference to his improved health, Mr. MacDonald told a story of how on his recent holiday he took out an old pair of trousers and found, when he put them on, they had a beautiful crease. He happened to be photographed in these. It was then said that he had deserted the workers because the crease in his trousers showed that he was getting for too respectable. On Friday he went into most respectable company, and had since read in the newspapers that he had turned up in a hat.

## WONDERS OF PACIFIC

#### MR. E. BROOKS

## MESSAGE FROM ARCTURUS EXPEDITION

Extraordinary good luck is attending the Arcturus expedition in the Pacific. The New York Times prints a copyrighted message from Dr. Beebe, the leader of the expedition, in which he reports the discovery on April 10 of two volcanoes in violent eruption of Albemarle Island, in the Galapagos group. No eruption has ever been witnessed there before. In honour of the two patrons of the expedition, Dr Beebe has named the volcanoes Mounts Williams and Whiton. He expresses the belief there there is some connection between the eruptions, the Japanese earthquakes, and changes in the Humboldt current. The Arcturus is standing by the islands to photograph the eruptions and be ready to give aid, if needed, to the tiny settlement on Albemarle.

Dr. Beebe reports that while drifting in the neighbourhood of the tide-rip, the Arcturus repeatedly, but vainly, attempted to break through "the saturation of the static" with its powerful wireless apparatus.

Before the discovery of the volcanoes the Arcturus came upon a gigantic tidierip where the Panama and Humboldt currents meet. In the mass of foam which extended for scores of miles wallowed great numbers of whales and porpoises. Wreckage, covered with organisms and fish of all sizes feeding on them, provided the scientists with an unexpected opportunity for observation. For the first time, they found there the eggs of halobates, the only marine insects in the world. The eggs are being hatched in tanks. Ivy credible numbers of jellyfish coloured the water purple for many square miles. The expedition has caught more than 150 species of fish, among them being transparent founders, deep-sea mackerel with blue and yellow lights, many kinds of jellyfish, and fish which live only on jellyfish.
