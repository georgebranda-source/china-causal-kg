London, July 25.—Figures just published by Lloyds Register of Shipping show that whereens British shipbuilding output has declined, more work is in hand on the Continent.

The total world tonnage under construction, viz., 2,369,831 tons, shows a decrease of 27,000 tons as compared with the previous quarter, and is over 1,000,000 tons below the pre-war record.

The motorship is steadily gaining headway, equalling 98 per cent. of the steam tonnage, although British yards have only 58 per cent. motorships on hand. On the other hand some countries, like Denmark, Germany, Holland, Italy and Sweden, are going in largely for motorships, the combined totals for these five countries being 216,257 tons of steamers, as against 645,600 tons of motorships.

The horsepower of the marine oil engines building in Denmark, for example, amounts to 125,950, compared with 2,100 horsepower of steam engines. Out of 294,800 horsepower of marine engines building in Germany no less than 227,000 is for Diesel engines, there being 57 sets of Diesels building in that country. Italy shows only 10 sets building; the enormous powers of the large motorships ordered there recently having evidently not yet been started.

Eight sets of 14,790 horsepower are under construction in the United States, and as only two motorships are building in that country they evidently represent some advance work, and possibly interesting research.

Sweden has more Diesel engines building than any other country in the world, there being 89 of 57,000-horsepower, but the average size is rather lower than that of other countries.

There is, however, general stagnation in the shipbuilding countries, with a few exceptions and recent statistics such as the freight index number of the Chamber of Shipping, the laid-up tonnage returns and the import

and export figures show that there is little likelihood of any immediate improvement.

SILVER AND ELECTRO-PLATE

Sheffield, July 29. — The Sheffield silver and electro-plate trades are passing through dull times again. The usual summer lethargy has been accentuated by a scarcity of big business from the colonies, which have been good buyers for some years. In the case of Australia the falling-off is most marked, especially in the better qualities of plated ware. Some firms who cater for buyers of cheaper qualities have booked some business with Australia, but from the point of view of value the trade has fallen off considerably. One of the striking features of the trade, both in electro-plate and nickel silver is the collapse at last of the phenomenal demand for spoons and forks. For over three years the resources of the factories have been taxed to meet the huge demand both for the home trade and export. Makers could not account for the extraordinarily heavy demand, and now they are at a loss to understand why it has collapsed. It had been sustained so long that they believed it had come to stay. Sterling silver sells extremely slowly. Best quality: electroplate, because of the relatively high price of nickel, is prohibitive in price to the average buyer, while electro-plate. Britannia metalware seems to meet the demands of the majority in these days of cutting down expenses. Stainless nickel has come to stay although progress with the sale of best qualities is slow.

According to the New York Journal of Commerce, fabric brokers at Akron, Ohio, note a slackening in the demand for tyre yarn and other cotton materials used in the rubber manufacturing industry, although production is still maintaining a very high level. The curtailment of activity in the fabric market, especially as regards future contracts, is attributed to the present high prices of rubber, as a result of which many of the smaller concerns have had to tie up a large portion of their capital in rubber, thus making them unable to purchase their normal requirements of fabric for future operations. Cotton materials formerly represented the bulk of the cost of raw materials entering into the manufacture of a tyre, but the situation is now reversed, so that the rubber content costs about three and a half times as much as the fabric. The tyre manufacturers are thus in an uncomfortable situation, since they will lose heavily on their stocks even if the price of raw rubber declines again.
