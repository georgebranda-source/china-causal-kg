## CHEAPER JUSTICE

LORD BIRKENHEAD ON

FANCY PRICES.

ABLE LAWYERS FOR COWER FEES.

The Earl of Birkenhead, a former Lord Chancellor, discusses in the "Sunday Pictorial" the heavy legal costs stated to have been incurred by parties to recent litigation.

After denling at length with the comparative brevity of the old cases and the extraordinary length of many modern proceedings and the causes of the change, he writes:—

A word or two may be added upon the practical question whether a client having a case in which he believes, need spend, in order to give that case a chance, the very large sums which are undoubtedly paid to any. In my opinion, he need not...

The ordinary client seems to make the mistake that he does not take advantage of the very great competition that exists both among solicitors and burristers. He may, of course, if he chooses, go to an extremely fashionable and successful firm of solicitors. In this case he buys, and rightly buys, for the reputation of the firm: for the rent of its offices, and for the salaries that are paid to many experienced oள்கள்.

But there are countless firms of soldiers, quite honest and quite efficient, who are neither so fashionable as to command very high fees nor so busily occupied as to refuse work for which a reasonable remuneration is offered. Perhaps in such a case the partner will attend to the matter instead of the managing clerk. Perhaps, also, in such a case the client will not be a loser by the substitution.

The same remark is at least equally true off the Bar. There always exists two or three fussligible leaders at the Bar who command very surprising fees, and do not always find it convenient even to make an appearance in court in order to earn them.

Few members of the Bar would go to such men if they became involved in "litigation" themselves. For the plain truth is that there are always at least twenty or thirty members of the Bar who could not advectes in any case—from

—with the most complete efficiency, and whose business engagements emble them to give sustained and continuous attention to the cases in which they are employed.

Personally, if I become involved in litigation, the consequences of which were grave to me: I would infinitely rather be represented by A, a competent and industrious lawyer, who I knew would not leave the court from the moment my case began until the moment it ended, than by B, who would, at ten times, the fee, saunter into court to cross-examine a "witness" whose examination-in-chief he had not heard, or to reply to a speech which had unfortunately been delivered in his absence.

I reach, therefore, the conclusion that in cases where colossal sums are spent upon litigation, the fault is as often as not chargeable upon the clients themselves. You can spend as much as you choose upon surgeons, counsel, dentists, and tailors, but it by no means follows that he's who pays most is best served.

## BISHOP DEFIED

### RECTOR.AND BANNED PREACHER

St. Mark's Church, in New York's tenement district, provided a stage recently for a dramatic climax over religious principles and the authority of episcopal bishops. When the "heretic bishop," the Rev. William Montgomery Brown, of Arkansas, spoke in St. Mark's, that act emphasized a direct challenge by Dr. William Guthrie, a rector to the authority of Bishop William Manning, of the diocese of New York. Bishop Manning, in a letter to Dr. Guthrie, forbade him to allow Bishop Brown to officiate or speak in St. Mark's, and Dr. Guthrie's decision to continue his determination to have Bishop Brown in St. Mark's really brings to an end a long battle waged between Bishop Manning and St. Mark's.

Bishop Brown was found guilty of heresy last autumn at an ecclesiastical trial at Cleveland, Ohio, but has not been deposed. Dr. Guthrie defended Bishop Brown's appearance in St. Mark's on the ground that the sanctuary of the church had been veiled, that the Church virtually became an auditorium," and in that sense a meeting or symposium was "secular" and clearly beyond Bishop Manning's authority, and jurisdiction. Two years ago St. Mark's was excluded from the Episcopal visitation and administration because of "pagan ritual and eurhythmic dances before the altar." Later Dr. Guthrie dropped the dances and substituted "colour music" in his ritual, various limelight effects being introduced to emphasise serious emotions.

## THORNE HANGED

OVER IN THN SECONDS.

一

John Norman Holmes Thorne was executed in Wandsworth at eight o'clock in the morning for the murder of his fiancee, Elsie Emily Cameron, a Cambeltown girl, who latterly lived at Kensal Rise, London.

A special representative of the Press who was present at the execution says it was probably one of the most expeditious on record. From the time that Thorne emerged from his cell door until the moment he passed into eternity only ten seconds elapsed, Thorne had no sooner stepped on to the scaffold than the lever was released and he had disappeared from view.

He was wearing his navy blue suit, and when he came from the condemned cell his face for a brief moment showed that peculiar flicker of a smile that was was observed on the occasion of his many, appearances in the police court at Crowborough and in the dock at the Assize Court at Lewes.

He seemed to be in the best of health, and from his personal appearance one could not detect any trace of the dread or deal that he had gone through or concern as to the terrible experience that was awaiting him.

As he passed from the cell to the scaffold Thorne was preceded by the Wesleyan chaplain, the Rev. Edward Finch. He gave a fleeting glance to one side as he walked, but did not betray the slightest sign of nervousness.

There was just a touch of halting in his footsteps when he first came from the cell, but he recovered his self-possession in a moment, and never required the assistance of the executioners who held him gently by his pinioned arms.

As he approached the threshold of the execution shed there was a sudden bracing of the nerves. But for the one fleeting glance aside Thorne looked straight in front of him, and carried himself with his head slightly raised.

In the execution shed the work of the executioner and his assistant was exceedingly quick. Almost in one moment the white cap was drawn over Thorne's head, his legs pinioned, the lever was pulled, and in an instant Thorne had expiated his crime

The executioner was Pierpont, who was assisted by Phillips and the officials present at the execution were the governor of the prison, the prison surgeon, and the Under-Sheriff, Mr. Walter C.S. Chapman.

## IN ACADEMY AT 19

## YOUNG ARTISTS SUCCESS

To be “hung” at the Royal Academy at the age of 19 is the remarkable achievement of Mr. John R. Pimlott, of Forest Hill, S.E., who has just received news of the acceptance of a water-colour painting which he submitted, together with two other works.

“I am naturally delight I at the news,” the youth said “particularly as this is only the second occasion on which I have submitted pictures to the Academy. The painting accepted is an Autumn Scene.”

Mr. Pimlett has been studying for two years at Goldsmiths' College.

"As other work keeps me occupied most of the day," he said. "I get good practice by sketching people I see in restaurants."

Mr. Pimlott's father, who is an artist, has had several pictures "hung," his first being at the age of twenty-four.

Mr. A. Sherwood Edwards, a young sculptor, of Ashton-on Mersey, Cheshire, has been informed that a landscape in oils, which he painted from a sketch made while in an anti-aircraft battery in France during the war, has been accepted by the Royal Academy. "The actual painting," he said "did not take more than two hours."

## DEAD COUNT PUZZLE

## ALLEGED POISONING BY AN UNKNOWN

"My son was poisoned," is all that the Countess de Beuret, the mother of the young Count Girard de Montfiore can say about the fatal illness that overcame him suddenly when motoring in Paris with his fiancee, Mlle. Yvonne Doux. The court was a young millionaire sportsman and owner of the Castle of Dompierre in the Aube Department.

The countess and her husband, the stepfather of the young man, have laid a formal charge with the police of murder against some person unknown

The post-mortem examination showed no traces of organic disease, but a chemical examination will have to be made for signs of poisoning.

A colt at the castle of Dompierre died under conditions which suggested poisoning some weeks ago.

The police are making inquiries at Dompierre into a report that the count had a violent quarrel with a person intimately known to him.

## SEEING MESSAGES:

## VISIBLE ONLY THROUGH TINTED GLASSES

Messages can now be flashed through the air which are visible only to those with glasses tinted in a special way to receive them, said Dr. Robert Wood to the American Chemical Conference at Baltimore.

These messages can be sent in full sunlight by means of red rays, usually invisible to the eye.

They have already been sent as far as eight miles, and the rays can be used in military signal lamps for flashing signals. Apparently this envisages an army of the future, all wearing special goggles, for Dr. Robert suggests the painting of aeroplanes to make them look to the enemy like his own machines, but to appear to their own men, armed with goggles, in their true colours. He also suggested that the use of invisible light might prove valuable in detecting the presence of cancer and other growths now invisible under ordinary light.
