## CANTON'S CHANCE

Officially, the boycott has been called off; actually, it still persists in large measure. The Canton Government, we have no doubt, is sincere in its efforts to restore trade between the sister ports, but is feeling its way with the pickets. Doubtless the Canton Government knows its own business best. Perhaps it is reluctant to force the issue with the pickets, or possibly it is anxious to avoid any steps which may appear to rebuff Labour, to whose support it owes so much.

The Canton Government, as we say, doubtless knows its own business best; but the matter goes beyond the purely domestic sphere. First there is the illegality of the pickets' activities, amounting to rank piracy, if not to open warfare against a friendly nation. This should be ample reason for finally curbing the pickets. Apart from possible consequences, no decent government can have any wish to permit piracy within its borders.

There is a second reason, however, only a little less forceful. The Canton Government, hearing chiefly the protests excited by the boycott and other illegal activities, possibly does not realize how much the tide is turning in its favour for recognition by the Powers. Perhaps it would not be saying too much to venture the opinion that but for the boycott and these other illegal activities, the Canton Government would be granted instant recognition.

"Some day the Canton Government will get rid of the absurd fetish that Britain wishes to reduce China to the status of a "semi-colony"; some day it will realize that nothing would please Britain better than a strong, united China bent on trade and progress. Could it only grasp these two facts now, the Canton Government would perhaps be able to realize how near it is to recognition."

Recognition can hardly be expected, however, while the boy

cott continues in any shape or form, or while treaties are either infringed or threatened with infringement. There can be no doubt on that point, Therefore it behaves the Canton Government to take stock of the position—to consider the risks it must run against the stakes it stands to win.

The stakes are high. „Doubtless they will be gained in due course: The waiting game will win in the end. “But is the waiting game the best? Think what a sudden gesture would mean in the eyes of the world,” how it would impress the peoples and the leaders of the Powers—the Powers who are ready to give so much for so little in return. The opportunity is here—it needs but the taking!

## Banditry and Piracy

has come to the conclusion that the most urgent question to be faced by the Provincial Government is, brigandage. Four divisions of troops are to be formed for the special purpose of suppressing banditry and piracy, whilst a corp of constabulary is to be organised and trained for the prevention of banditry. It is to be hoped that special attention will be given in the consideration of these measures to Bias Bay—the headquarters of the pirates. If the Kwangtung Government can uproot this nest and scatter the pirates once and for all beyond any chance of reassembling at a fresh rendezvous, it will certainly earn the gratitude of the thousands of Chinese who are in daily fear of their lives at the hands of the Bias Bay terrorists. Likewise will it "make good" in the eyes of foreigners in view of Mr. Eugene Chen's promise at the end of September that, once the boycott was terminated, his Government intended to tackle the piracy problem, probably with the co-operation of the British. The inhabitants of Kwangtung are suffering from banditry no less than piracy, not a village or hamlet being free from the fear of molestation. In seriously dealing with both piracy and banditry the Kwangtung Government will have an opportunity to show what it can do in the direction of restoring law and order, assuring the people of all possible protection and of getting rid of immense obstacles to social and traditional intercourse both in the interior and on the rivers and high seas.

THE GOVERNMENT CIVIL HOSPITAL.



[To the Editor of the "China Mail"] Sir,—You have done a public service in initiating discussion on this extremely important subject, and as one who has had long and special opportunity of observation I feel impelled to express agreement with the position you have taken up.

## Hong Kong is indeed fortunate in its Government hospitals

I have visited scores of patients in them and can truly say that nowhere have I known any comparable public institutions as to which there has seemed to be such abundant satisfaction and so little complaint. I have acquired the highest admiration for the diligence and devotion of both medical and nursing staffs, and have said repeatedly if ever I am taken seriously ill, "Me for the G.C.H." Put to the test I carried this out and entered for surgical treatment some few years ago, experiencing then the same skill, care and human kindness which I had so often seen accorded to others.

But alas, it was Chinese New Year, and the experience nearly made a Bhuddist of me; for surely, thought I, I must have sinned in some previous life to have merited the torture of those awful nights!

The whole hospital, staff and patients alike, was in a state of "nerves" because of the unceasing din, to modify which the police were said to be helpless. It is the same to some extent the whole year round, and I believe it is beyond doubt that retarded recovery in many cases and failure to recover at all in others must be put down to this cause, not to mention vitiated air and other drawbacks.

To rebuild on or near the present site would be an error of the first magnitude!

Such, I have reason to believe, is the real opinion of the medical department, and any authority which overrides it assumes indeed a heavy responsibility.

As to proximity for teaching purposes to the University, the less heard of that argument the

better. Our doctors must, of course, be trained, but the doctors exist for man, not man for the doctors.

Hong Kong, Nov. 8, 1926.

## A MISUNDERSTANDING

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"]

Sir,—Whilst I have no desire to prolong a correspondence on a matter which I can only deal with from the point of view of one who has experienced some of the inconveniences attendant on the present situation of the Government Civil Hospital in so far as "the patients and those visiting them are concerned, I feel called upon to explain that "S.W.P." has put a construction on an expression that I used which was not in my mind when I used it.

In submitting that if the G.C.H. was devoted entirely to the service of the Chinese its rebuilding would not be necessary, I was merely considering the release of accommodation at present devoted to the use of European patients. One of the chief reasons advanced for the necessity of rebuilding is, I believe, the inadequacy of present accommodation, and the point I wished to make was that if the hospital is devoted entirely to the needs of those to whom it is more conveniently situated, the putting in hand of whatsoever improvements that may be necessary would be more, politic than committing the Colony to unjustified expenditure. If it could reasonably have been implied from my letter that I was of the opinion that “anything will do” for those of a nationality other than European, I apologise for my ambiguity and take this opportunity of assuring “S.W.P.,” and any who gathered likewise, that such was not my intended meaning.

## CIVIC APATHY

[To the Editor of the "China Mail"] Sir,—It was, indeed, gratifying to note that at least one of your readers has come forward and congratulated you upon your attitude with regard to the way things are "run" in this Colony. I desire to associate myself fully with your correspondent "Ionides" and to supplement his, or her remarks with a few observations which I hope will not be hastily regarded as destructive criticism.

Are "the people of "Hong Kong going to submit indehnitely to the present system of Government?" Is it not time, as "Ionides" asks, that we should "get going" and make representations to the Home Government and let Colonel Amery and his colleagues in the British Cabinet know that we are sick and tired of this system of government by an autocratic majority?

In referring to the general lack of difference so very visible in the Colony at the present time, your most aptly state that, give a topic, a mass meeting with a, beating of big drum and a flow of eloquence sufficient to move, a boulder, and the result will be amazing—until a brief period slapses and the embers of enthusiasm have burnt out. Agreed, but is it not time their someone failed round the standard that you have so ably holsted?

May I appeal through the medium of your columns to all your readers who may be in a position to approach the Unofficial Members of the Legislative Council and other prominent residents of the Colony to lose no time in endavouring to set the ball rolling? It is clearly evident that unless the Constitutional Reform Association intends to do something in the matter, the present state of affairs will continue ad'ӏinfitum. If the C.R.A. will not take the lead, surely some public-spirited individuals can be found who will be willing to come forward and help us to get on with the job? It is the great merchant houses of this Colony who have made it what it is to-day and it is certainly surprising that the heads of those houses should be willing, practically without protest, to submit to this existing system.

Judging from your well-balanced and sober-minded judgments upon current topics which have appeared in your editorial columns from time to time, we may rely upon the support of at least one section of the press, whilst one is inclined to the belief that even your contemporary which is so anxious to do away with the University, would not turn a deaf ear to any movement seeking to do away with government by an autocratic minority. Let us get on with the job, Sir.

## Order's Origin

The Order of St. John had its origin in Jerusalem and Acre, as an international lay confraternity for the relief of crusaders, and was later sovereign in Rhodes and Malta, where its Knights kept galleys and galleons to attack the Barbary Corsairs and to prevent the spread of Turkish rule in the Mediterranean from 1580 until 1798.

The work of the British Order is the control of the St. John Association: Ambulance and Brigade, and of the Ophthalmic Hospital, Jerusalem.
